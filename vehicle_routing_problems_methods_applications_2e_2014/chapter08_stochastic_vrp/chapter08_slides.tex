\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Introduction: Why Stochastic VRP?}

Vehicle Routing Problems (VRPs) have been the subject of intensive research since the
landmark paper of Dantzig and Ramser (1959), followed by the Clarke--Wright savings
algorithm (1964) and decades of subsequent work.  The vast majority of this literature
treats VRP as a purely \emph{deterministic} problem: all customer demands, locations, and
travel times are assumed to be known with complete certainty at the time routes are planned.

In practice, however, these quantities are \emph{uncertain}.  A distribution company
dispatches its fleet in the morning based on forecasted demands, yet the actual demand at
each stop is only revealed when the vehicle arrives.  A service technician plans a daily
route, but some customers may cancel appointments or be unavailable.  An urban courier
cannot predict traffic delays precisely enough to guarantee on-time arrivals.

\begin{block}{Definition --- Stochastic VRP (SVRP)}
  A \textbf{Stochastic VRP} (SVRP) is any Vehicle Routing Problem in which one or more
  parameters are \emph{random variables} whose exact values are not known at the time the
  route plan is constructed:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item customer \textbf{demands} $D_i$ (how much each customer needs --- random
      because orders vary day-to-day),
    \item customer \textbf{presence} $Y_i$ (whether a customer will actually require
      service --- uncertain because appointments may be cancelled), or
    \item arc \textbf{travel times} $T_{ij}$ (how long it takes to travel from node $i$
      to node $j$ --- uncertain due to traffic, weather, or service-time variability).
  \end{itemize}
\end{block}

The central challenge is that a route which is optimal under the \emph{average} scenario
may perform very poorly under certain realizations.  Ignoring uncertainty leads to
capacity violations (vehicles run out of stock mid-route), missed time-window deadlines,
or costly emergency restocking trips that were not in the original plan.

\framebreak

Two fundamentally different paradigms have been developed for dealing with stochasticity
in VRP, and understanding the distinction between them is essential before studying any
specific model.

\medskip
\textbf{Paradigm 1 --- A Priori Optimization.}
An a priori (Latin: ``before the fact'') solution is a fixed route plan computed
\emph{before} any random quantity is revealed.  The quality of this plan is measured by
its \emph{expected cost} (average cost over all possible random outcomes).  When actual
demands are revealed during execution, only minor local corrections are made
according to a pre-specified \emph{recourse policy} (for example: if the vehicle runs
out of capacity, return to the depot, reload, and continue).

\medskip
\textbf{Paradigm 2 --- Reoptimization.}
The route is not fixed in advance.  After each random revelation (e.g., the demand at a
customer is observed upon arrival), the plan for all remaining customers is completely
recomputed.  This yields higher solution quality but requires solving a new VRP instance
online at each decision point, which is computationally expensive.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{fig_vrp_taxonomy.pdf}
  \caption{Taxonomy of VRP variants showing deterministic versus stochastic branches.
    The three main SVRP types (stochastic demands, customers, and travel times)
    correspond to different uncertain parameters.}
\end{figure}

\framebreak

The stochastic VRP literature is broadly classified into three major problem types,
each defined by which parameter is uncertain:

\medskip
\textbf{Type 1 --- VRPSD: VRP with Stochastic Demands.}
All customers are known and will definitely be visited, but the exact demand $D_i$
(read: ``D sub i'', the load the vehicle must pick up or deliver at customer $i$) is a
random variable revealed only upon arrival.  The main risk is that the total demand along
a route exceeds the vehicle capacity $Q$ (the maximum weight or volume the vehicle can
carry), forcing an unplanned return to the depot to reload.

\medskip
\textbf{Type 2 --- VRPSC: VRP with Stochastic Customers.}
Each customer $i$ is present with a known probability $p_i$ (read: ``p sub i'', the
probability that customer $i$ is actually home or requires service on a given day) and
absent with probability $1 - p_i$.  The set of customers requiring service is revealed
only at execution time.

\medskip
\textbf{Type 3 --- VRPST: VRP with Stochastic Travel Times.}
Travel times $T_{ij}$ (read: ``T sub i j'', the travel time from customer $i$ to
customer $j$) are random variables.  The primary concern is time-window feasibility:
the vehicle may arrive too early (and must wait, wasting time) or too late (violating
a customer's service window, incurring a penalty).

\medskip
Stochastic VRP research began with Tillman (1969) and was advanced theoretically by
Bertsimas (1992).  The influential survey by Gendreau, Laporte, and S\'eguin (1996)
remains the canonical reference, and the chapter we study here provides a comprehensive
update of the field as of 2014.

\begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: The Core Tension}
  The fundamental difficulty of SVRP is that one must commit to a plan \emph{before}
  the random values are known, yet the quality of the plan is judged \emph{after} they
  are revealed.  A plan optimised for the average scenario sacrifices performance in the
  tail scenarios.  Good SVRP algorithms explicitly model this tension and plan for
  uncertainty, not just for the expectation.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{A Priori Optimization}
% ===========================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{A Priori Optimization: Framework and Network Formulation}

Under the a priori optimization paradigm, the goal is to find a single fixed route plan
that achieves the best \emph{expected} performance across all possible realizations of the
random parameters, given a specified recourse policy that governs how minor corrections
are made during execution.

\begin{block}{Graph and Notation Setup}
  Let $G = (V, E)$ be a complete undirected graph where:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $V = \{v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$ is the set of nodes.  Node $v_0$ (``v sub
      zero'') is the \textbf{depot} (the warehouse where vehicles start and end).
      Nodes $V' = \{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$ are the \textbf{customers}.
    \item $E = \{(v_i, v_j) : i < j,\; i,j \in V\}$ is the edge set (the set of all
      possible direct connections between nodes).
    \item $c_{ij} \geq 0$ (``c sub i j'', read ``cost from $i$ to $j$'') is the travel
      cost of edge $(v_i, v_j)$, typically distance or time.
  \end{itemize}
  Each customer $v_i$ has a \textbf{random demand} $D_i$ (``D sub i'') drawn from a
  known probability distribution $F_i$.  A fleet of vehicles, each with capacity $Q$,
  must serve all customers while minimising expected total travel cost.
\end{block}

The random variables $D_1, D_2, \ldots, D_n$ are assumed to be \emph{independently
distributed} (each customer's demand is independent of all others), though they may
have different distributions.  This is a standard and realistic assumption: one customer's
order size does not affect another customer's order size.

\framebreak

\textbf{The Restocking Recourse Policy.}
The most widely studied recourse policy for VRPSD is the \emph{optimal restocking}
policy.  Let $\pi = (v_0, v_{i_1}, v_{i_2}, \ldots, v_{i_n}, v_0)$ be the a priori
route (the planned visiting order).  The vehicle follows this sequence.  When it arrives
at customer $v_{i_k}$ and finds that serving $v_{i_k}$ would cause the accumulated demand
to exceed the capacity $Q$, the vehicle \emph{returns to the depot} (cost
$c_{v_{i_{k-1}}, v_0}$), reloads to full capacity, then returns to $v_{i_k}$ (cost
$c_{v_0, v_{i_k}}$) and continues the route.  This unplanned return trip is called a
\textbf{recourse trip}, and its cost is the \emph{recourse penalty}.

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example --- Recourse Trip}
  Suppose $Q = 10$, route $\pi = (v_0, v_1, v_2, v_3, v_4, v_0)$, and one realization
  of demands is $d_1 = 4, d_2 = 5, d_3 = 3, d_4 = 2$.

  \textbf{Step 1:} Serve $v_1$ (accumulated load $= 4 \leq 10$). OK.

  \textbf{Step 2:} Serve $v_2$ (accumulated load $= 4 + 5 = 9 \leq 10$). OK.

  \textbf{Step 3:} Attempt $v_3$: accumulated load would be $9 + 3 = 12 > Q = 10$.
  Recourse trip: drive from $v_2$ to depot (cost $c_{v_2 v_0}$),
  reload, drive from depot to $v_3$ (cost $c_{v_0 v_3}$).  Now serve $v_3$
  (load $= 3$).

  \textbf{Step 4:} Serve $v_4$ (load $= 3 + 2 = 5 \leq 10$). Return to depot.

  The total execution cost equals the planned route cost \emph{plus} the recourse penalty
  $c_{v_2 v_0} + c_{v_0 v_3}$.  Since $d_1, d_2, d_3, d_4$ are random, this recourse
  penalty is itself random, and the VRPSD minimises its \emph{expected value}.
\end{exampleblock}

\framebreak

\subsection{Network Flow Formulation}

The a priori SVRP for VRPSD can be cast as a stochastic integer program.  We follow the
formulation of Christiansen and Lysgaard (2007) and Gendreau, Laporte, and S\'eguin (1995,
1996).

Let $\hat{G} = (\hat{V}, \hat{A})$ be the complete directed graph on the same node set,
where $\hat{A}$ contains a directed arc $(i,j)$ for each ordered pair of distinct nodes.
The decision variables are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $x_{ij} \in \{0,1\}$ (``x sub i j''): equals 1 if arc $(i,j)$ is traversed in
    the a priori plan, and 0 otherwise.  This variable encodes the routing structure.
  \item $\bm{D} = (D_1, \ldots, D_n)$ (``bold D''): the random demand vector.
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Network Flow Formulation --- Objective and Constraints (8.1)--(8.7)}
  \textbf{Minimise} the expected total cost:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:apriori-obj}
    E[\mathcal{C}(\pi)] \;=\;
      \underbrace{\sum_{(i,j)\in\hat{A}} c_{ij}\, x_{ij}}_{\text{planned travel cost}}
      \;+\;
      \underbrace{\sum_{i \in V'} (c_{v_0 v_i} + c_{v_i v_0})\,
        E\bigl[R(\pi, \bm{D}, i)\bigr]}_{\text{expected recourse cost}}
  \end{equation}
  subject to:
  \begin{align}
    \sum_{j \in V'} x_{v_0 j} &= m                        \tag{8.2}\\
    \sum_{i \in V \setminus \{k\}} x_{ik} &= 1         & \forall k \in V' \tag{8.3}\\
    \sum_{j \in V \setminus \{k\}} x_{kj} &= 1         & \forall k \in V' \tag{8.4}\\
    x_{ij} &\in \{0,1\}                                & \forall (i,j)\in\hat{A} \tag{8.5}
  \end{align}
  where $m$ is the number of vehicles (routes), and $E[R(\pi, \bm{D}, i)]$ is the
  expected number of restocking trips triggered at customer $v_i$.
\end{block}

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read This Objective Function}
  The objective function has two parts.  The first part, $\sum c_{ij} x_{ij}$, is the
  ordinary deterministic route cost (sum of arc traversal costs).  The second part adds
  the expected penalty: for each customer $v_i$, we pay a round-trip cost to the depot
  (cost $c_{v_0 v_i} + c_{v_i v_0}$) every time a restocking trip occurs there, and
  $E[R(\pi, \bm{D}, i)]$ is the expected number of such trips.  The algorithm must find
  the route $\pi$ (encoded by $x_{ij}$) that minimises the sum of planned travel cost
  and expected emergency-trip cost.  Routes that visit too many high-demand customers
  in a row will incur high recourse costs; routes that spread demand risk wisely will
  incur lower expected penalties.
\end{alertblock}

\medskip
Constraint (8.2) states that exactly $m$ routes depart from the depot.
Constraints (8.3) and (8.4) are \emph{flow-conservation} constraints ensuring that
every customer is visited exactly once: precisely one arc enters each customer node
and precisely one arc leaves.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.50\linewidth]{fig_network_flow.pdf}
  \caption{Network flow graph for the a priori SVRP formulation.
    Arcs $x_{ij}$ encode planned route edges; the depot node can be visited multiple
    times via restocking arcs.}
\end{figure}

\framebreak

\textbf{Computing the expected recourse cost.}
The key computational challenge is evaluating $E[R(\pi, \bm{D}, i)]$ for a given route
$\pi$.  This requires knowing the probability that the vehicle runs out of capacity at
each position in the route.

For independent discrete demands, this is computed efficiently using a
\emph{dynamic programming} (DP) recursion.  Let $\phi^k(q)$ denote the probability
that the cumulative demand of the first $k$ customers equals exactly $q$.  The recursion
is:
\[
  \phi^0(0) = 1,
  \qquad
  \phi^k(q) = \sum_{d=0}^{q} \phi^{k-1}(q-d)\cdot P(D_{i_k} = d),
  \quad q = 0,1,\ldots
\]
Here $\phi^k(q)$ is the probability (a number between 0 and 1) that the first $k$
customers together demand exactly $q$ units.  The recursion updates by convolving (mixing)
the current distribution with the next customer's demand distribution.  This runs in
$O(n Q)$ time (proportional to the number of customers times the vehicle capacity), which
is manageable for typical problem sizes.

The expected number of recourse trips on the entire route equals:
\[
  E[R(\pi, \bm{D})]
  \;=\;
  \sum_{k=1}^{n} P\!\left(\left\lfloor \frac{S_{k-1}}{Q} \right\rfloor
    < \left\lfloor \frac{S_k}{Q} \right\rfloor \right)
\]
where $S_k = D_{i_1} + \cdots + D_{i_k}$ is the cumulative demand after the first $k$
customers.  The floor function $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$ (read: ``floor of'') gives the
integer part.  The expression $\lfloor S/Q \rfloor$ counts how many complete ``loads''
have been consumed.  A recourse trip occurs at position $k$ whenever the cumulative
demand crosses a new multiple of $Q$, i.e., when $\lfloor S_k/Q \rfloor > \lfloor S_{k-1}/Q \rfloor$.

\begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Ordering Matters}
  The expected cost $E[\mathcal{C}(\pi)]$ depends not only on \emph{which} customers are
  on the route, but also on the \emph{order} in which they are visited.  Visiting a
  high-demand customer early (when the vehicle is nearly full) may cause a costly
  restocking trip sooner.  This makes VRPSD significantly harder than deterministic CVRP:
  even evaluating a candidate solution requires the $O(nQ)$ DP computation.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{The Integer L-Shaped Algorithm}
% -----------------------------------------------------------

The \textbf{integer L-shaped algorithm} is the leading exact method for the a priori
VRPSD.  It was originally developed by Laporte and Louveaux (1993) for general two-stage
stochastic integer programs and adapted by Gendreau, Laporte, and S\'eguin (1995)
specifically for the VRPSD.

The algorithm belongs to the family of \emph{Benders decomposition} methods (named after
the mathematician J.F. Benders, 1962).  The core idea is to split the problem into two
interacting parts:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{master problem} that optimises the routing structure (the binary
    variables $x_{ij}$), treating the recourse cost as a variable $\theta$ (theta,
    a lower-bound estimate).
  \item A \textbf{subproblem} that, given a candidate route $\pi$ from the master, computes
    the exact expected recourse cost $E[\mathcal{C}(\pi)]$ using the DP recursion described
    earlier, and generates \emph{optimality cuts} that prevent the master from
    underestimating the recourse cost in future iterations.
\end{enumerate}

The name ``L-shaped'' comes from the shape of the constraint matrix when the subproblem
constraints are appended to the master problem in a tableau form --- it resembles a
capital letter L.

\begin{block}{Integer L-Shaped Algorithm --- Full Pseudocode}
  \textbf{Initialisation.}\\
  \hspace{1em} Set $\theta \leftarrow -\infty$ (theta, initial lower bound on recourse
  cost; starts at negative infinity meaning no lower bound yet).\\
  \hspace{1em} Set iteration counter $\ell \leftarrow 0$ (ell, current iteration
  number).\\[4pt]

  \textbf{Step 1 (Master Problem).}\\
  \hspace{1em} Solve the LP or MIP relaxation of the master:
  \[
    \min\; \sum_{(i,j)} c_{ij}\, x_{ij} + \theta
    \quad \text{s.t. constraints (8.2)--(8.5) + all cuts generated so far.}
  \]
  \hspace{1em} Let $(x^*, \theta^*)$ be the optimal solution from this iteration.\\[4pt]

  \textbf{Step 2 (Subproblem --- Recourse Evaluation).}\\
  \hspace{1em} For each route $\pi^*$ encoded by $x^*$, compute $E[\mathcal{C}(\pi^*)]$
  exactly using the DP recursion on demand distributions.\\[4pt]

  \textbf{Step 3 (Optimality Cut).}\\
  \hspace{1em} If $\theta^* < E[\mathcal{C}(\pi^*)] - \varepsilon$ (epsilon, a small
  tolerance), then the master problem underestimates recourse cost.  Add the
  \emph{optimality cut}:
  \[
    \theta \;\geq\; \hat{\alpha} - \hat{\beta}\!\!\sum_{(i,j) \in \hat{\pi}^*} x_{ij}
  \]
  \hspace{1em} where $\hat{\alpha}$ (alpha-hat) and $\hat{\beta}$ (beta-hat) are
  coefficients computed from the subproblem evaluation (detailed below).\\[4pt]

  \textbf{Step 4 (Feasibility Cut).}\\
  \hspace{1em} If $x^*$ encodes disconnected sub-tours (violating connectivity), add a
  \emph{feasibility cut} (subtour elimination constraint) to exclude it.\\[4pt]

  \textbf{Step 5 (Termination Check).}\\
  \hspace{1em} If $\theta^* \geq E[\mathcal{C}(\pi^*)] - \varepsilon$, the current
  solution is proved optimal.  \textbf{Stop}.\\
  \hspace{1em} Otherwise: $\ell \leftarrow \ell + 1$ and return to Step 1.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\textbf{Understanding the Optimality Cut.}
The cut $\theta \geq \hat{\alpha} - \hat{\beta} \sum_{(i,j) \in \hat{\pi}^*} x_{ij}$
works as follows.  The quantity $\sum_{(i,j) \in \hat{\pi}^*} x_{ij}$ counts how many
arcs from the current route $\hat{\pi}^*$ are retained in any future solution.  When
the master proposes the same route (all arcs retained), the count equals $|\hat{\pi}^*|$
and the cut forces $\theta \geq \hat{\alpha} - \hat{\beta}|\hat{\pi}^*|
= E[\mathcal{C}(\hat{\pi}^*)]$, correctly capturing the true recourse cost.
When the master deviates to a different route, fewer arcs from $\hat{\pi}^*$ are kept,
and the cut provides a (potentially loose) lower bound on the new route's recourse cost.

The coefficients are:
\begin{align*}
  \hat{\alpha} &= E[\mathcal{C}(\hat{\pi}^*)]
    + \hat{\beta}\, |\hat{\pi}^*|, \\
  \hat{\beta}  &= \frac{E[\mathcal{C}(\hat{\pi}^*)]}{n+1}
\end{align*}
where $n$ is the number of customers on the route and $|\hat{\pi}^*|$ is the number of
arcs in the route (equals $n+1$ for a route visiting $n$ customers returning to depot).

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{fig_lshaped_convergence.pdf}
  \caption{Convergence of the integer L-shaped algorithm.  The lower bound
    (LP relaxation value) rises as optimality cuts are added; the upper bound
    (best integer solution found) falls.  The algorithm terminates when the gap
    closes to within tolerance $\varepsilon$ (epsilon).}
\end{figure}

\framebreak

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example --- Integer L-Shaped on a Small Instance}
  Consider $n = 4$ customers with capacity $Q = 10$ and discrete uniform demands:
  \[
    D_1 \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{3,4\},\;\;
    D_2 \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{2,3\},\;\;
    D_3 \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{4,5\},\;\;
    D_4 \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{1,2\}.
  \]
  Expected demands: $E[D_1]=3.5$, $E[D_2]=2.5$, $E[D_3]=4.5$, $E[D_4]=1.5$.
  Total expected demand $= 12 > Q = 10$, so restocking trips are inevitable.

  \textbf{Iteration 1 (master problem):}
  The master ignores recourse ($\theta = 0$) and finds the shortest Hamiltonian cycle
  $\pi_1 = (v_0, v_1, v_2, v_3, v_4, v_0)$ with planned cost $c_1 = 45$.

  \textbf{Subproblem:}
  Running the DP on $\pi_1$ gives $E[\mathcal{C}(\pi_1)] = 10.4$ (expected recourse
  penalty from 1--2 restocking trips depending on the realization).

  \textbf{Optimality cut added:}
  $\theta \geq 10.4 - 2.08(x_{01}+x_{12}+x_{23}+x_{34}+x_{40})$.

  \textbf{Iteration 2 (master with cut):}
  The master now penalises routes with high recourse cost and finds a different ordering
  that better balances travel cost and demand load.  The process repeats until the master's
  $\theta^*$ equals the subproblem's $E[\mathcal{C}]$, proving optimality.
\end{exampleblock}

\framebreak

\subsection{The Branch-and-Price Algorithm}

\textbf{Branch-and-Price (B\&P)} provides an alternative exact approach, particularly
effective for larger instances.  Rather than adding cuts to a fixed formulation, B\&P
dynamically generates columns (routes) as needed.

The LP relaxation of the set partitioning formulation is solved by \emph{column
generation}, where each column corresponds to one feasible route.  At each step, the
\emph{pricing subproblem} asks: ``Is there a route that would improve the current LP
solution?''  This amounts to finding a route with negative reduced cost, which is an
\emph{Elementary Shortest Path Problem with Resource Constraints} (ESPPRC) --- a
well-studied but NP-hard problem in itself.

Integrality of the final solution is enforced by branching (standard branch-and-bound
applied within the column-generation framework).

\medskip
\textbf{Set Partitioning Formulation (alternative to network flow).}
Let $\Omega$ be the set of all feasible routes (routes with expected overflow probability
below threshold $\alpha$).  For each route $r \in \Omega$, let $a_{ir} \in \{0,1\}$
indicate whether customer $v_i$ is served by route $r$, and let $E[\mathcal{C}(r)]$ be
the expected execution cost.

\begin{block}{Set Partitioning Formulation (8.8)--(8.10)}
  \begin{align}
    \min \quad & \sum_{r \in \Omega} E[\mathcal{C}(r)]\, z_r \tag{8.8}\\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & \sum_{r \in \Omega} a_{ir}\, z_r = 1
      & \forall i \in V' \tag{8.9}\\
    & z_r \in \{0,1\} & \forall r \in \Omega \tag{8.10}
  \end{align}
  Here $z_r$ (``z sub r'') equals 1 if route $r$ is selected, and 0 otherwise.
\end{block}

The notation $a_{ir}$ (``a sub i r'') is a binary indicator: it equals 1 if customer
$i$ is visited by route $r$.  Constraint (8.9) is the \emph{set partitioning constraint}:
every customer must appear in exactly one selected route (each customer is served exactly
once).  Because $|\Omega|$ is exponential in $n$, the LP relaxation is solved by
column generation, generating only the most promising routes.

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Set Partitioning Objective}
  The objective $\sum_r E[\mathcal{C}(r)] z_r$ is simply the total expected cost of all
  selected routes.  Since $z_r \in \{0,1\}$, this sums the expected costs of the chosen
  routes.  The set partitioning constraint ensures that every customer is covered exactly
  once, preventing both omissions and double-counting.  The ``stochastic'' content is
  entirely hidden inside the $E[\mathcal{C}(r)]$ terms, which are computed using the DP
  recourse formula.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Benchmark Results for A Priori SVRP Methods}
% -----------------------------------------------------------

Gendreau, Laporte, and S\'eguin (1995, 1996) established a standard set of benchmark
instances for exact SVRP algorithms, based on the Augerat et al.\ test sets commonly used
for deterministic CVRP, adapted by replacing deterministic demands with random variables.
Table 8.1 in the book reports computational results for the integer L-shaped algorithm,
B\&P, and several heuristics.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{fig_benchmark_table.pdf}
  \caption{Benchmark results comparing exact methods and heuristics on standard SVRP
    instances.  Column ``$N$'' = number of customers; ``\%Gap'' = deviation of the
    heuristic from the exact optimal value.}
\end{figure}

The key empirical findings from the benchmark study are as follows.

\medskip
\textbf{Finding 1:} The integer L-shaped method is competitive with B\&P on instances
with $n \leq 30$ customers but is outperformed on larger instances where column
generation becomes more efficient because it generates only the most promising routes
rather than reasoning about all possible routes simultaneously.

\medskip
\textbf{Finding 2:} All exact methods become computationally intractable beyond
approximately $n = 50$ customers due to the combinatorial explosion of the solution
space (the number of feasible routes grows super-exponentially with $n$).

\medskip
\textbf{Finding 3:} High-quality heuristics such as Iterated Local Search (ILS) and the
RAM (Random Adaptive Method) consistently find solutions within 1--3\% of the proven
optimal value while running in a fraction of the time required by exact methods.  For
practical applications with $n > 50$, heuristics are the only viable option.

\begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Value of Stochastic Modelling}
  An important benchmark comparison (not just between algorithms, but between problem
  formulations) is: what is the cost of treating the stochastic problem as if it were
  deterministic?  The answer is that the \emph{expected} cost of the deterministic optimal
  solution applied in a stochastic environment is typically 5--15\% higher than the
  expected cost of the stochastic optimal solution.  This gap, called the \emph{value of
  the stochastic solution} (VSS), quantifies the benefit of planning explicitly for
  uncertainty.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{The Reoptimization Model}
% ===========================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{The Reoptimization Model: Online Decision Making}

The reoptimization model takes a fundamentally different approach from a priori
optimization.  Rather than committing to a fixed route and making only minor local
corrections, reoptimization treats route planning as an \emph{ongoing, adaptive process}:
each time a new piece of information is revealed (the demand at a just-visited customer,
or whether a customer is present), the system completely re-solves the routing problem
for all remaining customers.  Think of it as GPS navigation that continuously recalculates
the route based on real-time traffic conditions, rather than following a fixed plan.

\begin{block}{Formal Setting: Markov Decision Process (MDP)}
  At each decision epoch $t$, the system observes the current \textbf{state}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $v_t$: the current position of the vehicle (which node it is currently at),
    \item $L_t$: the remaining vehicle load (capacity $Q$ minus cumulative demand served;
      how much more can the vehicle carry before it must reload),
    \item $S_t \subseteq V'$: the set of customers not yet served (remaining workload),
    \item $\xi_t$ (xi, pronounced ``ksee''): all random information revealed so far
      (e.g., demands $d_1, \ldots, d_{t-1}$ observed at previously visited customers).
  \end{itemize}
  A \textbf{policy} $\mu$ (mu, read ``myoo'') is a decision rule that maps each state
  $(v_t, L_t, S_t, \xi_t)$ to an action: which customer to visit next, or whether to
  return to the depot to reload.
\end{block}

The \textbf{optimal policy} $\mu^*$ (mu-star) minimises the expected total cost:
\[
  \mu^* = \arg\min_\mu\; E\bigl[\mathcal{C}(\mu) \mid (v_0, L_0, S_0)\bigr]
\]
This is a \emph{Markov Decision Process} (MDP).  The symbol $\arg\min$ (``argument of
the minimum'') means: find the policy $\mu$ that achieves the smallest expected cost
$E[\mathcal{C}(\mu)]$, given the initial state (starting at the depot with full load and
all customers unserved).

\medskip
The state space of this MDP has $O(2^n \times Q)$ states (there are $2^n$ possible
subsets of remaining customers and $Q$ possible load levels).  For $n = 20$ customers
and $Q = 100$, this is approximately $10^8$ states --- far too large to enumerate
explicitly.  Solving the MDP exactly is therefore tractable only for toy instances.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{fig_mdp_reoptimization.pdf}
  \caption{The reoptimization model as a Markov Decision Process.  At each state
    (current position, load, remaining customers), the vehicle chooses the next action.
    Stochastic demand reveals the true load consumed at each step.}
\end{figure}

\framebreak

\textbf{The Rollout Policy (Secomandi 2001).}
The rollout policy is a computationally practical approximation to the MDP solution.
The name comes from ``rolling out'' a heuristic forward from the current state to
evaluate each candidate action.

At decision epoch $t$ with state $(v_t, L_t, S_t)$, the rollout policy proceeds as
follows:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{For each candidate next action} $a \in S_t \cup \{\text{depot}\}$ (either
    visit a remaining customer or return to depot):
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Commit tentatively to action $a$.
      \item Apply a \textbf{base heuristic} $\mu_0$ (mu sub zero, e.g., nearest-neighbour
        routing) to complete the route for the remaining customers $S_t \setminus \{a\}$.
      \item Estimate the expected cost of this completion via Monte Carlo simulation over
        the random demands of remaining customers.
    \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Select the action} $a^* = \arg\min_a \hat{E}[\text{cost from state }
    (v_t, L_t, S_t) \text{ given action } a]$.
\end{enumerate}

This is significantly cheaper than solving the full MDP because: (a) it only looks one
step ahead, and (b) it uses a fast heuristic for the remainder.  Secomandi (2001) proved
that the rollout policy is \emph{policy improving}: it always achieves expected cost at
most as large as the base heuristic $\mu_0$ used for completion, often substantially
better.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{fig_reoptimization_timeline.pdf}
  \caption{Timeline of the reoptimization model.  At each customer visit, new demand
    information is revealed (yellow dots) and the remaining plan is recomputed.  When
    a capacity crisis occurs (red arrow), the vehicle may return to the depot between
    planned stops.}
\end{figure}

\framebreak

\textbf{Computational Comparison: Reoptimization vs. A Priori.}
Secomandi (2001) and Hvattum, L\o kketangen, and Laporte (2006) conducted extensive
experiments comparing reoptimization policies to a priori methods.

\medskip
Reoptimization consistently outperforms a priori methods in expected cost, reducing total
travel expense by 3--8\% on standard benchmark instances.  This improvement comes from
the ability to exploit revealed demand information: knowing that customer $A$ has a higher
than expected demand allows the vehicle to reorder remaining visits to avoid a costly
restocking trip.

\medskip
However, the computational overhead of reoptimization is substantial.  Each decision
epoch requires approximately solving a new VRP instance (or at least evaluating many
candidate next actions via simulation).  For real-time logistics, this may be
problematic if routing decisions must be made in seconds.

\medskip
The \emph{dynamic programming relaxation} (Secomandi 2000, Hvattum et al.\ 2006) provides
lower bounds on the optimal policy cost, allowing the gap between approximate and true
optimal policies to be quantified and giving a sense of how much improvement is
theoretically available.

\begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: When Is Reoptimization Worth It?}
  Reoptimization is most valuable when the \emph{coefficient of variation} (standard
  deviation divided by mean demand; a measure of relative variability) is high, and when
  the recourse cost (cost of a restocking trip) is large relative to planned route cost.
  When demands are tightly clustered around their means (low variability), the a priori
  solution with simple restocking recourse is nearly as good as full reoptimization --- but
  far cheaper to compute.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{Probabilistic Formulation}
% ===========================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Probabilistic Formulation: Two-Stage Stochastic Program}

The probabilistic formulation provides the most general and rigorous mathematical
framework for stochastic VRP.  It treats the problem as a \emph{two-stage stochastic
program} --- a well-studied class from the field of stochastic programming.

In a two-stage stochastic program, decisions are made in two temporal stages:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stage 1 (here-and-now):} Decide the a priori route $x$ (the routing
    plan) \emph{before} any random variables are observed.  These are the ``strategic''
    decisions made under uncertainty.
  \item \textbf{Stage 2 (wait-and-see):} After a realization $\bm{\xi}$ (xi, bold,
    the actual random outcome --- e.g., actual customer demands) is observed,
    take a \emph{recourse action} $y(\bm{\xi})$ to deal with any constraint violations
    (e.g., return to depot when capacity is exceeded).
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Two-Stage Stochastic Program for VRPSD}
  Let $\bm{\xi} = (D_1, D_2, \ldots, D_n)$ be the random demand vector (bold
  $\bm{\xi}$, ``bold xi'', collects all uncertain demands into one vector).
  The two-stage VRPSD is:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:twostage}
    \min_{x}\; \underbrace{\sum_{(i,j)} c_{ij}\, x_{ij}}_{\text{Stage 1 cost}}
    +\; E_{\bm{\xi}}\bigl[\underbrace{Q(x, \bm{\xi})}_{\text{Stage 2 cost}}\bigr]
  \end{equation}
  The second-stage value function $Q(x, \bm{\xi})$ is the minimum-cost recourse
  action given the first-stage plan $x$ and the realisation $\bm{\xi}$:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:recourse}
    Q(x, \bm{\xi}) = \min_{y \geq 0}\; \bigl\{ q^{\top} y : W y \geq h(\bm{\xi}) - Tx \bigr\}
  \end{equation}
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Symbol Glossary for the Two-Stage Formulation}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $q$ (``q'', a vector): the \emph{recourse cost vector} whose entry $q_j$
      is the cost per unit of recourse action $j$ (e.g., cost of one restocking trip).
    \item $y \geq 0$ (``y'', a vector): the \emph{recourse variables} --- the
      corrective actions taken in Stage 2 (e.g., number of restocking trips).
    \item $W$ (capital W, a matrix): the \emph{recourse matrix}, describing how
      recourse actions $y$ affect constraint satisfaction in Stage 2.
    \item $T$ (capital T, a matrix): the \emph{technology matrix}, linking the first-stage
      decisions $x$ to the Stage 2 constraint right-hand side.
    \item $h(\bm{\xi})$: the right-hand side of Stage 2 constraints, which depends on
      the realization $\bm{\xi}$ (e.g., actual customer demands).
    \item $E_{\bm{\xi}}[\cdot]$ (expectation over $\bm{\xi}$): the expected value of the
      Stage 2 cost function over all possible random outcomes.
  \end{itemize}
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Two-Stage Formulation}
  The objective $\sum c_{ij} x_{ij} + E[Q(x, \bm{\xi})]$ says: choose the route plan
  $x$ that minimises the sum of (1) its direct travel cost and (2) its expected
  emergency-correction cost.  A route with low travel distance but poor risk management
  (high chance of capacity overflow) will have a large $E[Q]$ term.  A route that is
  slightly longer but carefully balances demands will have a smaller $E[Q]$.  The
  algorithm finds the route that best trades off these two considerations.
\end{alertblock}

For the specific VRPSD with restocking recourse, the expected second-stage cost for
route $\pi = (v_0, v_{i_1}, \ldots, v_{i_n}, v_0)$ is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expected-recourse}
  E[Q(\pi, \bm{D})]
  = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \bigl(c_{v_0, v_{i_k}} + c_{v_{i_k}, v_0}\bigr)
    \cdot P\!\bigl(\text{restocking trip occurs at position } k\bigr)
\end{equation}
This formula says: for each customer $v_{i_k}$ in the route, the contribution to the
expected recourse cost is the round-trip distance to the depot (cost
$c_{v_0, v_{i_k}} + c_{v_{i_k}, v_0}$) multiplied by the probability that a restocking
trip actually occurs at that position.  The total expected recourse cost is the sum of
these contributions over all customers.

\medskip
The \textbf{complete recourse property} holds here: for any first-stage route $x$ and
any realisation $\bm{\xi}$, the Stage 2 problem always has a feasible solution (we can
always return to the depot and reload, regardless of how many times this is needed).
This means the two-stage formulation is \emph{always consistent} --- there are no
``infeasible scenarios'' to worry about.

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example --- Expected Recourse Cost Computation}
  Route $\pi = (v_0, v_1, v_2, v_3, v_0)$, capacity $Q = 10$, independent Poisson
  (``Pwa-SOHN'') demands: $D_1 \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(3)$,
  $D_2 \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(4)$, $D_3 \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(5)$.
  Depot-to-customer round-trip costs: $2c_{0,1}=10$, $2c_{0,2}=8$, $2c_{0,3}=12$.

  \textbf{Expected recourse at $v_2$:}
  $P(D_1 + D_2 > 10) = P(\mathrm{Poisson}(7) > 10) \approx 0.084$.
  Recourse contribution $= 0.084 \times 8 = 0.672$.

  \textbf{Expected recourse at $v_3$:}
  $P(D_1+D_2+D_3 > 20)$ (second overflow) $\approx 0.003$.
  Recourse contribution $= 0.003 \times 12 = 0.036$.

  \textbf{Total expected recourse cost:} $\approx 0.672 + 0.036 = 0.708$ distance units.
\end{exampleblock}

\framebreak

\textbf{Single-Location Adaptation (Laporte and Louveaux 1993).}
The VRPSC (stochastic customers) can also be embedded in the two-stage framework.  Here
$\bm{\xi} = (Y_1, \ldots, Y_n) \in \{0,1\}^n$ (``Y sub i'', equals 1 if customer $i$
is present, 0 if absent).  The Stage 1 decision is the a priori visit ordering; the Stage
2 recourse is simply skipping absent customers (a ``zero-cost'' recourse in the pure
formulation, though skipping changes the route cost due to arc-cost structure).

For the VRPST (stochastic travel times), $\bm{\xi} = (T_{ij})$ for all arcs.  The
Stage 1 plan is the route sequence; the Stage 2 recourse handles time-window violations
through waiting or tardiness penalties.

\begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Unification}
  The two-stage formulation is powerful because it \emph{unifies} all three SVRP types
  (stochastic demands, customers, travel times) under a single mathematical framework.
  The differences between the three types appear only in how $\bm{\xi}$, $h(\bm{\xi})$,
  and $Q(x, \bm{\xi})$ are defined.  This unification suggests that algorithms developed
  for one type can often be adapted for the others, and that general-purpose stochastic
  programming solvers may eventually handle all three types.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{Stochastic Demands}
% ===========================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Stochastic Demands: Models and Recourse Strategies}

The \textbf{VRP with Stochastic Demands (VRPSD)} is the most extensively studied variant
of SVRP.  All customers are known in advance and will definitely be visited, but the
exact demand $D_i$ of each customer is a random variable whose value is revealed only
when the vehicle arrives at that customer's location.  The vehicle must serve all
customers; if the cumulative demand along a route exceeds the vehicle capacity $Q$, a
recourse action is required.

\medskip
Three main recourse strategies have been studied in the literature, each making a
different trade-off between simplicity and cost:

\begin{block}{Recourse Strategy 1: Corrective Restocking (Return to Depot)}
  When the vehicle arrives at customer $v_i$ and finds that it cannot serve the full
  demand $D_i$ without exceeding capacity, it \textbf{returns to the depot}, reloads to
  full capacity, and then returns to $v_i$ to continue.  The recourse cost is
  $c_{v_i, v_0} + c_{v_0, v_i}$ (the full round-trip distance to the depot from $v_i$).
  This is the most common strategy in the literature because it is simple, always
  feasible, and leads to tractable expected-cost formulas.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Recourse Strategy 2: Preventive Restocking}
  Rather than waiting for a capacity violation, the vehicle returns to the depot
  \emph{proactively} after a subset of customers, based on an estimate of the probability
  that remaining capacity is insufficient.  If $P(\text{overflow before end of route})
  \geq \tau$ (tau, a pre-set threshold), return to depot now.  This strategy avoids the
  cost of partial service interruptions but requires a more sophisticated decision rule
  and more frequent depot visits even when not strictly necessary.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Recourse Strategy 3: Partial Service (Penalty)}
  If the remaining vehicle capacity is $L$ and the customer's demand is $D > L$, serve
  $L$ units immediately and charge a \emph{penalty} $\kappa \cdot (D - L)$ (kappa,
  ``KAP-a'', times the unmet demand) for the unserved portion.  This is used when
  partial delivery is acceptable (e.g., bulk commodity delivery) or when the penalty
  for under-delivery is small.
\end{block}

\subsection{Chance Constraints for VRPSD}

An alternative approach to stochastic demands replaces the expected recourse cost
in the objective with \emph{chance constraints} --- hard probabilistic limits on the
risk of a capacity violation.

\begin{block}{Chance Constraint Formulation}
  For each route $S_k$ (the set of customers assigned to vehicle $k$), the
  \textbf{chance constraint} is:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:chance}
    P\!\left(\sum_{i \in S_k} D_i \;\leq\; Q\right) \;\geq\; 1 - \alpha
  \end{equation}
  where $\alpha$ (alpha, ``AL-fa'') $\in (0,1)$ is the maximum acceptable probability
  of a capacity violation (typically $\alpha = 0.05$ meaning at most a 5\% failure
  rate), and $Q$ is the vehicle capacity.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Symbol Explanation for Chance Constraint}
  Here $D_i$ is the random demand of customer $i$ (unknown at planning time).
  The sum $\sum_{i \in S_k} D_i$ is the total demand on route $k$ --- a random variable
  because it sums random individual demands.  The constraint says: the probability that
  this total demand is within the vehicle's capacity must be at least $1 - \alpha$.
  If $\alpha = 0.05$, the route is planned to be feasible with at least 95\% probability.
\end{block}

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Chance Constraint}
  Think of the chance constraint as a safety guarantee.  Instead of trying to guarantee
  that the route is \emph{always} feasible (which would be impossible with random demands),
  we settle for a high-probability guarantee.  With $\alpha = 0.05$, we accept that
  roughly 1 in 20 times, the vehicle will run out of capacity and need an emergency
  restocking trip.  Setting $\alpha$ smaller makes routes safer but shorter (fewer
  customers per vehicle), requiring more vehicles overall.  Setting $\alpha$ larger
  allows more customers per vehicle but risks more restocking trips.
\end{alertblock}

When demands are independently normally distributed, $D_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$
(where $\mu_i$ (mu sub i, ``MYOO'') is the mean demand and $\sigma_i^2$ (sigma sub i
squared, ``SIG-ma'') is the variance), the total route demand is:
\[
  \sum_{i \in S_k} D_i \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\sum_i \mu_i,\; \sum_i \sigma_i^2\right)
\]
and the chance constraint becomes the deterministic inequality:
\[
  \sum_{i \in S_k} \mu_i + z_{1-\alpha} \sqrt{\sum_{i \in S_k} \sigma_i^2} \;\leq\; Q
\]
where $z_{1-\alpha}$ is the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of the standard normal distribution
(for $\alpha = 0.05$: $z_{0.95} = 1.645$).  This means the expected total demand plus
$1.645$ standard deviations must fit within capacity.  This deterministic equivalent can
be handled by standard VRP algorithms.

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example --- Chance Constraint with Normal Demands}
  Route $S = \{C_1, C_2, C_3, C_4\}$ with independent normal demands:
  $D_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(4, 1.44)$, $D_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(6, 2.25)$,
  $D_3 \sim \mathcal{N}(3, 0.64)$, $D_4 \sim \mathcal{N}(5, 1.00)$.

  Total demand: $\mathcal{N}(18,\; 1.44+2.25+0.64+1.00) = \mathcal{N}(18, 5.33)$,
  so $\sigma_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{5.33} \approx 2.31$.

  For $\alpha = 0.05$ (95\% service level): $z_{0.95} = 1.645$.

  Required capacity: $Q \geq 18 + 1.645 \times 2.31 \approx 21.8$.

  So a vehicle with $Q = 22$ satisfies this route's 95\% chance constraint.

  With $Q = 20$: $P(D_{\text{total}} \leq 20) = \Phi\!\bigl(\frac{20-18}{2.31}\bigr)
  = \Phi(0.87) \approx 0.808$.  This is only an 80.8\% service level, which does
  \emph{not} satisfy the 95\% chance constraint.
\end{exampleblock}

  \caption{Left: individual customer demand distributions (four customers, different
    means and variances).  Right: total route demand distribution; the red dashed line

\subsection{Key Algorithms for VRPSD}

Several families of algorithms address the VRPSD, ranging from exact methods for small
instances to metaheuristics for large-scale problems.

\medskip
\textbf{Exact methods} include the integer L-shaped algorithm (Sections 3.1--3.2) and
Branch-and-Price methods based on the set partitioning formulation.  These achieve proven
optimality for instances with $n \leq 50$ customers, but run times grow rapidly beyond
that.

\medskip
\textbf{Tabu Search (TS) for VRPSD.}
Tabu search is a local search metaheuristic that avoids cycling by maintaining a
\emph{tabu list} --- a record of recently performed moves that are temporarily forbidden
(``taboo'').  The word ``tabu'' comes from the Tongan word meaning ``forbidden''.

Starting from an initial solution (e.g., one route per customer), at each iteration
the algorithm evaluates all neighbours (solutions reachable by one move, such as moving
a customer from one route to another or reversing a sub-sequence).  The best non-tabu
neighbour is selected, even if it is worse than the current solution.  The tabu list
prevents immediately undoing recent moves.  Over time, the search escapes local optima
and explores the solution space more broadly.

For VRPSD, the key modification from deterministic tabu search is that the \emph{objective
function evaluation} requires the $O(nQ)$ DP computation to compute $E[\mathcal{C}]$ for
each candidate solution --- this is the main computational bottleneck.

\medskip
\textbf{Simulated Annealing (SA) for VRPSD.}
Simulated annealing (SA) is a probabilistic metaheuristic inspired by the physical process
of heating a material and then slowly cooling it (annealing).  At high ``temperature'' $T_k$
(``T sub k''), the algorithm freely explores the solution space; as $T_k$ decreases
according to a \emph{cooling schedule}, it increasingly focuses on improving moves.

A candidate solution is accepted with probability:
\[
  P(\text{accept}) = \begin{cases}
    1 & \text{if } \Delta E \leq 0 \quad \text{(improvement)}\\
    \exp(-\Delta E / T_k) & \text{if } \Delta E > 0 \quad \text{(deterioration)}
  \end{cases}
\]
where $\Delta E$ (``Delta E'', delta = change) is the increase in expected cost.
At high temperatures, $\exp(-\Delta E / T_k) \approx 1$ and almost all moves are accepted.
At low temperatures, only improvements are accepted.  Just as slowly cooled metal reaches
a low-energy crystalline state while rapidly cooled metal gets stuck in a high-energy
amorphous state, slow annealing finds better solutions than greedy local search.

\begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Computational Bottleneck}
  For all VRPSD algorithms, evaluating the expected cost $E[\mathcal{C}(\pi)]$ is the
  computational bottleneck.  The DP recursion runs in $O(nQ)$ time per route for discrete
  distributions --- polynomial and fast.  For continuous distributions, numerical
  integration or Monte Carlo sampling is needed and can be much more expensive.
  Efficient evaluation procedures are therefore central to all practical VRPSD algorithms.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{Stochastic Customers}
% ===========================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Stochastic Customers: VRPSC Formulation}

In the \textbf{VRP with Stochastic Customers (VRPSC)}, each customer $v_i$ is present
(requires service) with probability $p_i$ (``p sub i'', a number between 0 and 1) and
absent with probability $1 - p_i$.  Customer presence is statistically independent across
customers --- whether one customer is home does not affect whether another is home.
The presence of each customer is revealed only when the vehicle arrives at that location.
All present customers must be served; absent customers are simply skipped.

This problem models many real-world scenarios.  In home delivery services, customers may
not be present to accept a parcel.  In field service routing, some service calls may be
resolved by phone before the technician arrives.  In direct sales, only a fraction of
visited addresses actually result in a sale.

\begin{block}{VRPSC Problem Statement (Laporte, Louveaux, and van Hamme 2002)}
  Given a complete graph $G = (V, E)$ with edge costs $c_{ij}$ (``c sub i j''), a depot
  $v_0$, customers $V' = \{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$, and presence probabilities
  $p_i \in (0,1)$ for each customer, find an \textbf{a priori route}
  $\pi = (v_0, v_{i_1}, \ldots, v_{i_n}, v_0)$ that minimises the \textbf{expected
  travel cost} over all possible patterns of customer presence and absence.
\end{block}

When a realization $\bm{\xi} = (\xi_1, \ldots, \xi_n) \in \{0,1\}^n$ (where $\xi_i = 1$
if customer $i$ is present) occurs during execution, the vehicle follows the a priori
route but skips all absent customers, jumping directly from the last present customer
before an absent group to the first present customer after it.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{fig_stochastic_customers.pdf}
  \caption{VRPSC illustration.  Left: a priori route visiting all potential customers
    (node size proportional to presence probability $p_i$).  Right: one realization where
    low-probability customers are absent (red crosses); the vehicle follows the shortened
    route, visiting only present customers.}
\end{figure}

\framebreak

\textbf{Single-Vehicle Expected Cost Formula.}
For the single-vehicle VRPSC, the expected cost of the a priori route $\pi$ has a
remarkable closed-form expression:

\begin{block}{Expected Cost Formula (Laporte, Louveaux, van Hamme 2002)}
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:vrpsc-single}
    E[C(\pi, \bm{p})]
    = \underbrace{c_{v_0, v_{i_1}} p_{i_1}}_{\text{depot to first}}
    + \underbrace{\sum_{a=1}^{n} \sum_{b=a+1}^{n} c_{v_{i_a}, v_{i_b}}\,
      p_{i_a}\, p_{i_b} \prod_{k=a+1}^{b-1}(1-p_{i_k})}_{\text{transitions between consecutive present customers}}
    + \underbrace{c_{v_{i_n}, v_0}\, p_{i_n}}_{\text{last to depot}}
  \end{equation}
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Symbol Explanation for the Expected Cost Formula}
  Each term $c_{v_{i_a}, v_{i_b}} \cdot p_{i_a} \cdot p_{i_b}
  \prod_{k=a+1}^{b-1}(1 - p_{i_k})$ has a clear meaning:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $c_{v_{i_a}, v_{i_b}}$: the direct travel cost from customer $a$ to customer
      $b$ (this arc is only used when $a$ and $b$ are consecutive in the executed route).
    \item $p_{i_a}$: probability that customer $a$ is present (so the arc starts at $a$).
    \item $p_{i_b}$: probability that customer $b$ is present (so the arc ends at $b$).
    \item $\prod_{k=a+1}^{b-1}(1-p_{i_k})$: probability that \emph{all} customers
      between $a$ and $b$ (in the a priori ordering) are absent --- so $a$ and $b$ are
      consecutive in the executed route.
  \end{itemize}
  Together, the product $p_{i_a} \cdot p_{i_b} \cdot \prod (1-p_{i_k})$ is the
  probability that the vehicle travels directly from $a$ to $b$ in a given execution.
\end{block}

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Expected Cost Formula}
  The formula decomposes the expected execution cost into contributions from each
  ``potential direct arc'' $(a, b)$.  The vehicle uses arc $(a, b)$ in an execution
  if and only if $a$ is present, $b$ is present, and every customer between them in the
  planned order is absent.  The formula sums these contributions over all pairs, giving
  the total expected distance.  Remarkably, it is an $O(n^2)$ computation for a given
  route --- linear in the presence probabilities $p_i$.
\end{alertblock}

\framebreak

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example --- VRPSC with 5 Customers}
  A priori route $\pi = (v_0, v_1, v_2, v_3, v_4, v_5, v_0)$ with presence probabilities
  $p_1=0.9,\; p_2=0.5,\; p_3=0.8,\; p_4=0.6,\; p_5=0.7$.
  Direct costs: $c_{0,1}=3,\; c_{1,2}=4,\; c_{1,3}=5,\; c_{2,3}=2,\; c_{3,4}=3,\;
  c_{4,5}=5,\; c_{5,0}=4$.

  One realisation: $\xi = (1,0,1,1,0)$ --- customers $v_1, v_3, v_4$ present;
  $v_2, v_5$ absent.  Executed route: $v_0 \to v_1 \to v_3 \to v_4 \to v_0$.
  Cost $= c_{0,1} + c_{1,3} + c_{3,4} + c_{4,0} = 3 + 5 + 3 + 3 = 14$.

  This realization occurs with probability:
  $p_1(1-p_2)p_3 p_4(1-p_5) = 0.9 \times 0.5 \times 0.8 \times 0.6 \times 0.3 = 0.0648$.

  We compute the contribution of arc $(v_1, v_3)$ to expected cost:
  $c_{1,3} \cdot p_1 \cdot (1-p_2) \cdot p_3 = 5 \times 0.9 \times 0.5 \times 0.8 = 1.80$.
  This is the expected cost due to the arc directly from $v_1$ to $v_3$ (when $v_2$
  is absent).  The total expected cost sums such terms over all pairs.
\end{exampleblock}

\medskip
\textbf{Multi-vehicle VRPSC.}
The multi-vehicle case requires both assigning customers to vehicles and sequencing them.
It can be modelled as a set partitioning problem (analogous to VRPSD) where the cost of
each route is its expected execution cost under the presence-probability model.

\textbf{Heuristics for VRPSC.}
Stewart and Golden (1983) proposed a savings-based heuristic: start with individual
single-customer routes to each customer, then merge routes greedily based on the expected
cost formula.  Bianchi et al.\ (2007) applied Iterated Local Search (ILS) to the
multi-vehicle VRPSC, achieving solutions within 1--2\% of optimal on standard benchmarks.

\section{Stochastic Travel Times}
% ===========================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Stochastic Travel Times: Time-Window Feasibility}

The \textbf{VRP with Stochastic Travel Times (VRPST)} introduces uncertainty into the
\emph{temporal dimension} of the problem.  All customers are known and will be visited,
and all demands are deterministic.  What is uncertain is how long it takes to travel from
one customer to the next, and possibly how long service takes at each customer.  This is
particularly relevant in urban logistics (where traffic creates unpredictable delays) and
in service engineering (where on-site work duration varies).

\begin{block}{VRPST Problem Setting}
  Travel time between nodes $i$ and $j$ is a random variable $T_{ij}$ (``T sub i j''),
  typically modelled as independently distributed with known distribution $F_{ij}$.
  Service time at customer $v_i$ is a random variable $S_i$ (``S sub i'').
  Each customer has a \textbf{time window} $[a_i, b_i]$ within which service must begin:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If the vehicle arrives before $a_i$ (the window opens), it must \textbf{wait}
      until $a_i$ (no additional travel cost, but time is lost).
    \item If the vehicle arrives after $b_i$ (the window closes), the visit is
      \textbf{infeasible} (hard time window) or incurs a \textbf{penalty} (soft time window).
  \end{itemize}
\end{block}

Let $A_k$ denote the \textbf{arrival time} at the $k$-th customer (read: ``A sub k'',
a random variable).  The recursion linking successive arrival times is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arrival-recursion}
  A_1 = T_{0,i_1}, \qquad
  A_{k+1} = \underbrace{\max(a_{i_{k+1}},\; A_k + S_{i_k})}_{\text{departure after waiting}} + T_{i_k, i_{k+1}}
\end{equation}
The $\max(\cdot)$ captures the \emph{waiting constraint}: the vehicle cannot leave
customer $v_{i_k}$ before $a_{i_{k+1}}$ opens at the next customer.  Here
$A_k + S_{i_k}$ is the departure time from customer $k$ after completing service,
and adding $T_{i_k, i_{k+1}}$ gives the arrival time at the next customer.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{fig_stochastic_travel_times.pdf}
  \caption{Left: distribution of arrival time at a customer relative to the time window
    $[e, l]$ (e = earliest, l = latest).  Green: feasible; orange: early arrival (wait);
    red: late arrival (window violation).
    Right: as the time window widens, the probability of feasible arrival increases.}
\end{figure}

\framebreak

\textbf{Service-Level Constraints.}
A natural approach is to impose a \emph{service-level constraint} at each customer:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:service-level}
  P\bigl(A_k \leq b_{i_k}\bigr) \;\geq\; 1 - \alpha_k
  \quad \forall k
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_k$ (``alpha sub k'') is the maximum acceptable probability of late arrival
at customer $k$.  For example, $\alpha_k = 0.05$ means the vehicle is required to arrive
on time with at least 95\% probability.

This is structurally identical to the chance constraint for stochastic demands: both
require that an event (capacity feasibility or time-window feasibility) holds with
sufficiently high probability.  The key difference is that arrival time $A_k$ depends
on the cumulative sum of stochastic travel times along the route, creating serial
dependencies that make the distribution harder to compute analytically.

Under the assumption $T_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{ij}, \sigma_{ij}^2)$ (normal
travel times), the arrival time $A_k$ is approximately normal (ignoring the waiting
constraint), and the service-level constraint becomes a linear inequality in the
route design variables.  The waiting constraint introduces non-linearity and typically
requires Monte Carlo approximation or convolution techniques.

\medskip
\textbf{Two-Vehicle Model (Laporte, Louveaux, and Mercure 1992).}
This classical model considers a single depot with two vehicles, stochastic travel and
service times, and time windows.  If the first vehicle fails to serve some customers
within their time windows due to random delays, those customers are reassigned to the
second vehicle.  The objective is to minimise expected total cost including the
reassignment cost.  This model introduced many of the key concepts used in later VRPST
work.

\medskip
\textbf{Model of Laporte, Louveaux, and Mercure (1992) for single vehicle:}
\begin{align}
  \min \quad & E\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n} c_{i_k i_{k+1}}\, T_{i_k i_{k+1}}
    + \gamma \sum_{k=1}^{n} \max(0,\; A_k - b_{i_k})\right]
  \tag{VRPST-obj}\\
  \text{s.t.} \quad & P(A_k \leq b_{i_k}) \geq 1-\alpha_k \quad \forall k
  \tag{VRPST-c}
\end{align}
where $\gamma$ (gamma, ``GAM-a'') is the \emph{tardiness penalty} (cost per unit of
time that a vehicle arrives late), and $\max(0, A_k - b_{i_k})$ is the tardiness
(late arrival time, set to zero if on time).  The objective minimises the sum of expected
travel cost and expected tardiness penalty.

\begin{block}{Symbol Summary for VRPST Formulation}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $c_{i_k i_{k+1}}$: travel cost on arc from customer $k$ to customer $k{+}1$
      (a fixed cost; note that travel \emph{time} $T_{i_k i_{k+1}}$ is random while
      travel \emph{cost} $c$ may be the expected time or a separate quantity).
    \item $\gamma$ (gamma): tardiness cost per unit time (how expensive it is to be late).
    \item $A_k$: random arrival time at customer $k$ (depends on all preceding travel times).
    \item $b_{i_k}$: latest acceptable arrival time at customer $k$ (deadline).
    \item $\alpha_k$ (alpha sub k): allowed late-arrival probability at customer $k$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example --- Service-Level Constraint Check}
  Route $\pi = (v_0, v_1, v_2, v_3, v_0)$ with:
  $T_{01} \sim \mathcal{N}(10, 4)$, $T_{12} \sim \mathcal{N}(8, 2.25)$,
  $S_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(5, 1)$.  Time window at $v_2$: $[20, 30]$.

  Arrival at $v_2$ (ignoring the waiting constraint for simplicity):
  $A_2 = T_{01} + S_1 + T_{12} \sim \mathcal{N}(10+5+8,\; 4+1+2.25) = \mathcal{N}(23, 7.25)$.
  Standard deviation: $\sigma_{A_2} = \sqrt{7.25} \approx 2.69$.

  $P(A_2 \leq 30) = \Phi\!\bigl(\frac{30-23}{2.69}\bigr) = \Phi(2.60) \approx 0.995$.
  (Here $\Phi$ (Phi, ``FI'') is the cumulative distribution function of the standard
  normal; $\Phi(z)$ is the probability of being at most $z$ standard deviations above
  the mean.)

  $P(A_2 \geq 20) = 1 - \Phi\!\bigl(\frac{20-23}{2.69}\bigr) = 1 - \Phi(-1.11) \approx 0.867$.

  Thus $P(20 \leq A_2 \leq 30) \approx 0.867$, an 86.7\% service level.
  For a required 90\% service level ($\alpha = 0.10$), this route is \emph{marginally
  infeasible} at $v_2$.  The planner must either depart earlier (increasing the mean
  arrival time buffer) or find a route where $v_2$ is visited later.
\end{exampleblock}

\medskip
\textbf{Solution Approaches for VRPST.}
Four main approaches have been used:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Deterministic Equivalent.}  For normally distributed travel times, chance
    constraints convert to deterministic linear inequalities, enabling standard
    deterministic VRP algorithms to be applied directly.

  \item \textbf{Sample Average Approximation (SAA).}  For general distributions, a large
    number of scenarios are sampled, and the expected cost is approximated by the
    average over those scenarios.  The resulting (large) deterministic problem can be
    solved by any VRP algorithm.  Convergence to the true expected cost as the sample
    size grows is guaranteed by the law of large numbers.

  \item \textbf{Robust Optimisation.}  Instead of minimising expected cost, minimise the
    \emph{worst-case} cost over all travel-time realisations in an uncertainty set
    $\mathcal{U}$ (``U-set'').  This avoids distributional assumptions but may yield
    overly conservative solutions.

  \item \textbf{Simulation-Based Local Search.}  Evaluate each candidate solution by
    Monte Carlo simulation, use the simulated cost as the objective for a local search
    metaheuristic.  Simple and flexible, but computationally expensive.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Propagation of Uncertainty}
  The central difficulty in VRPST is that uncertainty \emph{accumulates} along the route.
  The arrival time at the $k$-th customer depends on all preceding travel times, service
  times, and waiting periods.  A small delay early in the route can cascade into large
  delays later, making the distribution of $A_k$ hard to compute analytically and
  motivating simulation-based approaches.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{Conclusions and Future Research Directions}
% ===========================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Conclusions and Future Research Directions}

Chapter 8 has surveyed the three main variants of the Stochastic Vehicle Routing Problem
and the leading solution methodologies for each.  The chapter reflects the state of the
art as of 2014 and identifies the most important open questions for future research.

\medskip
\textbf{Summary of Main Results.}

\medskip
\textbf{1.\ A Priori Optimization} produces a fixed route that minimises expected cost
under a pre-specified recourse policy.  The integer L-shaped algorithm (Laporte and
Louveaux 1993, Gendreau et al.\ 1995) and branch-and-price are the leading exact methods,
capable of solving instances with up to 50 customers to proven optimality.  Metaheuristics
(ILS, tabu search, simulated annealing) scale to larger instances with solutions typically
within 2\% of the optimal known value.

\medskip
\textbf{2.\ Reoptimization} achieves better solution quality than a priori methods
(3--8\% cost reduction) by fully re-solving the routing problem after each random
revelation.  Rollout policies (Secomandi 2001) provide a computationally tractable
approximation that is guaranteed to be at least as good as the base heuristic used for
completion.

\medskip
\textbf{3.\ VRPSD} (stochastic demands) is handled via expected recourse cost minimisation
or chance constraints.  The DP-based evaluation of $E[\mathcal{C}(\pi)]$ in $O(nQ)$ time
is the central computational primitive.  Both exact and heuristic methods are mature and
well-benchmarked.

\medskip
\textbf{4.\ VRPSC} (stochastic customers) is handled via the closed-form expected cost
formula of Laporte et al.\ (2002), which is linear in the presence probabilities $p_i$.
This linearity makes sensitivity analysis tractable and greedy construction heuristics
effective.

\medskip
\textbf{5.\ VRPST} (stochastic travel times) requires managing time-window feasibility
under random arrival times.  Service-level constraints or robust formulations are
typically used; the analytical complexity of arrival-time distributions motivates
simulation-based approaches.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{fig_svrp_summary.pdf}
  \caption{Summary comparison of the three main SVRP variants across four dimensions:
    uncertain parameter, typical recourse action, solution approach, and key challenge.}
\end{figure}

\framebreak

\textbf{Future Research Directions.}

The authors identify six major open problems and promising directions for future research:

\medskip
\textbf{1.\ Multi-attribute SVRP.}  Real-world problems often combine multiple sources
of uncertainty simultaneously --- for example, stochastic demands \emph{and} stochastic
travel times, with time windows.  Very few models address this combination, and the
interaction between different uncertainty types creates entirely new algorithmic challenges.

\medskip
\textbf{2.\ Scalability.}  Current exact methods solve instances with at most $n \approx 50$
customers.  Developing exact or near-exact algorithms for $n = 100$--200 customers is
an important open problem.  Decomposition approaches (Dantzig--Wolfe relaxation,
Lagrangian relaxation) and parallel computing are promising directions.

\medskip
\textbf{3.\ Dynamic SVRP.}  In many applications, new customers arrive dynamically
\emph{during} the execution of routes (real-time or dynamic VRP).  The SVRP framework
studied here assumes that the set of potential customers is known in advance (even if
their demands are uncertain).  Extending to fully dynamic settings --- where both the
set of customers and their demands are unknown until revealed --- is a major open problem.

\medskip
\textbf{4.\ Data-Driven and Distributionally Robust Approaches.}  Historical demand and
travel-time data can be used to estimate probability distributions.  However, the
sensitivity of optimal solutions to the assumed distributions is poorly understood.
\emph{Distributionally robust optimization} (DRO) provides a framework that hedges against
distributional uncertainty, finding solutions that perform well even when the true
distribution differs from the estimated one.

\medskip
\textbf{5.\ Integration with Machine Learning.}  Recent work has begun combining deep
learning (graph neural networks, reinforcement learning) with SVRP.  These approaches
learn good policies directly from data, potentially bypassing the need for explicit
probabilistic models and exact optimisation.

\medskip
\textbf{6.\ Green SVRP.}  Environmental concerns motivate minimising expected
\emph{emissions} rather than travel distance, or incorporating uncertain fuel consumption
and vehicle range (critical for electric vehicle routing with uncertain battery drain and
charging availability).

\begin{alertblock}{Closing Thought}
  The field of stochastic VRP has matured substantially since the 1990s.  The three main
  variants each have rigorous mathematical models and practical algorithms, and benchmark
  instances enable meaningful comparison.  The most impactful future work will bridge the
  gap between academic models and real-world operational complexity, drawing on modern
  computational tools --- machine learning, cloud-scale optimisation, and real-time data
  integration --- while maintaining the mathematical rigour that has characterised the
  best SVRP research.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Python: Dynamic Programming for VRPSD Expected Recourse Cost}
The following Python function implements the core computational primitive for VRPSD:
computing the expected total recourse cost for a given route and a set of independent
discrete demand distributions, using the DP convolution described in Section 2.

The function works by maintaining a probability distribution over ``cumulative demand so
far'' and updating it customer by customer.  Whenever the cumulative demand crosses a
multiple of the vehicle capacity $Q$, a restocking trip is counted and its cost is added
to the expected total.

\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np

def expected_recourse_cost(route, demands_pmf, capacity, costs_to_depot):
    """
    Compute expected recourse cost for a VRPSD route via DP.

    Parameters
    ----------
    route          : list of int  -- customer visit order
    demands_pmf    : dict {cust_id: {demand_value: probability}}
    capacity       : int          -- vehicle capacity Q
    costs_to_depot : dict {cust_id: round_trip_cost_to_depot}

    Returns: expected total recourse cost (float)
    """
    n   = len(route)
    max_q = capacity * (n + 1)   # upper bound on cumulative demand
    dp = np.zeros(max_q + 1)
    dp[0] = 1.0                  # initially, cumulative demand = 0 with prob 1
    expected_extra = 0.0

    for cust in route:
        vals  = list(demands_pmf[cust].keys())
        probs = list(demands_pmf[cust].values())
        new_dp = np.zeros(max_q + 1)

        for d, p in zip(vals, probs):
            d = int(d)
            # For each possible previous cumulative demand q_old,
            # the new cumulative demand is q_new = q_old + d.
            # Check whether a capacity boundary is crossed.
            for q_old in range(max_q + 1 - d):
                q_new = q_old + d
                prob_state = dp[q_old] * p
                trips_before = q_old // capacity
                trips_after  = q_new  // capacity
                if trips_after > trips_before:
                    # One (or more) restocking trips triggered
                    expected_extra += (prob_state
                        * costs_to_depot[cust]
                        * (trips_after - trips_before))
                new_dp[q_new] += prob_state

        dp = new_dp

    return expected_extra

# ---- Example: 4 customers, Q=10, uniform discrete demands ----
if __name__ == '__main__':
    route = [1, 2, 3, 4]
    demands_pmf = {
        1: {3: 0.5, 4: 0.5},   # demand is 3 or 4 with equal probability
        2: {2: 0.5, 3: 0.5},
        3: {4: 0.5, 5: 0.5},
        4: {1: 0.5, 2: 0.5},
    }
    capacity = 10
    # Round-trip costs to depot from each customer
    costs_to_depot = {1: 10, 2: 8, 3: 12, 4: 9}
    ec = expected_recourse_cost(route, demands_pmf, capacity, costs_to_depot)
    print(f"Expected recourse cost: {ec:.4f}")
    # Expected: ~ 6.25 (overflow at position 3 roughly 50% of the time)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Python: VRPSC Expected Cost (Laporte et al.\ Formula)}
This function computes the expected travel cost for the single-vehicle VRPSC using the
closed-form formula of Laporte, Louveaux, and van Hamme (2002).  The formula is $O(n^2)$
per evaluation and linear in the presence probabilities, making it ideal for use inside
heuristic optimisation loops.
\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np

def vrpsc_expected_cost(route, probs, cost_fn):
    """
    Expected travel cost for single-vehicle VRPSC.

    Parameters
    ----------
    route   : list of int  -- a priori customer order (depot=0 excluded)
    probs   : dict {customer_id: p_i}  -- presence probability
    cost_fn : callable (i, j) -> float -- travel cost between nodes

    Returns: expected cost (float)
    """
    n = len(route)
    ec = 0.0

    # 1. Cost from depot (node 0) to first present customer
    prob_all_absent_so_far = 1.0
    for k, cust in enumerate(route):
        # P(cust is the FIRST present customer)
        prob_first = probs[cust] * prob_all_absent_so_far
        ec += cost_fn(0, cust) * prob_first
        prob_all_absent_so_far *= (1.0 - probs[cust])

    # 2. Cost of travelling between consecutive present customers
    for i_idx in range(n):
        ci = route[i_idx]
        for j_idx in range(i_idx + 1, n):
            cj = route[j_idx]
            # P(ci present, cj present, all between absent)
            p_consec = probs[ci] * probs[cj]
            for m in range(i_idx + 1, j_idx):
                p_consec *= (1.0 - probs[route[m]])
            ec += cost_fn(ci, cj) * p_consec

    # 3. Cost from last present customer back to depot
    prob_all_absent_after = 1.0
    for k in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        cust = route[k]
        prob_last = probs[cust] * prob_all_absent_after
        ec += cost_fn(cust, 0) * prob_last
        prob_all_absent_after *= (1.0 - probs[cust])

    return ec

# ---- Example ------------------------------------------------
if __name__ == '__main__':
    route = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
    probs = {1: 0.9, 2: 0.5, 3: 0.8, 4: 0.6, 5: 0.7}
    C = np.array([
        [0,  3,  5,  4,  6,  7],
        [3,  0,  4,  5,  4,  6],
        [5,  4,  0,  2,  3,  5],
        [4,  5,  2,  0,  3,  3],
        [6,  4,  3,  3,  0,  5],
        [7,  6,  5,  3,  5,  0],
    ])
    def cost_fn(i, j): return C[i, j]
    ec = vrpsc_expected_cost(route, probs, cost_fn)
    print(f"Expected travel cost (VRPSC): {ec:.4f}")
    # Should be approximately 14.3 for these inputs
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Python: Monte Carlo Simulation for SVRP Policy Evaluation}
Monte Carlo simulation provides a general, flexible tool for evaluating the expected cost
of \emph{any} SVRP solution under \emph{any} demand distribution and recourse policy.
The idea is simple: sample many realisations of the random demands, simulate the route
execution for each, compute the total cost, and average over all simulations.

The law of large numbers guarantees that the sample average converges to the true expected
cost as the number of samples grows.  For $n_{\text{samples}} = 10{,}000$ samples, the
standard error of the estimate is typically less than 0.5\% of the true expected cost.
\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np

def svrp_monte_carlo(route, demand_samplers, capacity,
                     cost_matrix, n_samples=10000, seed=42):
    """
    Monte Carlo estimate of expected VRPSD cost (restocking recourse).

    Parameters
    ----------
    route           : list of int -- customer visit order
    demand_samplers : list of callables -- demand_samplers[k]() samples
                      demand for customer route[k]
    capacity        : float -- vehicle capacity Q
    cost_matrix     : 2D array -- cost_matrix[i][j] = c(i,j), depot=0
    n_samples       : int -- number of MC realisations
    seed            : int -- random seed for reproducibility

    Returns: (mean_cost, standard_error) both floats
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    scenario_costs = []

    for _ in range(n_samples):
        # Sample one realization of all customer demands
        demands = [s() for s in demand_samplers]

        # Simulate route execution with corrective restocking
        total_cost = 0.0
        load = 0.0          # current vehicle load (starts empty)
        prev = 0            # current position (0 = depot)

        for k, cust in enumerate(route):
            d = demands[k]
            if load + d > capacity:
                # Capacity exceeded: emergency trip back to depot and return
                total_cost += cost_matrix[prev][0]   # drive to depot
                total_cost += cost_matrix[0][cust]   # drive back to customer
                load = 0.0
                prev = 0
            total_cost += cost_matrix[prev][cust]    # planned arc
            load += d
            prev = cust

        total_cost += cost_matrix[prev][0]           # final return to depot
        scenario_costs.append(total_cost)

    costs = np.array(scenario_costs)
    se = costs.std() / np.sqrt(n_samples)            # standard error
    return float(costs.mean()), float(se)
