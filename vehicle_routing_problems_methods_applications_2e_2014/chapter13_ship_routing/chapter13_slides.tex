\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Chapter Overview}
  This chapter covers the rich family of ship routing and scheduling problems
  that arise in \textbf{industrial} and \textbf{tramp} maritime transportation.
  Unlike road vehicle routing, ship routing involves enormous cargo quantities,
  multi-week planning horizons, complex contractual arrangements, and
  variable fuel costs that depend on speed in a strongly non-linear way.

  \medskip
  \textbf{Topics covered in this chapter:}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Introduction} --- the economic and environmental context of
          maritime shipping; the three main shipping segments.
    \item \textbf{Cargo Routing and Scheduling} --- the core optimisation
          problem for industrial and tramp shipping; exact and heuristic solution methods.
    \item \textbf{Maritime Inventory Routing (MIR)} --- integrating inventory
          management at ports with ship scheduling decisions.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic and Stochastic Ship Routing} --- handling weather
          uncertainty, port congestion, and real-time cargo spot offers.
    \item \textbf{Conclusions and Future Directions}.
  \end{enumerate}

  \medskip
  \begin{alertblock}{Key Takeaway of the Chapter}
    Maritime transportation is responsible for more than 80\% of world trade
    by volume.  Optimising ship routing and scheduling can reduce operational
    costs by 10--20\% and substantially cut CO$_2$ emissions, making this
    one of the most impactful application areas of vehicle routing research.
  \end{alertblock}

\section{Introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Introduction: The Maritime Transportation Sector}
  International seaborne trade handles more than \textbf{8 billion tonnes} of
  goods annually (UNCTAD 2011), making maritime transport the backbone of
  global commerce.  The ship routing and scheduling problem is the operational
  challenge of deciding \emph{which ship goes where, in what order, carrying
  what cargo, and at what speed}.

  \medskip
  According to the book, international trade depends on maritime transportation
  for more than eight billion tonnes of goods per year.  Shipping produces more
  CO$_2$ than aviation --- motivating better routing for environmental as well
  as economic reasons.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Three Shipping Segments --- Definitions}
    Maritime transport is traditionally divided into three segments.
    Understanding which segment applies determines the structure of the
    optimisation problem.

    \textbf{Industrial shipping} means the shipowner controls both the
    fleet \emph{and} the cargo.  A mining company, for instance, might own both
    the iron-ore mine and the ships that deliver the ore to steel plants.
    Because the company is optimising its \emph{own} supply chain, the
    objective is to \textbf{minimise total transport cost}, and all cargoes
    must be served.

    \textbf{Liner shipping} means ships operate on \emph{fixed, published
    schedules} that they follow regardless of whether the ship is full ---
    analogous to a scheduled airline or bus service.  Ships visit the same
    sequence of ports (the ``rotation'') every week or two weeks.  Shippers
    pay per container slot.  The optimisation problem is primarily one of
    \textbf{network design}: which ports to connect, at what frequency, with
    how many ships.

    \textbf{Tramp shipping} means the ship follows the cargo, acting like a
    maritime taxi service.  The tramp operator bids for cargoes on the spot
    market and holds long-term contracts, then decides which cargoes to accept
    and how to route the fleet to \textbf{maximise profit}.  Accepted cargoes
    generate revenue; mandatory contract cargoes must be served (possibly by
    a hired spot ship if own fleet is insufficient).
  \end{block}

             shipping integrates cargo and fleet ownership.  Liner shipping

  \end{figure}

  \begin{exampleblock}{A Concrete Example of the Difference}
    Suppose a petroleum product (naphtha) needs to be moved from a refinery
    in Rotterdam to a chemical plant in Houston.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item An \textbf{industrial shipper} that owns both the refinery and
            the destination plant would schedule one of its own product tankers.
            Routing is an \emph{internal} cost-minimisation decision.
      \item A \textbf{liner carrier} would take the cargo as a paying slot
            on its regular Atlantic service, which departs every two weeks
            regardless of the load.
      \item A \textbf{tramp operator} would bid for the cargo on the spot
            market, assign whichever ship is most conveniently positioned,
            and negotiate the freight rate based on current supply and demand.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{exampleblock}

  \begin{alertblock}{Why Optimisation Matters}
    Operating a fleet of product tankers costs tens of millions of USD per year.
    Fuel alone accounts for 50--70\% of voyage costs, and fuel consumption
    scales as the \emph{cube} of ship speed.  A 10\% reduction in average speed
    reduces fuel consumption by about 27\%.  Optimising routes and speed
    profiles therefore has enormous economic and environmental impact.
    According to Vidal (2007), shipping produces twice as much CO$_2$
    as aviation; this makes efficient ship routing a pressing sustainability
    challenge.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section{Cargo Routing and Scheduling}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Section 13.2: Cargo Routing and Scheduling --- Overview}
  This section covers the core problem of \textbf{routing a heterogeneous
  fleet of ships to serve a set of cargoes}.  The problem arises in both
  industrial and tramp shipping contexts and is fundamentally a
  \emph{pickup-and-delivery} vehicle routing problem on a maritime network.

  \medskip
  Unlike classical road VRP, the maritime pickup-and-delivery problem has
  several distinctive features that shape the mathematical formulation:

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Cargo quantities are very large (thousands of tonnes), and a
          single ship may carry only \emph{one} cargo at a time (full
          ship-load) or several smaller cargoes simultaneously.
    \item Some cargoes are \textbf{mandatory} (long-term contracts that
          \emph{must} be served, possibly by hiring a spot ship at extra cost).
          Others are \textbf{optional} (spot market cargoes that can be
          declined).
    \item The planning horizon is typically \emph{weeks to months}, not hours.
    \item Each ship has its own \emph{origin} (current position) and an
          \emph{artificial destination} node representing the end of its
          planning period.
    \item Time windows are specified at each port for both loading and
          discharging, often with \emph{hard} limits enforced by tidal and
          terminal constraints.
  \end{enumerate}

  \medskip
  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{pickup_delivery_network.png}
    \caption{A tramp ship pickup-and-delivery network.  Green diamonds are

  \end{figure}
\end{frame}

\subsection{The Maritime Pickup-and-Delivery Problem}
%% -------------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{The Maritime Pickup-and-Delivery Problem: Network Structure}
  \begin{block}{Problem Definition}
    We are given a fleet $K$ of ships and a set $N^C$ of \emph{mandatory
    cargoes} together with a set $N^O$ of \emph{optional} (spot-market)
    cargoes.  Each cargo $i$ has a pickup port and a delivery port, a
    quantity $Q_i$ (tonnes), and a revenue $R_i$ per unit transported.
    The goal is to assign cargoes to ships and determine each ship's route
    and schedule to \textbf{maximise profit} (revenue minus voyage costs).
  \end{block}

  \medskip
  \textbf{Network structure.}  For each ship $k \in K$ we build a directed
  graph.  Let $N_k^P$ denote the set of \emph{pickup} nodes for ship $k$
  (one per cargo that ship $k$ could potentially carry), and let $N_k^D$
  be the corresponding \emph{delivery} nodes.  In addition, each ship $k$
  has an artificial \emph{origin} node $o(k)$ (its current location at the
  start of the planning period) and an artificial \emph{destination} node
  $d(k)$ (representing ``done'').

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Notation (read carefully)}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $K$ --- the set of all ships in the fleet.
      \item $N^C$ --- set of \textbf{mandatory} cargo indices (contracts that must be served).
      \item $N^O$ --- set of \textbf{optional} (spot market) cargo indices.
      \item $N_k = N_k^P \cup N_k^D \cup \{o(k), d(k)\}$ --- all nodes
            relevant to ship $k$.
      \item $A_k$ --- set of \textbf{arcs} for ship $k$; arc $(i,j) \in A_k$
            means ship $k$ can sail directly from node $i$ to node $j$.
      \item $Q_i$ --- the \textbf{quantity} (load in tonnes) of cargo $i$.
      \item $Q_k^C$ --- the \textbf{capacity} (maximum load in tonnes) of ship $k$.
      \item $R_i$ --- the \textbf{revenue} earned per tonne of cargo $i$ transported.
      \item $C_{ijk}$ --- the \textbf{voyage cost} for ship $k$ on arc $(i,j)$
            (fuel, port fees, canal tolls, etc.).
      \item $C_i^S$ --- the \textbf{spot market cost} --- the fee paid to hire
            an outside (spot) ship to carry mandatory cargo $i$ if none of
            our own ships can serve it.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Decision Variables}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $x_{ijk} \in \{0, 1\}$ --- equals 1 if ship $k$ sails \emph{directly}
            from node $i$ to node $j$; 0 otherwise.
            The subscript $ijk$ is read ``x sub i-j-k.''
      \item $z_i \in \{0, 1\}$ --- equals 1 if mandatory cargo $i \in N^C$
            is served by a \emph{spot market} ship rather than our own fleet.
      \item $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ --- equals 1 if optional cargo $i \in N^O$ is
            actually transported, 0 if declined.
      \item $t_{ik} \geq 0$ --- the \textbf{time} at which ship $k$ begins
            service at node $i$.
      \item $l_{ik} \geq 0$ --- the \textbf{load} (weight in tonnes) on ship
            $k$ when it \emph{departs} from node $i$.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{The MPDP Mathematical Model: Objective and Route Constraints}
  The tramp ship routing and scheduling problem is formulated as a
  \textbf{Mixed-Integer Programme (MIP)}.  We state the objective function
  and the key constraint groups, explaining each in plain language.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Objective Function (13.1) --- Maximise Profit}
    \begin{equation}
      \max \; \underbrace{\sum_{i \in N^C} R_i Q_i}_{\text{mandatory revenue}}
           + \underbrace{\sum_{i \in N^O} R_i Q_i y_i}_{\text{optional revenue}}
           - \underbrace{\sum_{k \in K} \sum_{(i,j) \in A_k} C_{ijk} x_{ijk}}_{\text{voyage costs}}
           - \underbrace{\sum_{i \in N^C} C_i^S z_i}_{\text{spot market cost}}
      \tag{13.1}
    \end{equation}
    Here $R_i$ is revenue per tonne, $Q_i$ is quantity in tonnes, $y_i$
    (y sub $i$) equals 1 if optional cargo $i$ is accepted, $C_{ijk}$ is
    the voyage cost for ship $k$ on leg $(i,j)$, $x_{ijk}$ equals 1 if
    that leg is used, $C_i^S$ is the spot-market surcharge for covering
    mandatory cargo $i$ with a hired ship, and $z_i$ equals 1 if that
    option is exercised.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Objective Function}
    The formula computes net profit: the first two terms are gross revenue
    (mandatory cargoes always earn revenue since they must be transported);
    the last two terms are costs.  Hiring a spot ship ($C_i^S z_i$) is
    expensive, so the solver prefers to assign mandatory cargoes to own ships.
    However, if a mandatory cargo is geographically inconvenient, paying the
    spot premium may still be optimal.  Optional cargoes ($y_i$) are accepted
    only if the extra revenue exceeds the extra voyage cost.
  \end{alertblock}

  \bigskip
  \textbf{Constraint (13.2) --- Every mandatory cargo is served:}
  \begin{equation}
    \sum_{k \in K} \sum_{j \in N_k} x_{ijk} + z_i = 1
    \qquad \forall\, i \in N^C
    \tag{13.2}
  \end{equation}
  The double sum counts how many of our ships pick up cargo $i$.  Adding
  $z_i$ (equals 1 if a spot ship covers it) gives exactly 1, ensuring the
  cargo is served once and only once.

  \medskip
  \textbf{Constraint (13.3) --- Optional cargo is accepted iff visited:}
  \begin{equation}
    \sum_{k \in K} \sum_{j \in N_k} x_{ijk} - y_i = 0
    \qquad \forall\, i \in N^O
    \tag{13.3}
  \end{equation}
  For optional cargo $i$: the indicator $y_i = 1$ if and only if some ship
  actually visits the pickup node.  If no ship goes there, $y_i = 0$ and
  the cargo is simply not carried --- there is no penalty.

  \medskip
  \textbf{Constraint (13.4) --- Each ship departs its origin exactly once:}
  \begin{equation}
    \sum_{j \in N_k} x_{o(k)jk} = 1 \qquad \forall\, k \in K
    \tag{13.4}
  \end{equation}
  This guarantees every ship actually departs from its starting position
  (it cannot ``stay at home'').

  \medskip
  \textbf{Constraint (13.5) --- Flow conservation (no teleportation):}
  \begin{equation}
    \sum_{j \in N_k} x_{ijk} - \sum_{j \in N_k} x_{jik} = 0
    \qquad \forall\, k \in K,\;
    i \in N_k \setminus \{o(k), d(k)\}
    \tag{13.5}
  \end{equation}
  For every intermediate node $i$ (not the origin or the artificial destination),
  the number of times ship $k$ \emph{arrives} at $i$ equals the number of times
  it \emph{departs}.  This prevents ships from ``disappearing'' or being duplicated.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{The MPDP Mathematical Model: Load and Time Constraints}
  \textbf{Constraint (13.6) --- Each ship arrives at its destination:}
  \begin{equation}
    \sum_{i \in N_k} x_{id(k)k} = 1 \qquad \forall\, k \in K
    \tag{13.6}
  \end{equation}
  Every ship must terminate its voyage at the artificial destination node.
  Combined with (13.4) and (13.5), this ensures a valid route from $o(k)$
  to $d(k)$ for each ship.

  \medskip
  \textbf{Constraint (13.7) --- Load increases at a pickup node $j$:}
  \begin{equation}
    l_{ik} + Q_j - l_{jk} - Q_k^C (1 - x_{ijk}) \leq 0
    \qquad \forall\, k \in K,\; (i,j) \in A_k,\; j \in N_k^P
    \tag{13.7}
  \end{equation}
  Here $l_{ik}$ is the load on ship $k$ \emph{after departing} node $i$.
  At a pickup node $j$, the cargo $Q_j$ is added: $l_{jk} \geq l_{ik} + Q_j$.
  The term $Q_k^C (1 - x_{ijk})$ is a \textbf{``big-M'' relaxation}: if ship $k$
  does \emph{not} use arc $(i,j)$ (i.e., $x_{ijk}=0$), this term adds a large
  positive number, making the constraint trivially satisfied.  The constraint
  only ``activates'' on arcs actually used.

  \medskip
  \textbf{Constraint (13.8) --- Load decreases at a delivery node:}
  \begin{equation}
    l_{ik} - Q_j - l_{n+j,k} - Q_k^C (1 - x_{i,n+j,k}) \leq 0
    \qquad j \in N_k^P
    \tag{13.8}
  \end{equation}
  At a delivery node (index $n+j$, where $n$ is the number of cargoes and
  $j$ is the index of the associated pickup), the load decreases by $Q_j$.
  The pair (pickup node $j$, delivery node $n+j$) together represent one
  complete cargo movement.

  \medskip
  \textbf{Constraints (13.9)--(13.10) --- Capacity bounds:}
  \begin{align}
    \sum_{j \in N_k} Q_i x_{ijk} &\leq l_{ik} \leq \sum_{j \in N_k} Q_k^C x_{ijk}
    \qquad \forall\, k \in K,\; i \in N_k^P \tag{13.9} \\
    0 &\leq l_{n+i,k} \leq \sum_{j \in N_k} (Q_k^C - Q_i) x_{n+i,jk}
    \qquad \forall\, k \in K,\; i \in N_k^P \tag{13.10}
  \end{align}
  These ensure the load is between the cargo quantity (at least as much as
  has been picked up) and the ship's capacity $Q_k^C$ at all times.

  \medskip
  \textbf{Constraint (13.11) --- Time-window feasibility:}
  \begin{equation}
    t_{ik} + T_{ik}^P + T_{ijk}^S - t_{jk} - M_{ijk}(1 - x_{ijk}) \leq 0
    \qquad \forall\, k \in K,\; (i,j) \in A_k
    \tag{13.11}
  \end{equation}
  Here $T_{ik}^P$ (T sub $ik$ superscript P) is the \textbf{port handling time}
  (loading or unloading) at node $i$ for ship $k$; $T_{ijk}^S$ is the
  \textbf{sailing time} from $i$ to $j$ at the ship's standard speed; and
  $M_{ijk}$ is a big-M constant.  The constraint says: if ship $k$ travels
  from $i$ to $j$, then $t_{jk} \geq t_{ik} + T_{ik}^P + T_{ijk}^S$
  (it cannot arrive at $j$ before it has finished at $i$ and sailed across).

  \medskip
  \textbf{Constraint (13.12) --- Pickup before delivery (pairing):}
  \begin{equation}
    \sum_{j \in N_k} x_{ijk} - \sum_{j \in N_k} x_{n+i,jk} = 0
    \qquad \forall\, k \in K,\; i \in N_k^P
    \tag{13.12}
  \end{equation}
  Ship $k$ must visit the pickup node of cargo $i$ if and only if it also
  visits the delivery node.  You cannot deliver what you have not picked up,
  and you cannot pick up without delivering.

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: The Big-M Technique}
    Constraints (13.7), (13.8), and (13.11) all use the ``big-M'' trick.
    A big constant $M$ is added or subtracted based on a binary decision
    variable.  This conditionally deactivates a constraint when a particular
    route arc is not used.  The choice of $M$ affects numerical stability:
    $M$ should be as small as possible while still being large enough to
    make the constraint trivially feasible when the arc is unused.
  \end{alertblock}

\subsection{Application-Driven Extensions}
%% -------------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Extension: Split Loads (Cargo Splitting)}
  In the standard formulation above, each cargo is assigned to
  \emph{exactly one} ship.  However, some industrial problems require
  \textbf{cargo splitting}: distributing a single cargo across multiple ships.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Why Split Loads?}
    Two practical situations motivate cargo splitting:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Oversized cargo}: cargo quantity $Q_i$ exceeds any single
            ship's capacity $Q_k^C$.  Without splitting, the cargo cannot be
            served at all by the own fleet.
      \item \textbf{Delivery urgency}: splitting across two ships allows
            partial delivery sooner, satisfying time-window constraints that
            a single ship would miss.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{cargo_splitting.png}
    \caption{Left: without splitting, one ship carries the full cargo and the
             other sails empty on this leg (wasted capacity).  Right: with
             splitting, both ships carry half the cargo, improving utilisation
             and potentially enabling faster delivery through parallelism.}
  \end{figure}

  \textbf{Model extension.}  When split loads are allowed, additional
  continuous variables $q_{ijk}$ (q sub $i$-$j$-$k$, representing the
  quantity of cargo $i$ carried by ship $k$ on arc $(i,j)$) are introduced.
  A new constraint ensures the total quantity delivered across all ships
  equals the cargo requirement:
  \begin{equation}
    \sum_{k \in K} \sum_{j \in N_k} q_{ijk} = Q_i
    \qquad \forall\, i
    \tag{13.35 (extended)}
  \end{equation}
  where the sum runs over all ships $k$ and all successor nodes $j$.

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: When Splitting Solves an Infeasibility}
    Suppose two mandatory cargoes and two ships, each with capacity 50 units:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Cargo A: quantity 70 units.  A single ship (capacity 50) cannot
            carry it.  \textbf{Without splitting}: infeasible; must hire a
            spot ship at cost $C_A^S$.
      \item Cargo B: quantity 30 units.  Fits on any ship.
    \end{itemize}
    \textbf{With splitting}: Ship~1 takes 50 units of Cargo~A; Ship~2 takes
    the remaining 20 units of Cargo~A together with 30 units of Cargo~B
    (total load = 50, within capacity).  Both cargoes are served without
    hiring a spot ship.  The saving equals $C_A^S$ minus the extra routing
    cost from coordinating two ships to the same pickup/delivery ports.
  \end{exampleblock}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Reference}
    Stalh\aa ne et al.\ (2012) develop a branch-and-cut algorithm for the
    split-load variant.  Their formulation adds linking constraints between
    the arc-flow variables $x_{ijk}$ and the quantity variables $q_{ijk}$,
    ensuring feasibility of the load plan.  Their branch-and-cut outperforms
    a direct MIP solver on instances with up to 30 cargoes and 6 ships.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Extension: Variable Sailing Speed}
  Real-world ship routing involves a powerful lever unavailable in road VRP:
  the ship's operator can \textbf{freely choose the speed} on each leg.
  This matters because the relationship between speed and fuel consumption
  is strongly non-linear, making slow steaming economically attractive.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{The Cubic Fuel Consumption Law}
    Let $v$ (v, for velocity measured in \textbf{knots}, where 1 knot is
    approximately 1.85 km/h) be the speed of a ship on a particular leg.
    The fuel consumption rate $F(v)$ (in tonnes of fuel per day) is
    approximately:
    \begin{equation}
      F(v) \;=\; \alpha \cdot v^3
    \end{equation}
    where $\alpha$ (alpha) is a ship-specific constant.
    This cubic relationship means: \textbf{reducing speed by 10\%} reduces
    fuel consumption by approximately $1 - 0.9^3 \approx 27\%$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Cubic Law}
    If you double the speed ($v \to 2v$), fuel consumption increases
    \emph{eightfold} ($2^3 = 8$).  Conversely, if you halve the speed,
    fuel consumption falls to one-eighth.  This strongly non-linear
    relationship makes slow steaming --- operating at 12--14 knots instead
    of the design speed of 18--20 knots --- financially very attractive
    when fuel prices are high.  The trade-off is that slower ships spend
    more days at sea, which may cause them to miss time windows or reduce
    the number of voyages completed per year.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{variable_speed.png}
    \caption{Left panel: total cost as a function of speed (fuel cost rises
             as $v^3$ while time-related cost falls as $1/v$; the optimum
             balances the two).  Right panel: fuel consumption rate versus
             speed, with specific values annotated at three example speeds.
             At $v=24$ knots, fuel consumption is $(24/12)^3 = 8$ times
             higher than at $v=12$ knots.}
  \end{figure}

  \medskip
  \textbf{Modelling variable speed.}  When speed can be optimised, the
  total fuel cost of a voyage from node $i$ to node $j$ for ship $k$ is:
  \begin{equation}
    C_{ijk}(v) \;=\; \alpha_k \cdot v^3 \cdot \frac{d_{ij}}{v \cdot 24}
                \;=\; \frac{\alpha_k \cdot d_{ij} \cdot v^2}{24}
  \end{equation}
  where $d_{ij}$ is the distance in nautical miles, $v$ is in knots, and
  the factor 24 converts from daily rate to per-hour rate.
  Choosing speed $v$ also determines transit time $T_{ijk}^S = d_{ij}/(v \cdot 24)$
  days, which affects time-window feasibility.

  \begin{exampleblock}{Numerical Example: Speed--Cost Trade-off on One Leg}
    Ship $k$ must sail from Rotterdam to Houston, a distance of
    $d = 7{,}800$ nautical miles.  Engine constant $\alpha_k = 0.002$
    tonnes of fuel per day per knot$^3$.  Fuel costs \$600 per tonne.

    \textbf{At 18 knots (design speed):}
    Transit time $= 7800 / (18 \times 24) = 18.1$ days.
    Fuel used $= 0.002 \times 18^3 \times 18.1 \approx 211$ tonnes.
    Fuel cost $\approx \$126{,}000$.

    \textbf{At 14 knots (slow steaming):}
    Transit time $= 7800 / (14 \times 24) = 23.2$ days.
    Fuel used $= 0.002 \times 14^3 \times 23.2 \approx 127$ tonnes.
    Fuel cost $\approx \$76{,}000$.

    \textbf{Saving $\approx \$50{,}000$ per voyage} by sailing 4 knots slower,
    at the cost of 5.1 extra days at sea.  If the time window permits, this
    is almost always worth doing.
  \end{exampleblock}

  The variable-speed model (13.16)--(13.18) replaces the fixed cost
  $C_{ijk}$ in the objective with a quadratic term in $v$.  The resulting
  formulation is a \textbf{Mixed-Integer Non-Linear Programme (MINLP)}.
  Fagerholt et al.\ (2010) propose a network-flow heuristic that decomposes
  the MINLP into a sequence of linear sub-problems, finding near-optimal
  speed profiles for fleets of 15--20 ships in minutes.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Solution Methods: Exact Approaches}
  The MPDP is NP-hard (it generalises the TSP), so exact algorithms can
  only solve instances of moderate size.  Two main exact approaches appear
  in the literature.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Branch-and-Bound (B\&B)}
    The standard MIP formulation (13.1)--(13.15) can be solved directly by
    an off-the-shelf MIP solver (CPLEX, Gurobi) using \textbf{branch-and-bound}.
    The solver relaxes integrality constraints on $x_{ijk}$, solves the LP
    relaxation, then \textbf{branches} on a fractional variable (fixing it to
    0 or 1 in two sub-problems) and recursively solves each.  Valid inequalities
    (cuts) tighten the LP bound.

    For ship routing, direct B\&B solves instances with up to about
    30 cargoes and 5--6 ships within one hour on modern hardware.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Branch-and-Price (B\&P) with Column Generation}
    A more powerful exact method is \textbf{branch-and-price}, which combines
    branch-and-bound with \textbf{column generation}.

    The key insight is that instead of working with individual arc variables
    $x_{ijk}$, the master problem works with \emph{complete ship routes}
    (sequences of cargo pickups and deliveries) as columns.  The number of
    possible columns is exponentially large, but column generation finds only
    the ``useful'' ones.

    \textbf{Algorithm: Column Generation for Ship Routing}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Start with a small pool of feasible routes (columns) for each ship.
      \item \textbf{Solve the Restricted Master Problem (RMP)}: select the
            best subset of routes from the current pool.  The LP relaxation
            of the RMP yields \textbf{dual prices} $\pi_i$ (pi sub $i$) for
            each cargo coverage constraint.  These prices measure how much
            the objective would improve by covering cargo $i$ more cheaply.
      \item \textbf{Solve the Pricing Subproblem} (one per ship): find the
            route with the most negative \emph{reduced cost}:
            \[
              \bar{c}_r = c_r - \sum_{i} \pi_i a_{ir}
            \]
            where $c_r$ is the voyage cost of route $r$ and $a_{ir}$ (a sub
            $ir$) equals 1 if route $r$ serves cargo $i$.  The reduced cost
            $\bar{c}_r$ measures how much better route $r$ is than what the
            dual prices suggest.  The pricing subproblem is solved as a
            \textbf{shortest-path problem with time windows and capacity}.
      \item If a route with $\bar{c}_r < 0$ is found (it improves the objective),
            add it to the master problem as a new column.  Otherwise, the
            LP relaxation is optimal.
      \item Branch on a fractional $\lambda_r$ variable (the fraction of time
            route $r$ is used) to enforce integrality.
    \end{enumerate}

    B\&P solves instances with 40--80 cargoes because it always works with
    a polynomial-sized master problem.
  \end{block}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{column_generation.png}
    \caption{Left: the set-partitioning matrix where rows are cargoes and
             columns are candidate ship routes; a ``1'' entry means that
             route covers that cargo.  Right: the column generation loop
             --- the Restricted Master Problem is solved for duals, then
             the pricing subproblem finds the best new column to add.}
  \end{figure}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{branch_price.png}
    \caption{Branch-and-price schematic.  Column generation runs at every
             node of the branch-and-bound tree.  At the root, the LP relaxation
             is solved via column generation.  When a fractional solution is
             found, two branches are created (fixing $x_{ijk}=0$ or $x_{ijk}=1$).
             This combination is far more efficient than standard B\&B for
             ship routing instances.}
  \end{figure}

  \begin{alertblock}{Why Column Generation Works for Ship Routing}
    The number of feasible ship routes grows factorially with the number of
    cargoes.  Column generation sidesteps this by asking: ``Given the current
    LP prices, which route would most improve the objective?''  The pricing
    subproblem --- finding the cheapest-cost route for one ship subject to
    time windows and capacity --- is a \emph{single-commodity shortest-path
    problem}, which is tractable.  The master problem only ever needs to
    see a small fraction of all possible routes.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Solution Methods: Heuristic Approaches}
  For large real-world instances (more than 50 cargoes, more than 10 ships),
  exact methods are too slow.  The chapter describes several powerful
  heuristics.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Tabu Search (TS)}
    Tabu search is a \textbf{local search metaheuristic} that systematically
    explores the neighbourhood of the current solution, accepting improving
    moves but also temporarily allowing ``uphill'' moves to escape local optima.

    The name ``tabu'' comes from the Polynesian word for ``forbidden.''
    A \textbf{tabu list} records recently visited moves.  A move is forbidden
    if it reverses a recent change, preventing the algorithm from cycling.

    In ship routing, a \textbf{neighbourhood move} is one of:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Reinsertion}: remove cargo $i$ from ship $k$'s route and
            insert it into ship $k'$'s route (or into the spot market).
      \item \textbf{Swap}: exchange cargo $i$ on ship $k$ with cargo $j$ on
            ship $k'$.
      \item \textbf{2-opt within a ship}: reverse a sub-sequence of port
            visits on the same ship's route.
    \end{itemize}
    Christiansen, Fagerholt, and Ronen (2004) apply TS to a real-world tramp
    problem and achieve solutions within 2--5\% of the optimum for instances
    that exact methods cannot solve.
  \end{block}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.58\textwidth]{tabu_convergence.png}
    \caption{Typical tabu search convergence.  The blue line is the current
             solution value (can move uphill, i.e., worsen temporarily); the
             red line tracks the best solution found so far.  The algorithm
             escapes several local optima before finding the final best.}
  \end{figure}

  \begin{block}{Adaptive Large Neighbourhood Search (ALNS)}
    ALNS extends tabu search by using multiple \textbf{destroy-and-repair}
    operators, selecting the most effective one adaptively based on recent
    performance history.

    The \textbf{destroy phase} removes a subset of cargoes from their current
    ship assignments using operators such as: random removal, worst-cargo
    removal (remove the cargo contributing least profit), or route-based
    removal (remove all cargoes on a selected ship).

    The \textbf{repair phase} reinserts removed cargoes using a greedy or
    regret-based heuristic.  The \textbf{regret-$k$} heuristic inserts the
    cargo for which the difference between the best and second-best insertion
    position is largest (high regret = urgency to insert now).

    Operators are weighted by recent success; the weight of operator $o$ is
    updated as: $w_o \leftarrow (1-\rho)\, w_o + \rho \, \sigma_o$, where
    $\rho$ (rho) is a learning rate and $\sigma_o$ is the score earned
    (high score if the operator found a new best solution).

    ALNS has been shown to outperform single-neighbourhood TS on the MPDP,
    especially for instances mixing mandatory and optional cargoes.
  \end{block}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{benchmark_comparison.png}
    \caption{Illustrative comparison of solution quality across methods for
             tramp ship routing.  The horizontal axis shows the average
             percentage gap from the best known optimal solution (lower is
             better).  Exact branch-and-price achieves 0\% gap but is only
             feasible for small instances.  ALNS achieves a small gap with
             much shorter computation time.}
  \end{figure}
\end{frame}

\section{Maritime Inventory Routing}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Section 13.3: Maritime Inventory Routing (MIR)}
  Maritime Inventory Routing (MIR) integrates \textbf{inventory management
  at ports} with \textbf{ship scheduling}.  Instead of treating cargoes as
  discrete units (as in MPDP), MIR models the supply chain as a continuous
  flow: production ports generate stock; consumption ports draw down stock;
  ships shuttle material between them.  The operator must ensure no port
  \emph{runs out of stock} (``stock-out'') or \emph{overflows} its storage
  tanks --- while minimising total shipping cost.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Where MIR Arises in Practice}
    MIR is the operational planning model for:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Oil companies managing fleets of product tankers between refineries
            and regional fuel-distribution terminals.
      \item LNG (liquefied natural gas) producers shipping from export
            terminals to regasification plants worldwide.
      \item Chemical companies transporting feedstocks and intermediates
            between offshore and onshore plants.
    \end{itemize}
    In all cases, the quantity loaded at each port visit is a
    \emph{decision variable}, and the timing of visits must be coordinated
    with the inventory dynamics at each port.
  \end{block}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{mirp_supply_chain.png}
    \caption{Maritime Inventory Routing integrates three supply-chain layers:
             production ports (left, green), the shipping fleet (centre, blue),
             and consumption ports (right, red).  An inventory feedback loop
             (orange arrow) drives the system: ships visit production ports
             when stock is high and consumption ports when stock is low.}
  \end{figure}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{13.3.1 MIR Problem Description and Example}
  \textbf{Ports and inventory dynamics.}  Let $N$ denote the set of ports.
  Each port $i \in N$ is either a \textbf{production port}
  ($I_i = +1$: stock accumulates between ship visits, as oil wells continue
  pumping into tanks) or a \textbf{consumption port}
  ($I_i = -1$: stock falls between visits, as the plant draws material from
  tanks).

  \medskip
  The inventory $s_{im}$ (s sub $i$-$m$) at port $i$ at the time of visit $m$
  satisfies a simple balance equation.  Between consecutive visits at times
  $t_{i,m-1}$ and $t_{im}$, the inventory changes at rate $R_i$ (production
  or consumption rate, in tonnes per day).  When a ship visits, a quantity
  $q_{imk}$ is loaded or unloaded.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Inventory Level Intuition}
    At a \textbf{production port}, inventory \emph{rises} steadily between
    ship visits (oil is pumped from wells into storage tanks), then
    \emph{drops sharply} when a ship arrives and loads.  If visits are too
    infrequent, the tanks overflow.  If visits are too frequent, ships
    sail partially empty.

    At a \textbf{consumption port}, inventory \emph{falls} steadily between
    visits (material is consumed in the plant), then \emph{rises} when a
    ship delivers more.  If visits are too infrequent, the port runs out
    of material (stock-out) and production halts --- a very costly event.
  \end{block}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{inventory_levels.png}
    \caption{Top panel: inventory at a production port.  The level rises at
             rate $R_4$ between ship visits (green dashed lines) and drops
             at each visit.  The red dashed line is the tank maximum
             $\overline{S}_4 = 32$; the orange dashed line is the minimum
             $\underline{S}_4 = 1$.  Bottom panel: inventory at a consumption
             port.  A feasible 3-visit schedule (green, solid) keeps inventory
             within $[5, 25]$; a 2-visit schedule (red, dashed) causes the
             inventory to fall below the minimum (infeasibility zone shaded).}
  \end{figure}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{mir_routes.png}
    \caption{Example MIR ship routes and schedules.  The horizontal axis is
             time (days); the vertical axis shows which port the ship is at.
             Horizontal segments represent time spent loading/unloading at a
             port; diagonal segments represent sailing legs.  Ship~1 (blue)
             and Ship~2 (purple) interleave their visits so that each port's
             inventory stays within its bounds throughout the 40-day horizon.}
  \end{figure}

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: Feasibility Check for a Single Port}
    Port 4 is a production port with:
    $R_4 = 0.8$ tonnes/day, $\underline{S}_4 = 1$, $\overline{S}_4 = 32$,
    initial inventory $S_4^0 = 10$.

    A ship visits on days 10, 22, and 34, loading 8 tonnes each visit.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Day 0--10}: inventory rises from 10 at rate 0.8: $10 + 0.8 \times 10 = 18$.
    Then ship loads 8 tonnes: inventory drops to $18 - 8 = 10$.

    \textbf{Day 10--22}: rises from 10: $10 + 0.8 \times 12 = 19.6$.
    Ship loads 8: drops to $19.6 - 8 = 11.6$.

    \textbf{Day 22--34}: rises from 11.6: $11.6 + 0.8 \times 12 = 21.2$.
    Ship loads 8: drops to $21.2 - 8 = 13.2$.

    Throughout, inventory stays in $[1, 32]$ --- the schedule is feasible.
    If the ship only visited twice (days 10 and 34), inventory would reach
    $10 + 0.8 \times 34 = 37.2 > 32$ --- infeasible (overflow).
  \end{exampleblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{13.3.2 MIR Mathematical Model}
  The full MIR model extends the MPDP formulation with additional variables
  and constraints that capture inventory dynamics.  The index $m$ tracks
  the \emph{sequence} of visits to each port during the planning horizon.

  \medskip
  \textbf{Additional sets and variables:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $M_i$ --- the ordered set of visit slots to port $i$
          ($m = 1, 2, \ldots, |M_i|$).
    \item $M_{ik}$ --- the subset of visits to port $i$ that ship $k$ could perform.
    \item $x_{imjnk} \in \{0,1\}$ --- equals 1 if ship $k$ travels from
          port-visit $(i,m)$ directly to port-visit $(j,n)$.  This is the
          arc-flow variable in the \emph{time-space} formulation.
    \item $w_{im} \in \{0,1\}$ --- equals 1 if visit slot $m$ at port $i$
          is \textbf{skipped} (no ship visits during that slot).
    \item $q_{imk} \geq 0$ --- quantity loaded (production port) or unloaded
          (consumption port) by ship $k$ at port $i$ visit $m$.
    \item $s_{im} \geq 0$ --- inventory level at port $i$ at visit time $m$.
    \item $t_{im}$ --- time (days from start) of the $m$-th visit to port $i$.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{block}{Coverage (13.41)}
    \begin{equation}
      \sum_{k \in K} \sum_{j \in N_k} \sum_{n \in M_{jk}} x_{imjnk} + w_{im} = 1
      \qquad \forall\, i \in N,\, m \in M_i
      \tag{13.41}
    \end{equation}
    Every visit slot is either served by exactly one ship or is skipped.
    The left sum counts how many ships visit port $i$ at slot $m$; adding
    the skip indicator $w_{im}$ brings the total to exactly 1.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Inventory Balance (13.52--13.53)}
    \begin{align}
      s_{i1} - R_i t_{i1} &= S_i^0
      \qquad \forall\, i \in N
      \tag{13.52} \\
      s_{i,m-1} - \sum_{k \in K} I_i q_{i,m-1,k}
      + R_i (t_{im} - t_{i,m-1}) - s_{im} &= 0
      \qquad m \geq 2
      \tag{13.53}
    \end{align}
    Constraint (13.52) initialises the inventory: at the first visit
    (time $t_{i1}$), the inventory $s_{i1}$ equals the initial level $S_i^0$
    plus the production/consumption since time 0.  Constraint (13.53) is the
    \textbf{inventory balance}: the inventory at visit $m$ equals the inventory
    at visit $m{-}1$, adjusted for any loading/unloading ($I_i q_{i,m-1,k}$,
    where $I_i = +1$ if production port and $-1$ if consumption port),
    plus the natural accumulation/depletion over the inter-visit time
    $(t_{im} - t_{i,m-1})$ at rate $R_i$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Inventory Balance}
    Think of the inventory as a bank account.  At a production port: money
    (inventory) accumulates at a steady rate $R_i$ (interest), and ships
    ``withdraw'' (load) $q_{imk}$ at each visit.  The balance equation simply
    says: ending balance = starting balance + accrual - withdrawal.  At a
    consumption port, the rate $R_i$ is a debit (withdrawal); ship visits
    are credits (deliveries).  The constraint enforces that this bank
    statement is internally consistent.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{block}{Inventory Bounds (13.54--13.55)}
    \begin{align}
      \underline{S}_i \leq s_{im} &\leq \overline{S}_i
      \qquad \forall\, i \in N,\, m \in M_i
      \tag{13.54} \\
      \underline{S}_i \leq s_{im} - \sum_{k \in K} I_i q_{im|M_i|k}
      + R_i(T - t_{im}) &\leq \overline{S}_i
      \qquad m = |M_i|
      \tag{13.55}
    \end{align}
    Constraint (13.54) enforces $[\underline{S}_i, \overline{S}_i]$ at each
    visit.  Constraint (13.55) additionally checks the \emph{end-of-horizon}
    inventory --- after the last visit at time $t_{i|M_i|}$, inventory must
    still be within bounds at the horizon end $T$.  This prevents the plan
    from scheduling a large delivery just before the horizon and then leaving
    the port in an infeasible state afterwards.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Load Feasibility (13.46--13.49)}
    Analogous to the MPDP constraints (13.7)--(13.9), the MIR model includes
    constraints ensuring the load $l_{imk}$ on ship $k$ (after visiting
    port $i$ at slot $m$) stays within capacity $[0, Q_k^C]$:
    \begin{align}
      l_{imk} + I_j q_{jnk} - l_{jnk} - Q_k^C(1 - x_{imjnk}) &\leq 0
      \tag{13.46} \\
      l_{imk} + I_j q_{jnk} - l_{jnk} + Q_k^C(1 - x_{imjnk}) &\geq 0
      \tag{13.47} \\
      q_{imk} \leq l_{imk} &\leq \sum_{j,n} Q_k^C x_{imjnk}
      \tag{13.48}
    \end{align}
    These constraints ``link'' the load on the ship to the quantity loaded
    or unloaded at each port visit, enforcing consistency between the ship's
    physical state and the inventory at each port.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{MIR Solution Methods}
  The MIR model (13.40)--(13.59) is a large MIP.  For a problem with 4 ports,
  5 ships, and a 40-day horizon, decision variables can exceed 100{,}000.
  Several solution approaches have been developed.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Arc-Flow vs.\ Path-Flow Formulations}
    The model described above is an \textbf{arc-flow} formulation: binary
    variables $x_{imjnk}$ for individual arcs between port-visit slots.
    An alternative is the \textbf{path-flow} formulation, which uses variables
    $\lambda_r \geq 0$ representing the usage of complete ship \emph{schedules}
    (full sequences of port visits for one ship over the entire horizon).
    Path-flow formulations have tighter LP relaxations and suit column
    generation well, but the pricing subproblem (finding the best ship schedule)
    is itself a complex shortest-path problem with inventory side constraints.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Heuristics for MIR}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Decomposition}: first determine how many times each port
            needs to be visited (a single-port inventory problem), then solve
            a smaller routing problem.
      \item \textbf{Rolling horizon}: solve MIR over a short window (e.g., 2
            weeks), fix the first week's decisions, and roll forward.  This
            handles instances that are too large for a single solve.
      \item \textbf{Column generation}: generate ship schedules (columns) that
            cover feasible sequences of port visits, with inventory implications
            computed implicitly within the pricing subproblem.
      \item \textbf{Approximate Dynamic Programming (ADP)}: for long-horizon
            MIR, Papageorgiou et al.\ (2014/2015) develop an ADP approach
            that approximates the value function using basis functions,
            enabling very long horizons (months to years) with multiple ships.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: MIR Captures Full Supply-Chain Cost}
    Traditional ship routing models minimise shipping cost alone.  MIR
    additionally accounts for: the cost of \emph{holding inventory} (capital
    tied up in stock), the cost of \emph{stock-outs} (lost production when
    material runs out), and the cost of \emph{storage overflow} (spillage or
    forced venting of gases).  Studies show MIR solutions can reduce total
    supply-chain cost by 5--15\% compared with separately optimising
    routing and inventory.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.60\textwidth]{supply_chain.png}
    \caption{The MIR optimizer integrates three traditionally separate
             decisions: production scheduling at supply ports (blue),
             fleet management (orange), and inventory control at demand
             ports (green).  The integrated approach avoids the suboptimality
             that arises when each layer optimises independently.}
  \end{figure}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{MIR Extensions: Multiple Products and Varying Quantities}
  The MIR setting opens several practically important extensions.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Multiple Products}
    When ships carry multiple products in separate compartments (as in a
    chemical tanker with several segregated tanks), the model must track
    inventory separately for each product.  Siswanto, Essam, and Sarker (2011)
    develop a MIP model and metaheuristic for this \textbf{multi-compartment MIR}.
    Key additional constraints specify: which product goes in which compartment,
    cleaning time between products, and stowage restrictions.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Varying Production and Consumption Rates}
    In reality, production rates at oil fields vary with geological depletion,
    and consumption rates at power plants vary with demand.  A robust MIR
    model treats rates $R_i(t)$ as time-varying parameters.  This turns the
    inventory balance into a time-dependent difference equation and typically
    requires discretising the planning horizon into time periods.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Integration with Production Scheduling}
    At the most integrated level, the MIR model is embedded within a
    \textbf{production-routing} model where the production schedule at
    each plant is also a decision variable.  For example, a refinery might
    choose \emph{when} to produce which products based on ship schedules.
    This integration further reduces total cost but also greatly increases
    model complexity.  Ronen (2002) and Dauzere-Peres et al.\ study this
    integration in the context of petroleum distribution.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Takeaway: MIR is the Richest VRP Variant in This Chapter}
    MIR simultaneously decides: which ships serve which ports, in what order,
    at what times, loading how much.  All these decisions interact through
    the inventory balance equation.  The result is a combinatorially explosive
    problem that requires dedicated formulations and algorithms to solve at
    industry scale.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section{Dynamic and Stochastic Ship Routing}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Section 13.4: Dynamic and Stochastic Ship Routing}
  All models described so far assume that all information is
  \textbf{known in advance}.  In reality, ship routing is subject to
  many sources of uncertainty:

  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Weather}: storms can delay a ship by several days on ocean
          routes; fuel consumption increases sharply in heavy seas.
    \item \textbf{Port congestion and closure}: berths can be unexpectedly
          unavailable due to maintenance, strikes, or tidal restrictions.
    \item \textbf{Spot market dynamics}: new cargo offers arrive continuously
          in real time, requiring immediate re-routing decisions.
    \item \textbf{Demand uncertainty}: in MIR settings, consumption rates at
          ports fluctuate with seasons, economic activity, or equipment failures.
  \end{itemize}

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Two Paradigms for Uncertainty}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Dynamic (online) routing}: the plan is re-optimised each
            time new information arrives (event-driven re-planning, also called
            a ``rolling horizon'' or ``model predictive control'' approach).
      \item \textbf{Stochastic programming}: uncertainty is modelled
            \emph{explicitly} via probability distributions and scenario trees.
            The optimiser finds first-stage decisions that perform well across
            all realisations of the uncertainty.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{13.4.1 Dynamic Routing: Rolling Horizon Re-planning}
  The most widely used practical approach to dynamic routing is the
  \textbf{rolling horizon} framework.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Rolling Horizon Algorithm}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item At decision time $t$, gather all currently known information:
            positions of ships, confirmed cargoes, weather forecasts,
            port status, and pending spot cargo offers.
      \item Solve the static MPDP or MIR over a planning horizon $[t, t+H]$
            (horizon length $H$, typically 2--4 weeks).
      \item \textbf{Execute only the first $\delta$ time units} of the
            resulting plan.  These decisions are \emph{committed} (binding).
      \item At time $t + \delta$, receive new information (new cargo offers,
            updated weather, port closures), and re-solve.
      \item Repeat until the end of the planning period.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  The key design choices are:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Horizon length $H$}: too short leads to myopic decisions
          that miss profitable future cargo; too long is expensive to re-solve
          and the far future is very uncertain.
    \item \textbf{Commitment horizon $\delta$}: how far ahead the plan is
          committed before the next re-optimisation.  Smaller $\delta$ means
          more frequent re-optimisation and better responsiveness, but higher
          computational cost.
    \item \textbf{End-of-horizon value function}: some approaches add a term
          to the objective representing the \emph{expected value of the
          fleet's position} at the end of the horizon, preventing
          artificially good decisions that leave ships in bad positions.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: Rolling Horizon in Action}
    We manage 3 ships, horizon $H = 30$ days, commitment $\delta = 7$ days.

    \textbf{Day 0}: solve MPDP for days 0--30.  The plan assigns:
    Ship~1 to Cargo~A (depart day 2, arrive day 9),
    Ship~2 to Cargo~B (depart day 1, arrive day 8),
    Ship~3 on standby for optional spot cargoes.

    \textbf{Day 7}: during the first 7 days, two events occurred:
    (a) a new spot cargo~C appeared (worth \$200k; pickup day 10, delivery
    day 20); (b) Cargo~B's destination port is closed until day 12.
    We re-solve the MPDP for days 7--37 incorporating these updates.
    New plan: Ship~2 rerouted to an alternative port; Ship~3 assigned
    to Cargo~C's pickup.

    The first 7 days of the new plan are now binding.

    \textbf{Key insight}: rolling horizon handles disruptions naturally ---
    the planner simply re-solves with updated data at each period.
    The quality of the solution degrades gracefully as disruptions
    become more frequent or severe.
  \end{exampleblock}

  Tirado et al.\ (2013) apply a rolling horizon heuristic based on ALNS
  to a dynamic industrial shipping problem with uncertain spot-cargo arrivals.
  Their method achieves solutions within 3--8\% of a perfect-information
  (clairvoyant) benchmark even under high cargo-uncertainty levels.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{13.4.2 Stochastic Programming for Ship Routing}
  A more principled treatment of uncertainty uses \textbf{stochastic
  programming}, which models the future explicitly as a probability
  distribution over \emph{scenarios}.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Two-Stage Stochastic Programme}
    Let $\Omega$ (Omega, the set of all possible future scenarios) and
    $p_\omega$ (p sub $\omega$ (omega), the probability of scenario
    $\omega \in \Omega$).  The two-stage stochastic formulation is:
    \begin{equation}
      \max_{x \in X} \; c^\top x + \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} p_\omega \, Q(x, \omega)
    \end{equation}
    where:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $x$ is the \textbf{first-stage} (``here-and-now'') decision made
            \emph{before} uncertainty is revealed (e.g., which mandatory
            cargoes to commit to, how to pre-position the fleet).
      \item $X$ is the feasible set of first-stage decisions.
      \item $c^\top x$ is the first-stage revenue/cost.
      \item $Q(x, \omega)$ is the \textbf{second-stage} optimal value
            given first-stage decisions $x$ and realised scenario $\omega$
            (e.g., the optimal routing adjustment after seeing actual weather).
      \item $\sum_\omega p_\omega Q(x, \omega)$ is the
            \textbf{expected recourse value}: the average second-stage payoff
            across all scenarios.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Stochastic Programme}
    The formula says: choose first-stage decisions now to maximise the sum
    of immediate payoff and expected future payoff.  This is like deciding
    today whether to book a cargo contract (binding) knowing that tomorrow's
    weather may force an expensive reroute.  The stochastic programme
    balances the value of the contract against the expected rerouting cost
    across all weather scenarios, weighted by their probabilities.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{stochastic_two_stage.png}
    \caption{Two-stage stochastic programming decision tree.  Stage~1
             decisions are made at the root (before uncertainty is resolved).
             Three scenarios then branch out, each with a probability.
             Stage~2 (recourse) adapts the plan to each realised scenario.
             The objective maximises the probability-weighted average across
             all branches.}
  \end{figure}

  \begin{block}{Value of the Stochastic Solution (VSS)}
    A key performance metric is the \textbf{Value of the Stochastic Solution
    (VSS)}, defined as:
    \begin{equation}
      \mathrm{VSS} = z^{SP} - z^{EV}
    \end{equation}
    where $z^{SP}$ is the objective value of the stochastic programme and
    $z^{EV}$ is the objective value of the \textbf{expected-value (EV)}
    solution --- the solution obtained by replacing all random quantities
    with their expected values and solving a deterministic problem.

    If $\mathrm{VSS} > 0$, the stochastic model provides real benefit over
    the deterministic approximation.  For ship routing problems, VSS is
    typically 5--15\% of the objective, meaning explicitly modelling
    uncertainty saves 5--15\% of costs.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Computational Challenge and Decomposition}
    The stochastic programme grows linearly with $|\Omega|$ (number of
    scenarios).  For hundreds of scenarios, special techniques are needed:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Scenario reduction}: cluster scenarios into $K$ representative
            ones (using distance-based clustering) that approximate the
            full distribution well.
      \item \textbf{Sample Average Approximation (SAA)}: sample $N$ scenarios
            from the distribution and solve the resulting deterministic problem.
            Average across multiple SAA solutions to estimate the true optimum.
      \item \textbf{Progressive hedging}: a Lagrangian decomposition that
            solves one sub-problem per scenario, iteratively adjusting
            multipliers until all sub-problems agree on the first-stage
            decisions.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{13.4.2 Applications: Weather Routing and Port Disruptions}
  \begin{block}{Weather Routing}
    Ocean weather strongly affects transit time and fuel consumption.
    A ship in a force-8 storm may be delayed by 1--3 days and consume
    30--50\% more fuel than in calm conditions.

    Weather routing models typically:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Obtain a probabilistic weather forecast (wind field, wave height,
            ocean current) over the planning horizon.
      \item Model ship speed and fuel consumption as functions of wave height
            and wave angle (using \emph{polar diagrams} from the ship's
            technical specification).
      \item Optimise the route (sequence of waypoints) to balance transit
            time, fuel cost, and risk of delay.
    \end{enumerate}
    Lo and Liu (2007) model weather routing as a stochastic dynamic
    programme on a discretised ocean grid.  The optimal route may
    intentionally \emph{deviate} from the great-circle (shortest-distance)
    path to avoid a storm, saving more in fuel and delay than the extra
    distance costs.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Stochastic Port Availability}
    Port congestion and berth availability are inherently stochastic.
    Modelling port availability as a random variable
    $\xi_{ik} \in \{0, 1\}$ (xi, pronounced ``ksee'', sub $i$-$k$,
    where 1 means the berth is available when ship $k$ arrives at port $i$)
    leads to a \textbf{chance-constrained programme}:
    \begin{equation}
      \Pr\!\bigl(t_{ik} \in [\underline{T}_{ik}, \overline{T}_{ik}]
      \text{ and berth is available}\bigr) \geq 1 - \varepsilon
    \end{equation}
    where $\varepsilon$ (epsilon) is the \emph{acceptable risk} of a
    time-window violation (e.g., $\varepsilon = 0.05$ for a 5\% risk).
    This enforces \emph{probabilistic} time-window compliance rather
    than hard deterministic limits, allowing the model to trade off
    schedule tightness against robustness to delays.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: When to Use Stochastic vs.\ Dynamic Models}
    Use \textbf{stochastic programming} when: uncertainty can be quantified
    via probability distributions, the planning cycle is long enough to
    justify the extra computation, and first-stage decisions must be robust
    (e.g., long-term contracts).
    Use \textbf{dynamic rolling-horizon re-planning} when: uncertainty is
    revealed sequentially in real time, re-computation can be done quickly
    (minutes to hours), and there is flexibility to change the plan as
    new information arrives.  In practice, both approaches are often combined:
    a stochastic model is used for strategic decisions and a rolling horizon
    for daily tactical adjustments.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

            framework.  The integrated approach reduces total supply-chain
            cost by 5--15\% compared with decoupled routing and inventory
            optimisation.
      \item \textbf{Dynamic and Stochastic Routing}: handling weather,
            port congestion, and real-time cargo offers via rolling-horizon
            re-optimisation and two-stage stochastic programming.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{What Makes Maritime Routing Distinctive}
    Compared with classical road VRP, maritime ship routing presents unique
    challenges:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Variable speed}: the cubic fuel--speed law creates a
            continuous optimisation dimension absent in road VRP.
      \item \textbf{Enormous cargo quantities}: a single voyage decision
            can move 100{,}000+ tonnes and generate millions in revenue,
            making every percentage-point of optimality improvement valuable.
      \item \textbf{Long planning horizons}: weeks to months require
            time-expanded graph models and rolling-horizon methods.
      \item \textbf{Supply-chain integration via MIR}: no direct road-VRP
            analogue combines production, shipping, and inventory control.
      \item \textbf{Environmental impact}: ships produce approximately 2--3\%
            of global CO$_2$; route and speed optimisation directly reduces
            the carbon footprint of world trade.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

import math

def haversine_nm(lat1, lon1, lat2, lon2):
    """Great-circle distance in nautical miles between two (lat, lon) points."""
    R = 3440.065  # Earth radius in nm
    phi1, phi2 = math.radians(lat1), math.radians(lat2)
    dphi = math.radians(lat2 - lat1)
    dlam = math.radians(lon2 - lon1)
    a = (math.sin(dphi/2)**2
         + math.cos(phi1) * math.cos(phi2) * math.sin(dlam/2)**2)
    return 2 * R * math.asin(math.sqrt(a))

def fuel_cost_usd(distance_nm, speed_kn=14.0,
                  alpha=0.002, fuel_price_per_tonne=600):
    """
    Total fuel cost (USD) for one leg.
    F(v) = alpha * v^3  (tonnes/day); voyage time = distance_nm / (speed_kn*24) days.
    """
    days = distance_nm / (speed_kn * 24)   # sailing time in days
    tonnes = alpha * (speed_kn ** 3) * days  # total fuel consumed
    return tonnes * fuel_price_per_tonne

class Cargo:

        self.pickup = pickup      # (lat, lon)
        self.delivery = delivery  # (lat, lon)
        self.qty = qty            # tonnes
        self.rev = rev_per_tonne  # USD per tonne
        self.mandatory = mandatory
        # spot market cost: default 1.5x revenue if not given
        self.spot_cost = (spot_cost_per_tonne or rev_per_tonne * 1.5) * qty

def greedy_insertion(ships, cargoes):
    """
    Assign cargoes to ships greedily: at each step, choose the (cargo, ship)
    pair that maximises marginal profit (revenue - extra voyage cost).
    Mandatory cargoes are prioritised; unservable ones go to the spot market.
    """
    assignments = {s.kid: [] for s in ships}
    spot_market  = []

    # Sort: mandatory cargoes first; within mandatory, highest revenue first
    sorted_cargoes = sorted(
        cargoes,
        key=lambda c: (-int(c.mandatory), -c.rev * c.qty)
    )

    for cargo in sorted_cargoes:
        best_gain, best_ship = -math.inf, None

        for ship in ships:
            current_load = sum(c.qty for c in assignments[ship.kid])
            if current_load + cargo.qty > ship.capacity:
                continue  # would exceed capacity -- skip

            # Marginal cost = sailing to pickup + pickup to delivery
            if assignments[ship.kid]:
                last_pos = assignments[ship.kid][-1].delivery
            else:
                last_pos = ship.current_pos
            d_to_pickup   = haversine_nm(*last_pos,    *cargo.pickup)
            d_to_delivery = haversine_nm(*cargo.pickup, *cargo.delivery)
            marginal_cost = fuel_cost_usd(d_to_pickup) + fuel_cost_usd(d_to_delivery)

            gain = cargo.rev * cargo.qty - marginal_cost
            if gain > best_gain:
                best_gain, best_ship = gain, ship

        if best_ship is not None:
            assignments[best_ship.kid].append(cargo)
        else:
            spot_market.append(cargo)

    # Report
    for sid, clist in assignments.items():
        for c in clist:
            print(f"  Cargo {c.cid} -> Ship {sid}")
    for c in spot_market:
        print(f"  Cargo {c.cid} -> SPOT (cost ${c.spot_cost:,.0f})")

def simulate_inventory(port_name, rate, s0, s_min, s_max,
                       visit_times, quantities, T=40):
    """
    Simulate inventory at one port over [0, T] days.
    rate       : production (+) or consumption (-) rate in units/day
    s0         : initial inventory at t=0
    s_min, s_max : inventory bounds
    visit_times: list of days when a ship visits
    quantities : quantity loaded (positive) or unloaded (negative) at each visit
    """
    events = sorted(zip(visit_times, quantities))
    t_current = 0.0
    s_current = s0
    trajectory = [(0.0, s0)]   # (time, inventory) pairs
    violations  = []

    for (t_visit, q) in events:
        # Evolve inventory from last event to just before ship arrival
        s_before = s_current + rate * (t_visit - t_current)
        trajectory.append((t_visit, s_before))
        if not (s_min <= s_before <= s_max):
            violations.append(('before visit', t_visit, s_before))

        # Ship loads / unloads
        s_after = s_before + q
        trajectory.append((t_visit, s_after))
        if not (s_min <= s_after <= s_max):
            violations.append(('after visit', t_visit, s_after))

        t_current = t_visit
        s_current = s_after

    # Evolve to end of horizon
    s_end = s_current + rate * (T - t_current)
    trajectory.append((T, s_end))

    feasible = len(violations) == 0 and s_min <= s_end <= s_max
    print(f"Port {port_name}: {'FEASIBLE' if feasible else 'INFEASIBLE'}")
    if violations:
        for v in violations:
            print(f"  Violation {v[0]} at t={v[1]:.1f}: s={v[2]:.1f}")
    return trajectory, feasible

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Finding the cost-optimal speed on a single leg, balancing
                             cubic fuel cost against time-charter (daily) cost.}]
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

def total_leg_cost(v, distance_nm, alpha=0.002,
                   fuel_price=600, time_charter_rate=20000):
    """
    Total cost for one sailing leg at constant speed v (knots).
    Components:
      1. Fuel cost: alpha * v^3 * (transit_days) * fuel_price
      2. Time-charter (opportunity) cost: time_charter_rate * transit_days
    """
    transit_days = distance_nm / (v * 24)  # 24 hours per day
    fuel_tonnes  = alpha * (v ** 3) * transit_days
    fuel_cost    = fuel_tonnes * fuel_price
    charter_cost = time_charter_rate * transit_days  # USD/day * days
    return fuel_cost + charter_cost

def optimal_speed(distance_nm, v_min=8.0, v_max=25.0,
                  alpha=0.002, fuel_price=600, tc_rate=20000):
    """Find the cost-minimising speed on a given leg."""
    result = minimize_scalar(
        total_leg_cost,
        bounds=(v_min, v_max),
        method='bounded',
        args=(distance_nm, alpha, fuel_price, tc_rate)
    )
    v_opt = result.x
    cost_opt = result.fun
    return v_opt, cost_opt

# Example: Rotterdam to Houston, 7800 nm
dist = 7800
v_opt, c_opt = optimal_speed(dist)
print(f"Optimal speed: {v_opt:.1f} knots")
print(f"Optimal cost:  ${c_opt:,.0f}")
print(f"Transit time:  {dist/(v_opt*24):.1f} days")

# Compare at three speeds
for v in [12, v_opt, 20]:
    c = total_leg_cost(v, dist)

    print(f"  v={v:.1f} kn: cost=${c:,.0f}, time={days:.1f} days")

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Chapter Summary: Key Concepts and Takeaways}
  This chapter has presented ship routing and scheduling as a rich family
  of combinatorial optimisation problems with significant real-world impact.

  \medskip
  \begin{block}{Core Message 1: Three Shipping Modes, Three Problem Classes}
    Industrial, liner, and tramp shipping each give rise to qualitatively
    different optimisation problems.  Tramp and industrial shipping lead to
    MIP-based pickup-and-delivery models; liner shipping leads to network
    design problems.  Maritime Inventory Routing bridges shipping and
    production planning into a unified optimisation model.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Core Message 2: The MIP Model (13.1)--(13.15) is the Foundation}
    Understanding the base MPDP formulation deeply makes all extensions
    accessible.  The objective (13.1) balances revenue from cargoes against
    voyage costs and spot-market hiring costs.  Constraints (13.2)--(13.6)
    define valid routes; (13.7)--(13.10) enforce capacity; (13.11)--(13.12)
    enforce time windows and pickup-before-delivery.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Core Message 3: Column Generation is the Workhorse Solver}
    For medium-to-large instances, branch-and-price (combining B\&B with
    column generation) is the standard exact method.  It avoids exponential
    route enumeration by generating only ``useful'' ship routes at each
    step.  For very large or dynamic instances, ALNS and tabu search provide
    high-quality solutions efficiently.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Core Message 4: Uncertainty Requires Explicit Treatment}
    Weather, port disruptions, and real-time cargo offers make ship routing
    inherently dynamic.  Rolling-horizon re-optimisation is the most
    practical approach; stochastic programming provides theoretically stronger
    guarantees at higher computational cost.  The Value of the Stochastic
    Solution (VSS $\approx$ 5--15\%) quantifies the benefit of modelling
    uncertainty explicitly.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Connection to Other VRP Chapters}
    Ship routing inherits the pickup-and-delivery structure from the
    classical PDVRP literature, the column-generation methodology from
    set-partitioning models, the metaheuristic framework from ALNS/TS
    research, and the stochastic modelling from two-stage stochastic
    programming.  The maritime setting provides an especially rich application
    where all these techniques combine --- and where the economic and
    environmental stakes are highest.
  \end{alertblock}
