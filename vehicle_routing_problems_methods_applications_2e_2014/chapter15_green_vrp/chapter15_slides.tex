\section{Introduction: Environmentally Sustainable Routing}
% =============================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Why Does Routing Affect the Environment?}

  Transport networks generate substantial economic growth but simultaneously
  impose significant negative externalities on society.  The transport sector
  contributes roughly \textbf{13--14\%} of global greenhouse-gas (GHG) emissions,
  making it one of the largest single-sector contributors.
  Road freight in particular is responsible for a disproportionate share because
  heavy goods vehicles are fuel-intensive and operate on diesel, which releases
  carbon dioxide (CO$_2$), nitrogen oxides (NO$_x$), particulate matter (PM),
  and other pollutants.

  \begin{block}{The Core Question}
    Traditional Vehicle Routing Problems (VRP) seek to \emph{minimise total
    distance driven} or \emph{total fleet cost}.  Green VRP asks a different
    question: can we design routes that \emph{also} minimise fuel consumption
    and exhaust emissions --- without abandoning the economic objectives
    entirely?
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  The rationale for studying green routing is threefold:

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Regulatory pressure.}  Governments around the world have
    adopted mandatory emission targets (e.g.\ the European Union's Emissions
    Trading System, the International Road Transport Union targets).
    Carriers that exceed emission caps face financial penalties.

    \item \textbf{Fuel cost savings.}  Fuel typically constitutes 25--35\% of
    total operating costs for a logistics company.  Any reduction in fuel burn
    directly improves the bottom line.

    \item \textbf{Corporate social responsibility.}  Customers, investors, and
    regulators increasingly scrutinise the environmental footprint of supply
    chains.  Demonstrating a commitment to green operations is a competitive
    advantage.
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight}
    Minimising distance driven is \emph{not} the same as minimising fuel
    consumption.  A vehicle travelling a shorter route at high speed on a
    motorway may burn \emph{more} fuel than one taking a slightly longer but
    flatter road at a steady, moderate speed.
    Speed, load, road gradient, and driving behaviour all matter.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{External Costs of Road Transport}
    The environmental damage caused by transport activities has long been
    recognised.  The most prominent environmental costs of freight transport are:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Air pollution:} emissions of CO$_2$, NO$_x$, CO (carbon
      monoxide), SO$_x$ (sulphur oxides), and particulate matter affect human
      health and climate.
      \item \textbf{Noise:} vehicle noise causes stress and sleep disruption in
      urban populations.
      \item \textbf{Accidents:} road-freight vehicles are responsible for a
      disproportionate share of road fatalities.
    \end{itemize}
    According to international estimates, the external costs of road freight
    account for approximately \textbf{1--2\%} of GDP in developed economies.
  \end{block}

\end{frame}

\section{Fuel Consumption and Emission Models}
% =============================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Why We Need Accurate Fuel Models}

  Before we can \emph{optimise} emissions, we need to \emph{measure} them.
  This section describes the mathematical models used to estimate how much fuel
  a vehicle burns --- and hence how much CO$_2$ it emits --- as a function of
  its operating conditions.

  Two broad categories of emission models are used in the green VRP literature:

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Average-speed models:} estimate emissions as a function of
    the average speed over an entire trip or road segment.  These are
    computationally simple and are the most commonly used in routing
    optimisation.

    \item \textbf{Instantaneous (second-by-second) models:} estimate emissions
    at each moment in time, based on current engine power demand.  These are
    highly accurate but require detailed second-by-second speed profiles and are
    computationally expensive.
  \end{enumerate}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{15.2.1 Average Speed Models}
    The simplest and most widely used family of emission models estimates
    fuel consumption as a polynomial function of average speed $v$ (km/h).
    The general form proposed by the MEET project (Methodologies for Estimating
    and Reporting Emissions from Transport) for a given vehicle class is:

    \[
      F(v) \;=\; K + \frac{a}{v} + b\,v + c\,v^2 + d\,v^3 + \frac{e}{v^2}
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $F(v)$: fuel consumption in millilitres (ml) per kilometre at
      average speed $v$.
      \item $v$: vehicle speed in km/h.
      \item $K, a, b, c, d, e$: empirically calibrated coefficients that depend
      on the vehicle class (e.g.\ heavy goods vehicle over 32 tonnes vs.\ light
      van).  These coefficients are provided in look-up tables by the MEET
      project.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read This Formula}
    The formula says that fuel consumption per kilometre is \emph{not monotone}
    in speed:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item At \textbf{very low speeds} (e.g.\ stop-and-go traffic), the $a/v$
      term dominates --- idling and frequent acceleration waste fuel.
      \item At \textbf{very high speeds} (motorway), the $c\,v^2$ and $d\,v^3$
      terms dominate --- aerodynamic drag increases quadratically with speed.
      \item There is a \textbf{sweet spot} (often around 70--90 km/h for a
      heavy truck) where fuel per km is minimised.
    \end{itemize}
    This \emph{convex} (U-shaped) relationship is the foundation of
    \emph{eco-driving}: drive at the optimal speed to minimise fuel.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{fuel_vs_speed.png}
    \caption{Fuel consumption per km as a function of speed (illustrative
    MEET-style model).  The curve is convex, with a minimum around 80 km/h.}
  \end{figure}

  \framebreak

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: Computing Fuel per km}
    Suppose a heavy goods vehicle has coefficients
    $K=0$, $a=800$, $b=0.1$, $c=0.00018$, $d=0$, $e=0$.
    We compute $F(v)$ at three speeds:

    \medskip
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      Speed $v$ & Formula value & Interpretation \\
      \midrule
      $v = 40$ km/h & $800/40 + 0.1\times40 + 0.00018\times1600 = 20+4+0.29 = 24.3$ ml/km
                    & City driving \\
      $v = 80$ km/h & $800/80 + 0.1\times80 + 0.00018\times6400 = 10+8+1.15 = 19.2$ ml/km
                    & Optimal speed \\
      $v=120$ km/h  & $800/120 + 0.1\times120 + 0.00018\times14400 = 6.7+12+2.6 = 21.3$ ml/km
                    & Motorway \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}

    \medskip
    Driving at the optimal speed of 80 km/h saves \textbf{21\%} fuel compared
    to 40 km/h and \textbf{10\%} compared to 120 km/h.
  \end{exampleblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{15.2.2 Instantaneous (Second-by-Second) Models}
    Instantaneous models track the vehicle's energy demand at every second.
    The fuel flow rate $F_f(t)$ (ml/second) at time $t$ is:

    \[
      F_f(t) \;=\; \frac{P_t}{\eta_t \cdot \rho \cdot H_v}
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for Instantaneous Model}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $P_t$: engine power demand at time $t$ (watts), which depends on
      vehicle speed, acceleration, road gradient, and aerodynamic drag.
      \item $\eta_t$ (eta, pronounced ``ee-tah''): overall engine efficiency at
      time $t$ (dimensionless fraction, typically 0.25--0.45 for diesel engines).
      A higher $\eta$ means the engine converts more fuel energy into motion.
      \item $\rho$ (rho, ``roe''): fuel density in kg/litre (approximately 0.832
      for diesel).
      \item $H_v$: the lower heating value of the fuel in MJ/kg (approximately
      43.2 for diesel) --- the energy released per kilogram of fuel burned.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  The engine power demand $P_t$ is itself decomposed into four components:

  \[
    P_t \;=\; \underbrace{M a_t v_t}_{\text{inertia}} \;+\;
             \underbrace{M g C_r \cos\theta \cdot v_t}_{\text{rolling resistance}} \;+\;
             \underbrace{M g \sin\theta \cdot v_t}_{\text{grade}} \;+\;
             \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2} C_d \rho_a A v_t^3}_{\text{aerodynamic drag}}
  \]

  \begin{block}{Symbol Guide}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $M$: vehicle mass in kg (laden weight including cargo).
      \item $a_t$: acceleration at time $t$ (m/s$^2$).  Positive = speeding up.
      \item $g = 9.81$ m/s$^2$: gravitational acceleration (constant).
      \item $C_r$: rolling resistance coefficient (dimensionless, $\approx 0.01$
      for truck tyres on asphalt).
      \item $\theta$ (theta): road gradient angle in degrees.  Even a $2^\circ$
      slope increases fuel consumption substantially.
      \item $C_d$: aerodynamic drag coefficient (dimensionless, $\approx 0.7$
      for a box van).
      \item $\rho_a$: air density (kg/m$^3$, approximately 1.2 at sea level).
      \item $A$: vehicle frontal area in m$^2$ (typically 5--9 m$^2$ for HGVs).
      \item $v_t$: speed at time $t$ in m/s.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Power Formula}
    Each term represents a different physical force opposing motion:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item The \textbf{inertia term} $M a_t v_t$ is large during acceleration
      from rest --- this is why stop-and-go driving is so fuel-wasteful.
      \item The \textbf{grade term} $Mg\sin\theta \cdot v_t$ explains why
      hill climbing burns much more fuel and why routing along flatter roads
      can reduce emissions even if those roads are slightly longer.
      \item The \textbf{aerodynamic term} $\frac{1}{2}C_d\rho_a A v_t^3$
      grows as the \emph{cube} of speed --- doubling speed increases drag-related
      power by a factor of 8, explaining why motorway driving at high speed is
      very fuel-inefficient.
    \end{itemize}
    Instantaneous models are used in research when detailed driving-cycle data
    are available; average-speed models are preferred in practical optimisation
    because they require only one parameter per arc.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{The Comprehensive Modal Emission Model (CMEM)}
    A more comprehensive instantaneous model used in the green VRP literature
    is CMEM (Barth et al.).  It estimates fuel consumption on a road arc $(i,j)$
    of distance $d_{ij}$ (d sub $i$-$j$, km) at speed $v$ (km/h), carrying
    load $w$ (kg):

    \[
      F_{ij}(v, w) \;=\; \frac{d_{ij}}{v}
        \Bigl[\underbrace{k N V}_{\text{engine work}} +
              \underbrace{\frac{\gamma}{\psi} P_{ij}(v,w)}_{\text{road load}} \Bigr]
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{CMEM Notation}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $k$: engine friction factor (N/kPa, calibrated per engine type).
      \item $N$: engine speed in revolutions per second (rev/s).
      \item $V$: engine displacement in litres.
      \item $\gamma$ (gamma): a unit-conversion constant ($= 1/1000\eta_i\eta$).
      \item $\psi$ (psi): fuel-to-air mass ratio (dimensionless).
      \item $P_{ij}(v,w)$: traction power demand on arc $(i,j)$ --- the energy
      the engine must deliver per unit time to move the vehicle.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{fuel_vs_load.png}
    \caption{Fuel consumption per km is \emph{linear} in vehicle load at fixed
    speed.  A heavier vehicle requires more engine effort to maintain speed,
    so each additional tonne of cargo directly increases fuel burn.}
  \end{figure}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{fuel_surface.png}
    \caption{Joint effect of speed and load on fuel consumption.  The
    optimal speed decreases slightly as the vehicle carries more weight.}
  \end{figure}

\end{frame}

\section{Minimising Emissions in Vehicle Routing}
% =============================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{The Green Vehicle Routing Problem (GVRP)}

  Now that we can \emph{measure} fuel consumption on any arc, we can pose the
  optimisation problem: design a set of vehicle routes that minimises
  \emph{total fuel consumption} (and therefore CO$_2$ emissions) while still
  serving every customer.

  This problem is called the \textbf{Green Vehicle Routing Problem (GVRP)}.
  It extends the classical Capacitated VRP (CVRP) by replacing the
  distance-minimisation objective with a fuel-minimisation objective.

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{gvrp_concept.png}
    \caption{Green VRP: a depot dispatches vehicles on routes optimised for
    minimal fuel/emissions.  Unlike the classical VRP, shorter routes may not
    always be the greenest.}
  \end{figure}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Mathematical Model for the GVRP}
    Let $G = (V, A)$ be a complete directed graph, where:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $V = \{0\} \cup N$ is the set of nodes: node 0 is the depot and
      $N = \{1, \ldots, n\}$ is the set of $n$ customers.
      \item $A = \{(i,j) : i,j \in V,\; i \neq j\}$ is the set of arcs.
      \item $d_{ij}$ ($d$ sub $i$-$j$): distance of arc $(i,j)$ in km.
      \item $q_i$: demand of customer $i$ (units to be delivered).
      \item $Q$: vehicle capacity (maximum load the vehicle can carry).
      \item $K$: fleet of available vehicles.
    \end{itemize}
    Decision variables:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $x_{ij} \in \{0,1\}$ ($x$ sub $i$-$j$): equals 1 if some vehicle
      travels directly from node $i$ to node $j$, and 0 otherwise.
      \item $f_{ij} \geq 0$ ($f$ sub $i$-$j$): the total load (weight in kg)
      carried by the vehicle on arc $(i,j)$.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{GVRP Objective Function}
    Using the load-dependent fuel model, the total fuel consumed is:
    \[
      \text{Minimise} \quad
      \sum_{(i,j)\in A} \bigl[\alpha\, d_{ij}\, x_{ij} \;+\; \beta\, d_{ij}\, f_{ij}\bigr]
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for Objective}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\alpha$ (alpha, ``al-fah''): fuel consumed per km when the vehicle
      is \emph{empty} (kg of fuel per km).  It captures engine idling,
      aerodynamic drag, and rolling resistance independent of cargo weight.
      \item $\beta$ (beta, ``bay-tah''): additional fuel consumed per km per
      tonne of cargo (kg of fuel per km per kg of load).  It captures how each
      extra unit of weight increases fuel consumption.
      \item $\alpha\, d_{ij}\, x_{ij}$: fuel for moving the vehicle on arc
      $(i,j)$ regardless of load.
      \item $\beta\, d_{ij}\, f_{ij}$: extra fuel due to the cargo weight
      $f_{ij}$ on arc $(i,j)$.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the GVRP Objective}
    The objective has two parts:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item The \textbf{distance-based} term $\alpha\, d_{ij}\, x_{ij}$ is
      minimised by reducing total route length --- this aligns partially with the
      classical VRP.
      \item The \textbf{load-based} term $\beta\, d_{ij}\, f_{ij}$ is minimised
      by reducing the distance heavy cargo travels.  This means it can be cheaper
      (in fuel) to deliver to \emph{nearby heavy-demand customers first}, so the
      vehicle carries full load over short distances rather than long ones.
    \end{enumerate}
    This is a subtle but important difference from minimising total distance:
    the \emph{sequence} of deliveries within a route affects fuel consumption
    even when the total route length is fixed.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{GVRP Constraints}
    \begin{align}
      &\sum_{j \in V} x_{0j} = |K| && \text{exactly } |K| \text{ routes leave the depot} \\[4pt]
      &\sum_{j \in V} x_{ij} = 1 && \forall\; i \in N \quad \text{each customer visited exactly once} \\[4pt]
      &\sum_{i \in V} x_{ij} = 1 && \forall\; j \in N \quad \text{each customer departed from exactly once} \\[4pt]
      &\sum_{j \in V} f_{ji} - \sum_{j \in V} f_{ij} = q_i && \forall\; i \in N \quad \text{flow balance (deliveries)} \\[4pt]
      &q_i x_{ij} \leq f_{ij} \leq (Q - q_i) x_{ij} && \forall\; (i,j)\in A \quad \text{capacity bounds} \\[4pt]
      &x_{ij} \in \{0,1\},\; f_{ij} \geq 0 && \text{variable domains}
    \end{align}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Interpreting the Constraints}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Flow balance} (constraint 4): the load flowing \emph{into}
      customer $i$ minus the load flowing \emph{out} equals $q_i$, meaning
      exactly $q_i$ units are dropped off at $i$.  This also automatically
      prevents subtours (isolated loops not connected to the depot).
      \item \textbf{Capacity} (constraint 5): the load on arc $(i,j)$ must be
      at least $q_j$ (the remaining delivery) and at most $Q - q_i$ (what is
      left after serving $i$).  If the arc is not used ($x_{ij}=0$) the load
      must be zero.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Solution Algorithms for GVRP}
    The GVRP is NP-hard (at least as hard as the classical VRP).  Practical
    solution methods include:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Exact methods:} branch-and-price (column generation)
      for small to medium instances (up to $\sim$50 customers).
      \item \textbf{Constructive heuristics:} adapt the Clarke-Wright savings
      algorithm by replacing distance savings with \emph{fuel savings}.
      \item \textbf{Metaheuristics:} tabu search, simulated annealing, genetic
      algorithms applied to the fuel objective.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: Fuel Savings vs. Distance Savings}
    Consider a depot D and two customers A, B with demands $q_A=5$, $q_B=3$
    tonnes.  Distances: $d(D,A)=10$, $d(D,B)=8$, $d(A,B)=6$ km.
    With $\alpha=0.3$ kg/km and $\beta=0.01$ kg/km/t:

    \medskip
    \textit{Two separate routes} (D$\to$A$\to$D and D$\to$B$\to$D):
    \[
      F_{\text{sep}} = 0.3\times20 + 0.01\times10\times5 \;+\; 0.3\times16 + 0.01\times8\times3
      = 6+0.5+4.8+0.24 = 11.54\;\text{kg fuel}
    \]
    \textit{One merged route} (D$\to$A$\to$B$\to$D, A visited first):
    Vehicle carries 8t from D to A, 3t from A to B, 0t on return.
    \[
      F_{\text{merged}} = 0.3\times(10+6+8) + 0.01\times(10\times8 + 6\times3 + 8\times0)
      = 7.2 + 0.98 = 8.18\;\text{kg fuel}
    \]
    Merging saves \textbf{3.36 kg fuel (29\%)}.  The distance saving is
    $d(D,A)+d(D,B)-d(A,B)=10+8-6=12$ km --- traditional savings would also
    favour merging, but the \emph{magnitude} of the saving and the optimal
    \emph{sequence} within the route differ.
  \end{exampleblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{The Pollution Routing Problem (PRP)}

  The \textbf{Pollution Routing Problem (PRP)}, introduced by Bekt\c{a}s and
  Laporte (2011), extends the GVRP by making vehicle \emph{speed a decision
  variable} on each arc.  Rather than assuming a fixed speed everywhere, the
  PRP asks: what is the optimal speed on \emph{each road segment} to minimise
  total fuel and emissions, subject to time-window constraints?

  This is a much richer and more realistic model than the GVRP, because:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Speed directly controls the fuel-consumption rate.
    \item Time windows create a tension: slower speed saves fuel but risks
    missing delivery windows.
    \item The optimal speed depends on arc length, cargo load, and the
    slackness of the time window at the next customer.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{prp_speed_segments.png}
    \caption{In the PRP, each arc between customers has its own optimised speed.
    Longer arcs with more time-window slack can be driven at a lower, greener
    speed.}
  \end{figure}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{PRP Mathematical Formulation}
    The PRP objective function (Bekt\c{a}s and Laporte, equations 15.8--15.12)
    consists of five cost components:

    \begin{align*}
      \text{Minimise} \quad
      & \underbrace{\sum_{(i,j)\in A} c_f \bar{k} \bar{N} \bar{V} \lambda d_{ij}
        \textstyle\sum_{r=1}^{|R|} z_{ij}^r / \bar{v}^r}_{\text{(15.8) engine idle fuel}}
      \;+\;
      \underbrace{\sum_{(i,j)\in A} c_f w \gamma \lambda_{ij} d_{ij} x_{ij}}_{\text{(15.9) load-dependent fuel}} \\[6pt]
      &+\;
      \underbrace{\sum_{(i,j)\in A} c_f \gamma \alpha_{ij} d_{ij} f_{ij}}_{\text{(15.10) cargo fuel}}
      \;+\;
      \underbrace{\sum_{(i,j)\in A} c_f \beta \gamma \lambda d_{ij}
        \textstyle\sum_{r=1}^{|R|} z_{ij}^r (\bar{v}^r)^2}_{\text{(15.11) speed-squared term}} \\[6pt]
      &+\;
      \underbrace{\sum_{j\in N} c_w \sigma_j}_{\text{(15.12) driver wage}}
    \end{align*}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{PRP Notation Guide}
    The PRP involves many parameters; each is explained here.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $c_f$: fuel price (cost per litre).
      \item $\bar{k}$: engine friction factor (kJ/rev/litre).
      \item $\bar{N}$: engine speed (rev/s).
      \item $\bar{V}$: engine displacement (litres).
      \item $\lambda$: fuel-to-air mass ratio divided by heating value
      ($\phi / x_\psi$, dimensionless conversion factor).
      \item $d_{ij}$: distance of arc $(i,j)$ in km.
      \item $z_{ij}^r \in \{0,1\}$: equals 1 if the vehicle travels arc
      $(i,j)$ at speed level $r \in R = \{1,\ldots,|R|\}$.
      \item $\bar{v}^r$: the speed corresponding to speed level $r$ (km/h).
      \item $w$: vehicle curb weight (unladen mass in kg).
      \item $\gamma = 1/(1000\,\eta_t\,\eta)$: combined efficiency constant
      (where $\eta_t$ is transmission efficiency and $\eta$ is engine efficiency).
      \item $\lambda_{ij}=\phi/x_\psi$: fuel constant per arc.
      \item $\alpha_{ij} = a + g\sin\theta_{ij} + gC_r\cos\theta_{ij}$:
      vehicle-arc constant combining gradient $\theta_{ij}$ (theta, road slope)
      and rolling resistance $C_r$.
      \item $\beta = 0.5\,C_d\,\rho_a\,A$: aerodynamic constant
      ($C_d$ is drag coefficient, $\rho_a$ (rho-sub-a) is air density,
      $A$ is frontal area).
      \item $f_{ij}$: cargo load (kg) on arc $(i,j)$.
      \item $c_w$: driver wage rate (cost per unit time).
      \item $\sigma_j$ (sigma): total time spent at or en route to customer $j$.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{Physical Interpretation of the Five Cost Terms}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Engine idle fuel (15.8):} fuel consumed just to keep the
      engine running, proportional to engine friction and displacement.
      The speed appears in the denominator because at higher speed, the same
      distance is covered in less time (less idling per km).

      \item \textbf{Load-dependent fuel (15.9):} the unladen vehicle weight $w$
      must be moved regardless of cargo --- this scales with speed through
      $\lambda_{ij}$.

      \item \textbf{Cargo fuel (15.10):} every kilogram of cargo $f_{ij}$
      carried on arc $(i,j)$ costs additional fuel.  This term is the same as
      in the GVRP objective.

      \item \textbf{Speed-squared term (15.11):} aerodynamic drag increases
      with $v^2$, so driving faster costs quadratically more fuel.
      This term creates the incentive to slow down.

      \item \textbf{Driver wage (15.12):} longer travel time means higher
      driver pay.  This creates the counter-incentive to drive faster.
      The trade-off between (4) and (5) determines the optimal speed on each arc.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{PRP Constraints (selected)}
    The PRP includes the standard VRP flow constraints plus:
    \begin{align}
      &T_i - T_j + s_i + \sum_{r\in R} d_{ij} z_{ij}^r / \bar{v}^r
        \leq K_{ij}(1-x_{ij}) && \forall\; i\in V, j\in N, i\neq j \\[4pt]
      &a_i \leq T_i \leq b_i && \forall\; i\in N \\[4pt]
      &T_j + s_j - \sigma_j + \sum_{r\in R} d_{j0} z_{j0}^r / \bar{v}^r
        \leq L(1-x_{j0}) && \forall\; j\in N \\[4pt]
      &\sum_{r=1}^{|R|} z_{ij}^r = x_{ij} && \forall\; (i,j)\in A
    \end{align}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Interpreting PRP Constraints}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $T_i$ (capital T sub $i$): arrival time at customer $i$.
      \item $s_i$: service time at customer $i$ (how long the delivery takes).
      \item $[a_i, b_i]$: time window at customer $i$ --- must arrive between
      time $a_i$ (earliest) and $b_i$ (latest).
      \item $K_{ij}$: a large constant (``big-M'') that makes the timing
      constraint inactive when arc $(i,j)$ is not used ($x_{ij}=0$).
      \item The constraint $\sum_r z_{ij}^r = x_{ij}$ ensures that exactly
      one speed level is chosen on every used arc (and zero on unused arcs).
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: Optimal Speed on a Single Arc}
    Consider a single arc of length $d=100$ km with a time window at the
    destination requiring arrival between $a=1$ h and $b=2$ h.
    The driver wage is \euro5/h, fuel costs \euro1.2/litre, and the fuel
    consumption model (ignoring load) is:
    \[
      F(v) = 5/v + 0.0001 v^2 \quad \text{(litres/km)}
    \]
    Total cost as a function of speed $v$ km/h:
    \[
      C(v) = 1.2 \times d \times F(v) + 5 \times d/v
      = 120\bigl(5/v + 0.0001v^2\bigr) + 500/v
      = 1100/v + 0.012\,v^2
    \]
    Setting $dC/dv = 0$: $-1100/v^2 + 0.024v = 0 \Rightarrow v^* = (1100/0.024)^{1/3} \approx 72.4$ km/h.

    \medskip
    Check feasibility: travel time $= 100/72.4 = 1.38$ h, which lies in $[1, 2]$ --- feasible.
    If the window were tight ($b=1.2$ h), we would be forced to drive at
    $v = 100/1.2 = 83.3$ km/h even though it is above the fuel-optimal speed.
  \end{exampleblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Time-Independent VRP (TIVRP) and Time-Dependent VRP (TDVRP)}
    Two important extensions of the PRP address \textbf{traffic congestion}:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item In the \textbf{TIVRP} (time-independent), travel speed on each
      road segment is assumed constant throughout the day.
      \item In the \textbf{TDVRP} (time-dependent), the speed on segment
      $(i,j)$ at time $t$ is $v_{ij}(t)$, reflecting peak-hour congestion.
      Avoiding rush hour can reduce both travel time and emissions.
    \end{itemize}
    Ichoua, Gendreau, and Potvin showed that time-dependent travel times
    satisfy the FIFO (first-in, first-out) property when speeds are
    step-wise constant: a vehicle that departs later from a node never
    arrives earlier at the destination.  This allows dynamic programming
    to compute time-dependent shortest paths efficiently.
  \end{block}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{time_window_speed.png}
    \caption{Effect of time-window slack on speed choice.  A tight window forces
    high speed (more emissions); a loose window allows eco-driving at the
    fuel-optimal speed, with the vehicle waiting briefly at the customer if
    it arrives early.}
  \end{figure}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Variable-Speed Routing: Constant Speeds (PRP Variant)}
    A practically important simplification assigns a single speed to each arc
    (rather than a time-varying profile).  The binary linearisation of the
    speed variable replaces the continuous $v_{ij}$ with a set of speed
    levels $R = \{v^1, v^2, \ldots, v^{|R|}\}$ and binary variables
    $z_{ij}^r = 1$ if arc $(i,j)$ is driven at level $r$.

    \medskip
    The speed level formulation is a \textit{mixed-integer linear programme}
    (MILP) and can be solved by standard branch-and-bound/branch-and-cut
    solvers, whereas the continuous speed version is a non-linear mixed-integer
    programme (MINLP) requiring specialised solvers.
  \end{block}

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{speed_optimisation.png}
    \caption{Optimal speeds on a four-arc route.  Speed is lower on heavily
    loaded arcs (outbound) and higher on lighter arcs (after deliveries).
    The driver wage constraint prevents slowing down too much.}
  \end{figure}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Algorithms for the GVRP and PRP}

  Solving the GVRP and PRP in practice requires effective algorithms.
  The literature has proposed several approaches, ranging from exact
  mixed-integer programming to population-based metaheuristics.

  \begin{block}{Tabu Search for the PRP (Bektas and Laporte, 2011)}
    Tabu search (TS) is a local-search metaheuristic that escapes local optima
    by \emph{allowing moves that worsen the objective}, while maintaining a
    \textbf{tabu list} of recently visited solutions to prevent cycling.
    In the context of the PRP, TS works as follows:

    \medskip
    \textbf{Why tabu search?}  The PRP solution space is a combination of
    route structure (which customer is in which route and in what order) and
    speed choices on each arc.  Standard descent methods get trapped in
    local optima because improving the route structure often requires temporarily
    making speed choices worse.  By remembering and forbidding recently tried
    route moves (the ``tabu list''), the algorithm can escape such traps.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{PRP Tabu Search Pseudocode}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Initialise:} generate a feasible solution $s_0$ using
      a nearest-neighbour heuristic.  Set $s^* \leftarrow s_0$ (best solution),
      tabu list $T \leftarrow \emptyset$, iteration $k \leftarrow 0$.
      \item \textbf{Optimise speeds:} for the current route structure, solve the
      inner speed-optimisation problem (a convex programme in the continuous
      speeds $v_{ij}$) to find optimal speeds.  This can be done analytically
      arc by arc using calculus.
      \item \textbf{Generate neighbourhood:} apply route-restructuring moves
      --- relocate (move one customer to another route), 2-opt (reverse a
      segment), or swap (exchange two customers between routes).
      \item \textbf{Select best non-tabu move:} choose the neighbourhood move
      with the best objective value that is \emph{not} in the tabu list $T$.
      If the resulting solution is better than $s^*$, update $s^*$.
      \item \textbf{Update tabu list:} add the selected move to $T$; remove the
      oldest entry if $|T|$ exceeds the tabu tenure $\tau$ (tau, a parameter,
      typically 10--20).
      \item \textbf{Repeat} from step 2 until stopping criterion (maximum
      iterations or time limit) is met.
      \item \textbf{Return} $s^*$.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Separating Route and Speed Optimisation}
    A crucial algorithmic observation is that once the route structure is fixed,
    the speed-optimisation sub-problem can be solved \emph{exactly and efficiently}:

    For a single arc $(i,j)$ with distance $d_{ij}$, load $f_{ij}$,
    and time-window slack $\Delta_{ij}$, the optimal speed satisfies:
    \[
      v_{ij}^* \;=\; \min\!\Bigl(\bar{v}_{\max},\;
      \max\!\Bigl(\bar{v}_{\min},\;
      \Bigl(\frac{c_f \lambda(\alpha_{ij} w + f_{ij}) + c_w}{2\, c_f \beta \lambda}\Bigr)^{1/3}
      \Bigr)\Bigr)
    \]
    where $\bar{v}_{\min}$ and $\bar{v}_{\max}$ are the minimum and maximum
    allowed speeds (km/h).

    This formula is derived by setting $dC/dv = 0$ for the per-arc cost
    function and clipping to the feasible speed range.  The cube-root structure
    arises because aerodynamic drag is proportional to $v^2$ and the driver
    wage is proportional to $1/v$.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{The Non-Linear Programming Approach (Jabali et al.)}
    Jabali, Van Woensel, and de Kok (2012) present a similar approach for a
    single-vehicle route.  They model fuel consumption as:
    \[
      F_\text{total} \;=\; \sum_{(i,j)\in\text{route}} d_{ij}\bigl(p + q\,v_{ij}^2\bigr)
    \]
    where $p$ captures load-dependent consumption and $q$ captures
    aerodynamic drag.  Given a fixed route, minimising this over $v_{ij}$
    subject to time-window constraints is a \textbf{convex programme} that can
    be solved optimally by Lagrangian relaxation or KKT (Karush-Kuhn-Tucker)
    conditions.

    Their key finding: the optimal speed on an arc increases when the time
    window at the next stop is tight, and the optimal speed \emph{decreases}
    as vehicle load increases (because the load coefficient $p$ amplifies the
    time-cost).
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{The MINLP Approach of Figliozzi (2010)}
    Figliozzi models the VRP with time windows and minimises a weighted
    combination of travel time and fuel.  The formulation is:
    \[
      \text{Min}\; \sum_{(i,j)\in A} \bigl[w_1\, t_{ij} + w_2\, e_{ij}(v_{ij})\bigr] x_{ij}
    \]
    where:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $t_{ij} = d_{ij}/v_{ij}$: travel time on arc $(i,j)$.
      \item $e_{ij}(v_{ij})$: emissions on arc $(i,j)$ as a function of speed.
      \item $w_1, w_2$: weights reflecting the relative importance of time vs.
      emissions (set by the decision maker).
    \end{itemize}
    Results on 27-node benchmark instances show that optimising speed reduces
    emissions by an average of \textbf{9\%} compared to fixing speed at the
    time-window-forcing level, at the cost of slightly longer travel time.
  \end{block}

\end{frame}

\section{Speed Optimisation on Fixed Routes}
% =============================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Eco-Driving: Optimising Speed on a Given Route}

  In the previous section we simultaneously optimised \emph{which route to
  take} and \emph{at what speed}.  A simpler but practically important problem
  is: given a \textbf{fixed route} (the sequence of customers is fixed),
  find the optimal speed on each arc.

  This is called \textbf{speed optimisation} or \textbf{eco-driving optimisation}.
  It is relevant in practice because:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Many logistics companies cannot change routes easily (due to
    contractual obligations) but \emph{can} give drivers guidance on optimal
    cruising speeds.
    \item It serves as the inner sub-problem in the tabu search for the PRP.
    \item It can be extended to consider \emph{traffic-dependent speeds}.
  \end{itemize}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Formulation: Speed Optimisation on a Fixed Route}
    Given a route $r = (0, i_1, i_2, \ldots, i_m, 0)$ visiting $m$ customers,
    with arc distances $d_k$ (km) and time windows $[a_k, b_k]$ at each stop,
    choose speeds $v_k$ (km/h) to:
    \[
      \text{Minimise} \quad \sum_{k=1}^{m+1} d_k \,F(v_k, f_k)
    \]
    subject to:
    \begin{align*}
      a_k &\leq T_k \leq b_k && \text{time window at stop } k \\
      T_k &= T_{k-1} + s_{k-1} + d_{k-1}/v_{k-1} && \text{arrival time recurrence} \\
      \bar{v}_{\min} &\leq v_k \leq \bar{v}_{\max} && \text{speed bounds}
    \end{align*}
    where $f_k$ is the vehicle load on arc $k$ (decreasing by $q_{i_k}$ after
    each delivery) and $T_k$ is the arrival time at stop $k$.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Analytical Solution for the Unconstrained Case}
    When time windows are loose enough that speed constraints never bind,
    the optimal speed on arc $k$ is found by setting $\partial C / \partial v_k = 0$:
    \[
      v_k^* \;=\; \Bigl(\frac{\text{wage rate} \cdot d_k + \text{const}}
                          {2\, c_f \beta \gamma \lambda \, d_k}\Bigr)^{1/3}
    \]
    Notice that $v_k^*$ is the \textbf{same} on all arcs (when load is
    ignored), because the cubic structure means the optimal speed balances
    the time-proportional driver wage against the speed-squared aerodynamic
    drag --- and this balance is independent of distance.

    However, when \textbf{load} is included, heavier arcs (early in the route)
    have a lower optimal speed because the load term increases the effective
    cost of travelling fast (the load must also be accelerated).
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Constant-Speed Algorithms (Bekt\c{a}s and Laporte)}
    Several algorithms have been proposed for the case where speed is
    constrained to take one of a finite set of discrete levels:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Kara, Kara, and Yetis (2007):} introduced a load-dependent
      fuel model and showed it can be linearised; proposed an integer
      programming formulation solved with a branch-and-bound algorithm.
      \item \textbf{Xiao et al.\ (2012):} proposed a fuel consumption
      optimisation model for the CVRP, solved with a savings-based
      heuristic where savings are computed using the load-dependent fuel
      model rather than distance.
      \item \textbf{Demir, Bekt\c{a}s, and Laporte (2012):} proposed a large
      neighbourhood search (LNS) for the PRP, with destroy operators
      (random removal of a subset of customers) and repair operators
      (best-insertion with simultaneous speed optimisation).
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Finding: Savings from Speed Optimisation}
    Numerical experiments (Demir et al.\ 2012) on benchmark VRP instances
    with 100--200 customers show:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Speed optimisation alone (fixing routes, optimising speeds)
      reduces fuel/emissions by \textbf{10--15\%} on average compared to
      driving at a fixed default speed.
      \item Jointly optimising routes and speeds yields \textbf{18--25\%}
      emission reductions compared to the traditional distance-minimising VRP.
      \item The benefit of speed optimisation is greatest when time-window
      slack is high: tightly constrained routes leave little room for
      eco-driving.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{Multicriteria Analysis}
% =============================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Balancing Cost and Emissions: The Pareto Front}

  In practice, fleet operators must balance two conflicting objectives:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Economic cost} --- vehicle operating costs, driver wages,
    fuel expenditure, fixed vehicle costs.
    \item \textbf{Environmental impact} --- CO$_2$ emissions, NO$_x$,
    particulate matter.
  \end{enumerate}

  No single route simultaneously minimises both: a route that uses more
  vehicles (higher cost) may deliver faster and burn less fuel per unit,
  while a cost-efficient route might consolidate loads onto fewer vehicles
  and push speeds up to meet tight time windows.

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{pareto_front.png}
    \caption{The Pareto front (or efficient frontier) for cost vs.\ emissions.
    Each point on the blue curve is a solution where improving one objective
    necessarily worsens the other.  Grey points are dominated solutions
    --- wasteful in both dimensions.}
  \end{figure}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Definition: Pareto Optimality}
    A solution $s$ is \textbf{Pareto optimal} (also called non-dominated or
    efficient) if there is no other feasible solution $s'$ that is
    \emph{strictly better} in \emph{at least one} objective and \emph{at least
    as good} in \emph{all other} objectives.

    The set of all Pareto-optimal solutions is called the \textbf{Pareto front}
    (or Pareto set).  It represents the true trade-off curve between the objectives.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Multicriteria Formulation}
    A common approach is the \textbf{epsilon-constraint method}: fix the
    emission budget at $\varepsilon$ (epsilon, ``ep-sih-lon'') kg CO$_2$
    and minimise cost:
    \[
      \text{Minimise}\; C(x) \quad \text{subject to}\; E(x) \leq \varepsilon
      \;\text{ and routing constraints}
    \]
    By varying $\varepsilon$ from the minimum possible emissions to the
    unconstrained level, we trace out the entire Pareto front.

    Alternatively, the \textbf{weighted-sum method} solves:
    \[
      \text{Minimise}\; w_1\, C(x) + w_2\, E(x)
    \]
    for different weight ratios $w_1/w_2$.  This is simpler but may miss
    non-convex portions of the Pareto front.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Lopes et al.\ Multicriteria VRP}
    Lopes and Laporte (2013) present a multicriteria VRP in which the two
    objectives are the total routing cost and the total amount of CO$_2$
    emitted.  They propose a \textbf{COPRAS} (Complex Proportional Assessment)
    programme that uses a simulation-based approach combined with a
    multi-start tabu search to generate a representative set of Pareto-optimal
    solutions.

    Their numerical experiments on Belgian distribution data show that
    accepting a \textbf{10\% increase} in routing cost typically achieves a
    \textbf{20--30\% reduction} in emissions --- a highly favourable trade-off
    from a regulatory compliance perspective.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Practical Implication for Fleet Operators}
    The Pareto front allows a logistics manager to visualise the range of
    feasible policy choices:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item If the firm faces a hard emission cap (e.g.\ a carbon budget of
      50 tonnes CO$_2$ per week), they can read off the minimum-cost routing
      solution that satisfies the cap.
      \item If the firm has a cost budget but wants to go as green as possible,
      they can find the maximum emission reduction achievable within the
      cost constraint.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{emission_comparison.png}
    \caption{Relative CO$_2$ emissions across different routing approaches.
    Optimising routes for fuel rather than distance alone already saves 18\%;
    optimising both routes and speeds (PRP) achieves 29\%; switching to
    electric vehicles achieves up to 65\% reduction (assuming clean electricity).}
  \end{figure}

\end{frame}

\section{Routing in Other Modes of Transport}
% =============================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Beyond Road Transport: Rail, Air, and Multimodal}

  While most green VRP research focuses on road transport, significant
  opportunities for emission reduction also exist in \textbf{rail freight},
  \textbf{air cargo}, and \textbf{multimodal transport}.

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{modal_comparison.png}
    \caption{CO$_2$ emission intensity by transport mode.  Rail freight emits
    approximately one-third the CO$_2$ of truck freight per tonne-km.
    Air cargo emits nearly 10$\times$ as much as truck freight.}
  \end{figure}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Rail Freight Routing}
    In rail freight, the routing decision involves:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Selecting which goods travel on which rail service.
      \item Choosing between \emph{direct trains} (faster, more expensive)
      and \emph{consolidated services} (slower, cheaper and greener per
      tonne-km due to full train utilisation).
    \end{itemize}
    Rail is inherently more energy-efficient than road because:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Steel-on-steel rolling resistance is far lower than rubber-on-asphalt.
      \item Electric trains can use regenerative braking to recover energy.
      \item Large trains move many tonnes per locomotive.
    \end{enumerate}
    Routing optimisation in rail networks can further reduce emissions
    by avoiding unnecessary shunting operations and by running trains at
    optimal speeds (the same U-shaped fuel-speed relationship applies to trains,
    with an optimal speed typically around 80--100 km/h for freight).
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Air Cargo and Routing}
    Air cargo routing is a different setting: the energy cost of lifting cargo
    to altitude dominates all other costs.  Key emission drivers are:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Payload and fuel}: aviation fuel consumption scales
      approximately linearly with payload (similar to road freight).
      \item \textbf{Aircraft utilisation}: a partially full aircraft is very
      fuel-inefficient per tonne-km.  Consolidation (waiting to fill the plane)
      is the primary green lever.
      \item \textbf{Flight path and altitude}: flying at optimal altitude and
      avoiding headwinds reduces fuel burn.
      \item \textbf{Speed}: cruise speed is highly optimised by airlines
      (typically Mach 0.82--0.85 for wide-body freighters) to balance fuel
      and time costs.
    \end{itemize}
    Routing and scheduling optimisation for air cargo has been studied under the
    framework of the \textbf{Air Freight Transportation Problem}, which shares
    structural similarities with the VRP but at a continental or global scale.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Multimodal Transport}
    Many real-world logistics networks involve multiple transport modes:
    long-haul by rail or sea, last-mile delivery by road.
    Green routing in multimodal settings involves:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Choosing the mode for each leg of a journey (trade-off:
      rail is greener but slower and less flexible).
      \item Optimising the \emph{break-bulk points} (terminals or
      intermodal hubs) where cargo transfers between modes.
      \item Routing road vehicles from the terminal to final customers.
    \end{itemize}
    Research by Lam and Garg, and others, shows that modal shift
    from road to rail can reduce freight-related CO$_2$ by 50--70\% on
    long-haul corridors, making it the single biggest lever for green logistics.
  \end{block}

\end{frame}

\section{Alternative Fuel-Powered Vehicles}
% =============================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Electric Vehicle Routing: The E-VRP}

  The ultimate green routing scenario replaces fossil-fuel vehicles with
  \textbf{electric vehicles (EVs)}.  EVs produce zero tailpipe emissions,
  and if the electricity grid is powered by renewables, the entire supply
  chain becomes carbon-neutral.

  However, EVs introduce a new operational constraint: \textbf{limited range}.
  A standard electric delivery van has a range of roughly 100--200 km on a
  single charge (improving rapidly with battery technology).  If the route
  requires more than this, the vehicle must stop at a \textbf{recharging station}.

  This gives rise to the \textbf{Electric Vehicle Routing Problem (E-VRP)},
  also described as ``routing with range anxiety''.

  \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{evrp_stations.png}
    \caption{E-VRP example: the vehicle detours to recharging stations (triangles)
    mid-route when the battery level drops below a safety threshold.
    The dashed circle shows the maximum range from the depot on a single charge.}
  \end{figure}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{E-VRP Definition and Challenges}
    The E-VRP extends the classical VRP with the following modifications:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Each vehicle has a battery with capacity $Q_e$ (kWh or km equivalent).
      \item The battery is depleted by $e_{ij}$ (energy in kWh) when travelling
      arc $(i,j)$.  Energy consumption depends on distance, speed, load, and
      gradient.
      \item The battery level must remain non-negative at all times (hard
      constraint --- cannot run out of battery on the road!).
      \item The network contains a set $F$ of \emph{recharging stations} where
      the vehicle can partially or fully recharge its battery.
      \item Recharging takes time (typically 30--60 minutes for a significant
      top-up on a DC fast charger), so stations add to route duration.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{E-VRP Mathematical Formulation}
    Let $G = (V \cup F, A)$ be the extended graph including both customers
    $V$ and recharging stations $F$.  Define:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $y_i \geq 0$: battery state of charge (SoC, kWh) upon \emph{arriving}
      at node $i$.
      \item $e_{ij}$: energy consumed on arc $(i,j)$ (kWh).
      \item $g$: recharging rate (kWh per minute, depends on charger type).
    \end{itemize}

    Key additional constraints (on top of standard VRP constraints):
    \begin{align}
      y_j &\leq y_i - e_{ij} x_{ij} + Q_e(1-x_{ij})
          && \forall\;(i,j)\in A \quad \text{battery depletion} \\[4pt]
      0   &\leq y_i \leq Q_e
          && \forall\; i \in V\cup F \quad \text{SoC bounds} \\[4pt]
      y_f &= Q_e
          && \forall\; f\in F \;\text{ if full charge assumed}
    \end{align}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Interpreting E-VRP Battery Constraints}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Constraint (1) says: the battery on arriving at $j$ must equal
      the battery on arriving at $i$ minus the energy $e_{ij}$ consumed
      on the arc.  The term $Q_e(1-x_{ij})$ is a big-M term that makes
      the constraint inactive for unused arcs.
      \item Constraint (2) ensures the battery never exceeds its capacity
      and never goes negative (range constraint).
      \item Constraint (3) assumes the vehicle recharges to full at every
      station visit (a simplification; partial charging can also be modelled).
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Why E-VRP is Harder than Classic VRP}
    The E-VRP requires \emph{planning when and where to recharge}, which
    involves choosing: (a) whether to detour to a station, (b) which station
    (there may be several nearby), and (c) how much to charge
    (partial charging is faster but may require another stop later).
    These decisions interact with the route structure, making the problem
    significantly harder than the classical VRP.  Moreover, the optimal
    recharging policy depends on the future route --- a vehicle should recharge
    more if it has many distant customers remaining.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{E-VRP Algorithms}
    Several algorithms have been proposed:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Exact branch-and-price} (Desrochers et al.\ style):
      the column generation sub-problem is a shortest-path problem with
      resource constraints (time, battery level).  Solving the sub-problem
      requires a modified ESPPRC (Elementary Shortest Path Problem with
      Resource Constraints) algorithm.

      \item \textbf{Modified Clarke-Wright savings with station insertion:}
      after building initial routes with the savings algorithm, a post-processing
      step inserts station visits whenever a route would exceed the battery
      range.  The station is inserted at the point in the route where the
      battery would first be depleted.

      \item \textbf{Tabu search with battery management:}
      includes moves that insert, remove, or change stations.  The battery
      level is tracked throughout the route and infeasible solutions (those
      that run out of battery) are penalised.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: E-VRP Station Insertion}
    A route D $\to$ C1 $\to$ C2 $\to$ C3 $\to$ D with distances
    D-C1: 60 km, C1-C2: 80 km, C2-C3: 70 km, C3-D: 50 km.
    Battery capacity $Q_e = 150$ km-equivalent (i.e.\ 150 km on a full charge).
    Energy consumption: 1 km-equivalent per km (for simplicity).

    \medskip
    \textit{Without a station:}
    Total distance $= 60+80+70+50 = 260$ km $> 150$ km (infeasible!).

    \medskip
    \textit{With station R1 at km-60 from D (near C1):}
    Leg 1: D $\to$ C1 $\to$ R1, energy used $= 60 + 5 = 65$ km (feasible).
    Recharge at R1: battery reset to 150.
    Leg 2: R1 $\to$ C2 $\to$ C3 $\to$ D, energy $= 75+70+50 = 195$ km (still infeasible!).

    \medskip
    \textit{With two station visits:}
    Leg 1: D $\to$ C1 $\to$ R1 (65 km), Leg 2: R1 $\to$ C2 $\to$ R2
    (near C2, 85 km), Leg 3: R2 $\to$ C3 $\to$ D (120 km).
    All legs $\leq 150$ km --- feasible!

    \medskip
    \textbf{Takeaway:} Station insertion may require multiple stops and
    adds significant travel distance.  Optimising station placement and
    the number of recharging stops is a key challenge in E-VRP.
  \end{exampleblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Alternative Fuel Vehicles: CNG and Hybrid}
    Besides pure EVs, other alternative-fuel technologies are relevant for
    green VRP:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Compressed Natural Gas (CNG) vehicles:} emit 20--25\%
      less CO$_2$ per km than diesel, but require a CNG refuelling
      infrastructure.  Routing must account for limited CNG station availability.
      \item \textbf{Hybrid electric vehicles (HEV):} combine a conventional
      engine with an electric motor.  Hybrids self-recharge through regenerative
      braking, so they have no range constraint but do have reduced emissions.
      \item \textbf{Hydrogen fuel cell vehicles:} zero tailpipe emissions,
      with water as the only by-product.  Refuelling is fast (minutes vs.
      hours for EV charging) but infrastructure is very limited.
    \end{itemize}
    Each technology leads to a different variant of the green routing problem,
    with different range constraints, refuelling times, and emission profiles.
  \end{block}

\end{frame}

\section{Conclusions and Future Research}
% =============================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Conclusions: What Green VRP Has Achieved}

  Chapter 15 has surveyed the rapidly growing field of \textbf{green vehicle
  routing}, covering the full spectrum from emission models to exact
  algorithms, from speed optimisation to electric vehicle routing.

  \begin{block}{Summary of Main Contributions}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Emission models.}  The chapter reviewed average-speed models
      (MEET), instantaneous models (CMEM), and the load-speed decomposition
      model that underpins the GVRP and PRP.  These models reveal that
      \emph{speed and load are the primary drivers of fuel consumption} ---
      not just distance.

      \item \textbf{Green VRP and Pollution Routing Problem.}  The PRP is the
      most comprehensive formulation, jointly optimising routes and speeds.
      Exact and heuristic solution methods achieve 18--25\% emission reductions
      compared to traditional VRP.

      \item \textbf{Speed optimisation.}  Even on fixed routes, optimising
      vehicle speed across arcs reduces emissions by 10--15\%.

      \item \textbf{Multicriteria methods.}  Pareto-front analysis reveals
      that modest cost increases (5--10\%) can achieve substantial emission
      reductions (20--30\%).

      \item \textbf{Electric VRP.}  The E-VRP framework handles range
      constraints and recharging station decisions.  It is the most complex
      variant but represents the future of zero-emission logistics.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Future Research Directions}
    The chapter identifies several open problems and promising directions:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Real-world traffic data integration.}  Most models use
      simplified speed profiles.  Incorporating real-time traffic data
      (GPS, road sensors) into routing decisions would make models more
      accurate and actionable.

      \item \textbf{Dynamic green VRP.}  Routes are currently planned in
      advance.  Dynamic variants that re-optimise routes in real time as
      conditions change (traffic, new orders) are largely unexplored.

      \item \textbf{Lifecycle emission assessment.}  Current models focus on
      tailpipe emissions.  A full lifecycle analysis would include the
      emissions from vehicle manufacturing, fuel production (well-to-wheel),
      and end-of-life recycling.

      \item \textbf{Stochastic demand and emissions.}  Travel speeds, fuel
      consumption, and customer demand are all uncertain in practice.  Robust
      and stochastic programming approaches to green VRP remain an open
      research frontier.

      \item \textbf{Fleet mix optimisation.}  Most models assume a homogeneous
      fleet.  Optimising the \emph{composition} of a fleet (how many EVs,
      CNG trucks, and diesel vehicles) simultaneously with routing is a
      complex but important problem.

      \item \textbf{Collaborative logistics.}  Multiple carriers sharing
      vehicles and routes can dramatically reduce empty running and improve
      load factors, cutting emissions industry-wide.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{The Big Picture}
    Green vehicle routing is not just an academic exercise: the freight
    transport sector is one of the largest and most difficult-to-decarbonise
    parts of the economy.  Mathematical optimisation provides a rigorous
    toolkit for quantifying emission trade-offs and designing provably
    efficient green logistics systems.

    The field has grown from a handful of papers in the early 2000s to one
    of the most active areas of operations research, with contributions
    ranging from theoretical complexity results to real-world case studies
    at companies such as DHL, UPS, and national postal services.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Computing fuel consumption for a GVRP route using the load-dependent model.}]
import numpy as np

def fuel_consumption_route(route, demands, dist, alpha=0.3, beta=0.01):
    """
    Compute total fuel for one route using the load-dependent model:
        F = sum_arcs [ alpha * d_ij * x_ij  +  beta * d_ij * f_ij ]

    Parameters
    ----------
    route   : list of node indices, e.g. [0, 3, 1, 5, 0]
              (must start and end at depot = 0)
    demands : list/array of customer demands (index 0 = depot demand = 0)
    dist    : 2-D distance matrix  dist[i][j] in km
    alpha   : empty-vehicle fuel rate (kg fuel / km)
    beta    : load-dependent fuel rate (kg fuel / km / kg cargo)

    Returns
    -------
    total_fuel : float (kg of fuel consumed)
    """
    # Compute load on each arc (decreasing as deliveries are made)
    total_fuel = 0.0
    load = sum(demands[c] for c in route[1:-1])   # start fully loaded

    for k in range(len(route) - 1):
        i, j = route[k], route[k+1]
        d_ij = dist[i][j]
        # Fuel on this arc = empty-vehicle fuel + load-dependent fuel
        total_fuel += alpha * d_ij + beta * d_ij * load
        # After delivering to j, reduce load
        if j != 0:                                 # if j is a customer
            load -= demands[j]
    return total_fuel

# --- Worked example ---------------------------------------------------------
n = 5   # customers 1..5, depot = 0
demands = [0, 5, 3, 8, 2, 6]   # depot demand = 0

# Symmetric distance matrix (km)
dist = np.array([
    [0, 10,  8, 15,  6, 12],
    [10, 0,  6, 11,  9, 14],
    [ 8, 6,  0,  9,  7, 10],
    [15,11,  9,  0, 12,  8],
    [ 6, 9,  7, 12,  0,  5],
    [12,14, 10,  8,  5,  0],
])

route = [0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 0]
fuel = fuel_consumption_route(route, demands, dist)
print(f"Total fuel: {fuel:.2f} kg")
# Output: Total fuel: 27.84 kg (illustrative)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Python: Optimal Speed on a Single Arc}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Compute the fuel-optimal speed on a single arc with time-window constraints.}]
def optimal_speed_arc(d_ij, load, slack, c_f=1.2, c_w=5.0,
                      beta=0.0001, alpha_ij=1.2, v_min=50, v_max=110):
    """
    Optimal speed on arc (i->j) minimising fuel + driver wage cost.

    Fuel model (simplified):  F(v) = (alpha_ij + beta * v^2) * d_ij  litres
    Driver wage:              W(v) = c_w * d_ij / v
    Total cost:               C(v) = c_f * F(v) + W(v)

    Optimal (unconstrained):  v* = (c_w / (2 * c_f * beta))^(1/3)
    Then clip to [v_min,  d_ij/slack] and [v_min, v_max].

    Parameters
    ----------
    d_ij    : arc distance (km)
    load    : vehicle load on this arc (tonnes)
    slack   : available time window slack on this arc (hours)
    c_f     : fuel cost (EUR/litre)
    c_w     : driver wage (EUR/hour)
    beta    : aerodynamic drag coefficient
    alpha_ij: constant fuel term (litres/km)
    v_min, v_max : speed bounds (km/h)

    Returns
    -------
    v_opt   : optimal speed in km/h
    cost    : total cost in EUR
    """
    # Unconstrained optimum (ignoring load effect for simplicity)
    v_unconstrained = (c_w / (2 * c_f * beta)) ** (1/3)

    # Must arrive within slack time window: v >= d_ij / slack
    v_forced = d_ij / slack if slack > 0 else v_max
    v_lo = max(v_min, v_forced)          # effective lower bound

    # Clip to feasible range
    v_opt = min(v_max, max(v_lo, v_unconstrained))

    fuel_litres = (alpha_ij + beta * v_opt**2) * d_ij
    cost = c_f * fuel_litres + c_w * d_ij / v_opt
    return v_opt, cost

# --- Worked example ----------------------------------------------------------
# Arc with d=100 km, load=10t, slack=1.5 h (must arrive within 1.5h)
v, c = optimal_speed_arc(d_ij=100, load=10, slack=1.5)
print(f"Optimal speed: {v:.1f} km/h,  Cost: EUR {c:.2f}")

# With tight slack: slack=0.9 h
v2, c2 = optimal_speed_arc(d_ij=100, load=10, slack=0.9)
print(f"Tight window:  {v2:.1f} km/h,  Cost: EUR {c2:.2f}")

    """

    Returns (cost, emission) for the best solution found.

    emission = base_emission * (0.3 + 0.7 * w_emission / (w_cost + w_emission + 1e-9))
    noise = np.random.randn() * 20
    return cost + noise, emission

def generate_pareto_front(instance, n_weights=15):
    """
    Sweep weight pairs to approximate the Pareto front.
    w_cost ranges from 0 to 1; w_emission = 1 - w_cost.
    """
    results = []
    for w1 in np.linspace(0.01, 0.99, n_weights):
        w2 = 1.0 - w1
        cost, emis = solve_weighted_vrp(w1, w2, instance)
        results.append((cost, emis, w1))

    # Sort by cost (ascending) for plotting
    results.sort(key=lambda r: r[0])
    costs = [r[0] for r in results]
    emissions = [r[1] for r in results]
    weights = [r[2] for r in results]
    return costs, emissions, weights

ax.plot(costs, emissions, 'b--', alpha=0.4)
plt.colorbar(sc, ax=ax, label='Weight on cost w1')
ax.set_xlabel('Total Cost (EUR)'); ax.set_ylabel('CO2 Emissions (kg)')
