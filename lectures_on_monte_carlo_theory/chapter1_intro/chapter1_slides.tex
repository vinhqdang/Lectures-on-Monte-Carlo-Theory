\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{1.1 What Are Monte Carlo Methods?}

  \begin{block}{Definition --- Monte Carlo (MC) Methods}
    \textbf{Monte Carlo methods} are computational algorithms that rely on
    \emph{repeated random (pseudo-random) sampling} to obtain numerical
    results.  They are used to solve problems in numerical integration,
    optimisation, simulation of stochastic systems, and Bayesian statistics.
  \end{block}

  The name ``Monte Carlo'' was coined by Metropolis and Ulam in 1949, after
  the famous casino in Monaco, because the method resembles repeated gambling
  experiments.  The approach was developed at Los Alamos in the 1940s by
  Stanislaw Ulam, John von Neumann, and Nicholas Metropolis, originally to
  simulate neutron diffusion in nuclear weapons design.

  \bigskip
  The fundamental idea is elegant: if you cannot compute a quantity
  analytically (by formula), \emph{simulate} the underlying random experiment
  a very large number of times and average the results.  Two classical theorems
  from probability theory guarantee that this works:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{Law of Large Numbers} (LLN) guarantees that the average
      of many independent repetitions converges to the true expected value ---
      the method is correct in the limit.
    \item The \textbf{Central Limit Theorem} (CLT) tells us how large the
      error is for a given number of repetitions, allowing us to build
      confidence intervals around our estimate.
  \end{itemize}

  \framebreak

  \textbf{Why does randomness help?}  There are three broad problem classes
  where deterministic methods struggle and Monte Carlo excels:

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{High-dimensional integration.}  Evaluating an integral over a
      $d$-dimensional domain using a regular grid requires $m^d$ function
      evaluations (where $m$ is the number of grid points per dimension).
      This is called the \emph{curse of dimensionality} --- the cost grows
      exponentially with $d$.  For example, with $m = 10$ points per dimension
      and $d = 20$ dimensions, a grid would require $10^{20}$ evaluations ---
      far beyond any computer.  The Monte Carlo estimator, by contrast,
      achieves a root-mean-squared error of $O(n^{-1/2})$ regardless of $d$,
      where $n$ (italic $n$) is the number of random samples.  This
      \emph{dimension-independence} makes MC the method of choice for
      high-dimensional problems in finance, physics, and machine learning.

    \item \textbf{NP-hard combinatorial problems.}  Problems like the
      Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP) have a search space of $n!$ (read
      ``$n$ factorial'') permutations, which grows faster than any
      exponential.  For $n = 50$ cities, $50!$ is approximately $3 \times
      10^{64}$ --- exhaustive search is physically impossible.  Monte Carlo
      heuristics such as simulated annealing and the cross-entropy method find
      near-optimal solutions efficiently by intelligently exploring the space.

    \item \textbf{Complex stochastic systems.}  When the dynamics of a system
      involve intrinsic randomness (e.g., queuing networks, financial models,
      particle physics), direct simulation is often the only tractable approach.
      We simply run the system many times and record statistics of interest.
  \end{enumerate}

  \framebreak

  \textbf{Structure of this book.}  The book is structured to build from
  foundational theory to advanced methods, always connecting mathematical
  theory to Python implementations.  Chapter 1 provides three motivating
  examples that recur throughout the text.

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ch.~1} Introduction: three motivating examples ---
      random walks (the arcsine law and the law of the iterated logarithm),
      the Travelling Salesman Problem, and numerical integration.
    \item \textbf{Ch.~2} Theory of pseudorandom number generators (PRNGs)
      and statistical tests for randomness quality.
    \item \textbf{Ch.~3} Generating random variables from arbitrary
      distributions: inversion, rejection, and transformation methods.
    \item \textbf{Ch.~4} Output analysis: confidence intervals, bias,
      variance estimation, and consistent estimators.
    \item \textbf{Ch.~5} Variance reduction techniques: stratification,
      antithetic variables, importance sampling, and the cross-entropy method.
    \item \textbf{Ch.~6} Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC): the
      Metropolis--Hastings algorithm, the Gibbs sampler, and coupling from
      the past (CFTP) for exact sampling.
    \item \textbf{Ch.~7} Stochastic optimisation: simulated annealing and
      the cross-entropy method applied to the TSP and other problems.
    \item \textbf{Ch.~8} Simulation of queues and related stochastic processes.
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Distinction: MC vs.\ Quasi-MC}
    \textbf{Monte Carlo (MC)} uses \emph{pseudo-random} sequences ---
    deterministic but statistically indistinguishable from true randomness.
    \textbf{Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC)} uses carefully designed
    \emph{low-discrepancy} sequences that fill $[0,1]^d$ (the $d$-dimensional
    unit hypercube) more uniformly than random points.  QMC can converge
    faster for smooth integrands, but has important limitations for simulation
    tasks.  Section~1.4 and 1.5 compare the two approaches in detail.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{1.1 Pseudorandom Numbers in Python}

  Before tackling the theory, it is essential to know how to generate
  pseudo-random numbers in Python.  The \texttt{NumPy} library provides a
  modern, reproducible interface via \texttt{default\_rng()}.  Passing a seed
  (an integer starting value) makes the entire sequence deterministic, which
  is crucial for reproducibility and debugging: if you share your seed, anyone
  can reproduce your results exactly.

  \begin{block}{NumPy Recommended API (NumPy $\ge$ 1.17)}
    The function \texttt{default\_rng(seed)} returns a generator object backed
    by the PCG64 algorithm, where \texttt{seed} is any integer you choose.
    Calling \texttt{rng.random(k)} draws $k$ (integer $k$) samples from
    $\mathcal{U}[0,1)$ (the uniform distribution on the interval from 0
    inclusive to 1 exclusive).  Calling \texttt{rng.integers(low=0, high=M,
    size=k)} draws $k$ integers uniformly from $\{0, 1, \ldots, M-1\}$.
  \end{block}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np
from numpy.random import default_rng, PCG64

# Recommended: default_rng with explicit seed
rng = default_rng(seed=31415)           # PCG64 generator, seeded

u   = rng.random(size=5)                # 5 samples from Uniform[0,1)
b   = rng.integers(low=0, high=2, size=10)  # 10 values in {0,1}
steps = 1 - 2 * b                       # convert {0,1} -> {-1,+1}

print("Uniform samples:", u)
print("Random steps:   ", steps)
# Explicit generator (same result):
rng2 = default_rng(PCG64(seed=31415))
\end{lstlisting}

  The PCG64 generator has a period (cycle length before repeating) of
  $2^{128}$, which is so astronomically large that it will never cycle in
  any practical simulation.  It passes all standard statistical tests for
  randomness.  Successive calls draw from the same stream, so
  \texttt{rng.random(5)} followed by \texttt{rng.random(5)} yields ten
  \emph{different} values --- the internal state advances automatically.

\end{frame}

\section{1.2 Simple Symmetric Random Walk}
%% ===================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{1.2.1 Setup: The Coin-Toss Game}

  The \emph{simple symmetric random walk} is one of the most fundamental
  objects in probability theory.  It models phenomena as diverse as stock
  price fluctuations, polymer chain conformations, and diffusion of particles
  in a liquid.  It is a perfect test bed for Monte Carlo simulation: the exact
  analytical answers are known from classical limit theorems (due to Feller,
  1950), so we can verify that our simulations converge to the right values.
  This is how practitioners ``validate'' simulation code before applying it
  to problems with unknown answers.

  \textbf{The game (Feller, 1950).}  Two players, A and B, repeatedly bet on
  coin tosses.  Player A wins $+1$ monetary unit from B when heads appears,
  and loses $1$ unit when tails appears.  Player A's cumulative fortune (net
  winnings) after $n$ (integer $n$) tosses is denoted $S_n$ (S subscript n).
  The starting fortune is $S_0 = 0$.

  \begin{block}{Definition --- Simple Symmetric Random Walk (SSRW)}
    Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ (X subscript 1, X subscript 2, etc.) be
    independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with
    \[
      \mathbb{P}(X_j = +1) \;=\; \mathbb{P}(X_j = -1) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}.
    \]
    Here $\mathbb{P}$ (blackboard P) denotes probability, $X_j = +1$ means
    heads on toss $j$, and $X_j = -1$ means tails.  The symbol $j$ (j) runs
    over $1, 2, 3, \ldots$\,.  Define the \textbf{partial sums}:
    \[
      S_0 = 0, \qquad S_n = \sum_{j=1}^{n} X_j, \quad n = 1, 2, \ldots
    \]
    Here $\sum$ (Sigma, meaning ``sum'') adds up all the steps $X_j$ for
    $j$ running from 1 to $n$.  The sequence $S_0, S_1, S_2, \ldots$ is
    called the \textbf{simple symmetric random walk}.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read This Formula}
    $S_n$ is player A's running total after $n$ coin tosses.  Because the
    steps $X_j$ are independent (each toss is fair), the walk is equally
    likely to go up or down at each step.  Note that $S_n$ must lie in the
    set $\{-n, -n+2, \ldots, n-2, n\}$, since the net result of $n$ steps of
    $\pm 1$ has the same parity as $n$.  In particular, $|S_n| \le n$: the
    walk can never exceed its maximum possible value.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \textbf{Four questions about the walk.}  We consider a walk of $2N$ steps
  (an even number, so that ties can only occur at even times).  The following
  four questions all have surprising answers:

  \begin{description}
    \item[Q1.] Let $L_{2N}$ (L subscript 2N) $= \max\{i \le 2N : S_i = 0\}$
      be the \emph{last moment of a tie}, i.e., the last time the two players
      are exactly equal.  What is the distribution of $L_{2N}$?  Intuition
      says it should be near the end --- is it?
    \item[Q2.] Let $P(\alpha, \beta)$ (where $\alpha$ (alpha) and $\beta$
      (beta) are fractions between 0 and 1 with $\alpha < \beta$) be the
      probability that player A is winning for a fraction of time between
      $\alpha$ and $\beta$.  Is this distribution concentrated near $1/2$,
      as one might expect for a fair game?
    \item[Q3.] How do the oscillations (fluctuations) of $S_n$ grow with $n$?
      Are they proportional to $n$, to $\sqrt{n}$ (square root of $n$), or
      something in between?
    \item[Q4.] What is the probability that one player is \emph{always}
      winning, i.e., that $S_k > 0$ for all steps $k = 1, \ldots, 2N$?
  \end{description}

  All four questions have exact analytical answers from classical limit
  theorems.  Monte Carlo simulation lets us \emph{verify} these answers
  numerically and develop intuition about them before studying the proofs.

  \framebreak

  \textbf{Formal definitions of the key quantities.}

  \begin{block}{Last Tie ($L_{2n}$) and Time Above Zero ($L^+_{2n}$)}
    \[
      L_{2n} = \sup\{m \le 2n : S_m = 0\}
    \]
    reads as: $L_{2n}$ is the \emph{last time up to step $2n$ at which the
    walk equals zero}, i.e., the last tie.  Here ``sup'' means
    \emph{supremum} (the largest element of the set).

    \[
      L^+_{2n} = \#\{j = 1, \ldots, 2n : S_{j-1} \ge 0 \;\text{or}\; S_j \ge 0\}
    \]
    reads as: $L^+_{2n}$ (L-plus subscript 2n) is the \emph{number of steps
    during which player A is winning}.  A step $j$ is counted as ``above
    zero'' if \emph{at least one} of the two endpoints of the segment
    (from step $j-1$ to step $j$) is non-negative.  The symbol $\#$
    (hash or ``counting measure'') denotes the number of elements in a set.
  \end{block}

  Both $L_{2n}$ and $L^+_{2n}$ grow to infinity as $n \to \infty$ (as the
  number of steps $n$ grows without bound).  However, the \emph{fractions}
  $L_{2n}/(2n)$ and $L^+_{2n}/(2n)$ both lie in $[0,1]$, and their
  distributions converge to the famous \emph{arcsine distribution}.

    \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{fig_ssrw_single}

  \textit{A single realisation of the SSRW for $N = 1000$ steps.  Notice the
  long ``excursions'' on one side of zero --- a consequence of the arcsine law
  discussed in Section~1.2.3.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{1.2.2 How Large Are the Fluctuations? The Law of the Iterated Logarithm}

  Our first question is: how large does $|S_n|$ (the absolute value of the
  walk, measuring how far from zero it has gone) get as $n$ grows?

  \textbf{Three candidate growth rates.}  Since $|S_n| \le n$, one might try
  several normalisations:

  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normalise by $n$:} The strong law of large numbers (SLLN)
      gives $S_n/n \to 0$ almost surely (with probability 1) because the
      mean of each step is zero.  This means dividing by $n$ is
      \emph{too aggressive} --- the normalised walk vanishes.

    \item \textbf{Normalise by $\sqrt{n}$ (square root of $n$):}  The Central
      Limit Theorem (CLT) says $S_n / \sqrt{n}$ converges \emph{in
      distribution} to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ (a standard normal distribution,
      the bell curve).  Here $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ has mean 0 and variance 1.
      However, the sequence $S_n/\sqrt{n}$ does \emph{not} converge almost
      surely --- it oscillates indefinitely.  So $\sqrt{n}$ is
      \emph{too weak}: the normalised walk does not settle down.

    \item \textbf{Normalise by $\sqrt{2n \log \log n}$:}  This is the
      \emph{correct} scale, as stated by the Law of the Iterated Logarithm.
      Here ``$\log$'' (log) means the natural logarithm (base $e \approx
      2.718$), and ``$\log \log n$'' means the logarithm of the logarithm ---
      a very slowly growing function.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{block}{Law of the Iterated Logarithm (LIL) --- Khintchine, 1924; Kolmogorov, 1929}
    For the simple symmetric random walk $S_n = \sum_{j=1}^n X_j$,
    where $X_j \in \{-1, +1\}$ each with probability $1/2$:
    \[
      \mathbb{P}\!\left(
        \limsup_{n \to \infty}
        \frac{S_n}{\sqrt{2n \log \log n}} = +1
      \right) = 1
      \quad\text{and}\quad
      \mathbb{P}\!\left(
        \liminf_{n \to \infty}
        \frac{S_n}{\sqrt{2n \log \log n}} = -1
      \right) = 1.
    \]
  \end{block}

  Here ``$\limsup$'' (limit superior, or lim-sup) is the largest accumulation
  point of the sequence --- the highest value it gets close to infinitely
  often.  Symmetrically, ``$\liminf$'' (limit inferior) is the smallest
  accumulation point.  The formula says both are $\pm 1$ with probability 1.

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the LIL}
    In plain language: the walk $S_n$ fluctuates without ever settling down.
    It touches values arbitrarily close to $+\sqrt{2n \log \log n}$ (the
    upper envelope) and $-\sqrt{2n \log \log n}$ (the lower envelope)
    infinitely often as $n$ grows.  But it never permanently \emph{exceeds}
    these envelopes.  The ``iterated logarithm'' $\log \log n$ is the
    correction factor beyond $\sqrt{n}$: it grows incredibly slowly (for
    $n = 10^{100}$, we have $\log \log n \approx 5.4$), so the envelope is
    only slightly wider than $\sqrt{n}$.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \textbf{What does $\sqrt{2n \log \log n}$ look like numerically?}

  Let us plug in a concrete value.  For $n = 10{,}000$:
  \begin{align*}
    \sqrt{n} &= \sqrt{10{,}000} = 100, \\
    \log n &= \log(10{,}000) \approx 9.21 \quad \text{(natural log)}, \\
    \log \log n &= \log(9.21) \approx 2.22, \\
    \sqrt{2n \log \log n} &= \sqrt{2 \times 10{,}000 \times 2.22} \approx \sqrt{44{,}400} \approx 211.
  \end{align*}
  So at step $n = 10{,}000$, the LIL envelope is approximately $211$, which
  is about $2.1$ times larger than $\sqrt{n} = 100$.  The gap between the
  two envelopes widens only very slowly (logarithmically) as $n$ grows.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_lil_envelope}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.45\textwidth}
      \textbf{Simulation evidence.}
      The figure shows 80 independent SSRW trajectories of length
      $n = 2^{14} = 16{,}384$ steps.  The blue curves are the LIL envelopes
      $\pm\sqrt{2n \log \log n}$; the red dashed lines are $\pm\sqrt{n}$
      for comparison.  Every simulated path stays inside the LIL envelope,
      consistent with the theorem.  The paths occasionally touch the envelope
      --- consistent with the theorem saying this happens ``infinitely often''
      in the limit.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Takeaway}
    The walk grows neither as slowly as $\sqrt{n}$ nor as fast as $n$.  The
    correct scale is $\sqrt{2n \log \log n}$, which is slightly (but only
    slightly) larger than $\sqrt{n}$.  This precise scaling has important
    consequences for statistical tests and for understanding variability in
    long simulations.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{1.2.3 The Arcsine Law}

  The arcsine law answers questions Q1 and Q2, and is among the most
  counter-intuitive results in elementary probability theory.

  \textbf{What you might expect.}  Since the walk is perfectly symmetric
  (equal probability of going up or down), you might think that the fraction
  of time that player A is winning is typically close to $1/2$.  After all,
  the game is fair, so shouldn't both players lead for roughly equal amounts
  of time?  The arcsine law says the \emph{opposite}: being ahead for
  \emph{nearly all} or \emph{nearly none} of the time is \emph{most likely},
  and being ahead for exactly half the time is the \emph{least likely}
  outcome.

  \begin{block}{Arcsine Law (Feller, 1950; Durrett, 1996)}
    For $0 \le \alpha < \beta \le 1$ (fractions between 0 and 1), as
    $n \to \infty$ (as the number of steps grows without bound):
    \[
      \mathbb{P}\!\left(
        \alpha \;\le\; \frac{L^+_{2n}}{2n} \;\le\; \beta
      \right)
      \;\longrightarrow\;
      \int_\alpha^\beta \frac{dx}{\pi \sqrt{x(1-x)}}.
      \tag{1.2.1}
    \]
  \end{block}

  Here $\pi$ (pi) is the mathematical constant $\approx 3.14159$, and the
  integral $\int_\alpha^\beta$ (integral from $\alpha$ to $\beta$) computes the
  area under the curve $1/(\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)})$ between $x = \alpha$ and
  $x = \beta$.  The same limiting distribution holds for $L_{2n}/(2n)$.

  \textbf{Why is this called the ``arcsine'' law?}  The cumulative
  distribution function (the probability that the fraction is at most
  $\beta$) is:
  \[
    \int_0^\beta \frac{dx}{\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)}}
    \;=\; \frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\!\bigl(\sqrt{\beta}\bigr),
  \]
  where $\arcsin$ (arcsine, the inverse of the sine function) appears.  This
  is where the name comes from.

  The probability density function $p(x) = 1/\bigl(\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)}\bigr)$
  has a characteristic \textbf{U-shape}: it equals $+\infty$ at $x = 0$
  and $x = 1$ (the walk is most likely to spend all or none of its time above
  zero), and achieves its \emph{minimum} value of $2/\pi \approx 0.637$ at
  $x = 1/2$ (the least likely outcome is splitting the lead evenly).

  \framebreak

  \textbf{Numerical illustration of the U-shape.}  Let us compute the
  probability of a ``fair'' outcome (player A leads between 40\% and 60\% of
  the time) and compare it to the probability of an ``extreme'' outcome
  (player A leads more than 90\% or less than 10\% of the time):

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Numerical Calculation}
    The probability of a ``near-equal'' outcome:
    \[
      \mathbb{P}\!\left(0.4 \le \frac{L^+_{2n}}{2n} \le 0.6\right)
      \approx \frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\sqrt{0.6}) - \frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\sqrt{0.4})
      \approx 0.795 - 0.667 \approx 0.128.
    \]
    Only about $12.8\%$ of games end with a ``fair'' lead split!

    The probability of an ``extreme'' outcome (one player dominates):
    \[
      \mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{L^+_{2n}}{2n} \le 0.1\right)
      + \mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{L^+_{2n}}{2n} \ge 0.9\right)
      \approx 2 \times \frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\sqrt{0.1})
      \approx 2 \times 0.205 \approx 0.410.
    \]
    Over $41\%$ of games end with one player leading for more than $90\%$ of
    the time --- more than three times as common as the ``fair'' outcome!
  \end{exampleblock}

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.60\textwidth]{fig_arcsine_law}
  \end{center}
  \textit{Histogram of $L^+_{1000}/1000$ from $R = 10{,}000$ replications of
  1000-step walks.  The red curve is the theoretical arcsine density
  $1/(\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)})$.  The U-shape is clearly visible: most walks spend
  nearly all their time on one side of zero.}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Gambler's Fallacy Refuted}
    The arcsine law overturns the naive intuition that ``fair'' games lead to
    equal sharing of the lead.  A player who is behind for the first $99\%$
    of a game is \emph{not} especially likely to ``recover'' --- in fact,
    being behind for the entire game is a completely typical outcome.
    This is the \emph{gambler's fallacy} refuted: past losses do not make
    future wins more likely.  The game has no memory.
  \end{alertblock}

  \textbf{Connection to randomness testing.}  The arcsine law is not merely
  a theoretical curiosity.  Later in the book (Section~2.5), we will use the
  arcsine law to construct a \emph{statistical test} for the quality of a
  pseudorandom number generator: if the generator produces numbers that are
  truly random, then the $L^+$ statistic should follow the arcsine
  distribution.  A significant deviation from the arcsine law signals a
  defect in the generator.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{1.2.4 Python: Simulating the SSRW and the LIL Envelope}

  The following code simulates a single SSRW trajectory and overlays the
  LIL envelope $\pm\sqrt{2n \log \log n}$.  The key implementation step is
  converting uniform integers in $\{0, 1\}$ to steps in $\{-1, +1\}$ via the
  formula $X_j = 1 - 2B_j$ where $B_j \in \{0, 1\}$: when $B_j = 0$ we get
  $X_j = 1$ (heads), and when $B_j = 1$ we get $X_j = -1$ (tails).  Then
  \texttt{np.cumsum} (cumulative sum) computes all the partial sums at once.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from numpy.random import default_rng

rng = default_rng(seed=31415)
N   = 1000                               # walk has 2N steps

steps = 1 - 2 * rng.integers(0, 2, size=2*N)  # {-1, +1}
S     = np.concatenate([[0], steps.cumsum()])  # S_0=0, S_1,...,S_{2N}

# LIL envelope: +/- sqrt(2 * n * log(log(n)))
# We start from n=2 to avoid log(log(1))=log(0) which is -infinity
n_arr    = np.arange(2, 2*N + 1)
envelope = np.sqrt(2 * n_arr * np.log(np.log(n_arr)))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 3.5))
ax.plot(range(2*N + 1), S, lw=0.8, color='steelblue', label='$S_n$')
ax.plot(n_arr,  envelope, 'b-', lw=1.8, alpha=0.7,
        label=r'LIL: $\pm\sqrt{2n\log\log n}$')
ax.plot(n_arr, -envelope, 'b-', lw=1.8, alpha=0.7)
ax.axhline(0, color='k', lw=0.5, ls='--')
ax.set_xlabel('Step $n$')
ax.set_ylabel('$S_n$')
ax.legend(loc='upper left')
ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
\end{lstlisting}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{1.2.5 Python: Verifying the Arcsine Law}

  To verify the arcsine law statistically, we simulate $R = 10{,}000$
  independent walks of $2n = 1000$ steps each, compute $L^+_{2n}/(2n)$ for
  each walk, and compare the histogram to the theoretical arcsine density.
  Vectorising the simulation over all $R$ replications simultaneously using
  NumPy's array operations makes this fast enough to run interactively.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from numpy.random import default_rng

def fraction_above_zero(n=500, R=10_000, seed=0):
    """Return array of L^+_{2n}/(2n) for R independent walks.

    n  : half-length (walk has 2n steps)
    R  : number of independent replications
    Returns: array of shape (R,), each value in [0,1]
    """
    rng   = default_rng(seed)
    # shape: (R, 2n) -- each row is one walk's steps
    steps = 1 - 2 * rng.integers(0, 2, size=(R, 2*n))
    # cumsum along axis=1, prepend column of zeros --> shape (R, 2n+1)
    S = np.hstack([np.zeros((R, 1)), steps.cumsum(axis=1)])
    # Step j is counted if S_{j-1}>=0 OR S_j>=0 (at least one endpoint >= 0)
    above = (S[:, :-1] >= 0) | (S[:, 1:] >= 0)
    return above.sum(axis=1) / (2*n)   # shape (R,), each in [0,1]

fracs = fraction_above_zero(n=500, R=10_000)

# Theoretical arcsine density: f(x) = 1 / (pi * sqrt(x*(1-x)))
x   = np.linspace(0.005, 0.995, 500)
pdf = 1.0 / (np.pi * np.sqrt(x * (1.0 - x)))

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 4))
plt.hist(fracs, bins=60, density=True, alpha=0.6, color='steelblue',
         label='Simulation ($n=500$, $R=10000$)')
plt.plot(x, pdf, 'r-', lw=2.2,
         label=r'Arcsine density $\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)}}$')
plt.xlabel(r'$L^+_{2n}/(2n)$  (fraction of time player A is winning)')
plt.ylabel('Density')
plt.title('Arcsine Law: verified by simulation')
plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
\end{lstlisting}

\end{frame}

\section{1.3 Travelling Salesman Problem}
%% ===================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{1.3.1 The TSP: Problem Statement and Why It Is Hard}

  The Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP) is a paradigmatic hard combinatorial
  optimisation problem.  It arises in logistics (planning delivery routes),
  circuit board design (minimising wire length), genome sequencing (ordering
  DNA fragments), and many other practical domains.

  \textbf{The problem.}  A salesman must visit $n$ cities exactly once and
  return to the starting city.  The \emph{distance} (or cost) of travelling
  from city $i$ to city $j$ is given by the entry $\mathbf{M}_{ij}$ (M
  subscript ij) in an $n \times n$ matrix $\mathbf{M}$ (bold M, an array of
  distances).  The entry is $\mathbf{M}_{ij} = \infty$ (infinity) if there
  is no direct route from $i$ to $j$.  The goal is to find the
  \emph{shortest closed tour} (a route that starts and ends at the same city
  and visits each city exactly once).

  Mathematically, a \emph{tour} is a permutation $\sigma$ (sigma, a
  re-ordering) of the cities $\{1, \ldots, n\}$.  The symbol $\sigma(k)$
  (sigma of k) means the $k$-th city visited.  The total length of the tour is:
  \[
    l(\sigma) = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \mathbf{M}_{\sigma(k),\,\sigma(k+1)}
    + \mathbf{M}_{\sigma(n),\,\sigma(1)}.
  \]
  The first sum adds up the distances of all legs except the last, and the
  final term $\mathbf{M}_{\sigma(n),\sigma(1)}$ closes the loop by returning
  to the starting city.

  \begin{block}{Optimisation Problem}
    Find the optimal permutation (the shortest tour):
    \[
      \sigma^* = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\sigma \in S_n} l(\sigma),
    \]
    where $S_n$ (S subscript n) denotes the set of all $n!$ permutations of
    $n$ cities, and ``$\operatorname{arg\,min}$'' means ``the permutation
    that achieves the minimum value of $l(\sigma)$''.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \textbf{Why is the TSP computationally hard?}

  The search space has $n!$ (n factorial) elements.  The factorial function
  grows faster than any exponential:

  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{rlr}
    \toprule
    $n$ cities & $n!$ permutations & Time at $10^9$/sec \\
    \midrule
    10  & $3{,}628{,}800 \approx 3.6 \times 10^6$  & $0.004$ seconds \\
    15  & $\approx 1.3 \times 10^{12}$  & $21$ minutes \\
    20  & $\approx 2.4 \times 10^{18}$  & $76$ years \\
    50  & $\approx 3.0 \times 10^{64}$  & longer than the age of the universe \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  At $10^9$ (one billion) permutation evaluations per second, checking all
  $50!$ tours would require approximately $10^{55}$ years --- the current age
  of the universe is only about $1.4 \times 10^{10}$ years.  Exhaustive
  search is completely infeasible for $n \ge 20$.

  The TSP belongs to the class of \textbf{NP-complete} problems: no algorithm
  is known that solves all instances in polynomial time (time proportional to
  $n^k$ for some fixed $k$), and most computer scientists believe no such
  algorithm exists.  Exact algorithms are practical only for $n \lesssim 50$
  (using clever branch-and-bound methods), and even then they can be slow.

  \framebreak

  \textbf{Monte Carlo heuristics for the TSP.}  Since exact optimisation is
  infeasible for large $n$, we use \emph{randomised approximation algorithms}
  that find near-optimal (but not necessarily optimal) solutions quickly.

  \begin{description}
    \item[Random restart.] Sample $R$ (integer $R$) random permutations
      uniformly from $S_n$, evaluate $l(\sigma)$ for each, and return the
      best.  This is the simplest Monte Carlo approach.  For $n = 15$ cities
      and $R = 20{,}000$ trials, random search typically finds a tour that is
      30--50\% longer than the optimal.  The figure below shows the result.

    \item[Simulated Annealing (Ch.~7).] Start with a random tour.  At each
      step, propose a small local modification called a \emph{2-opt move}:
      reverse a segment of the tour (remove two edges and reconnect the
      endpoints differently).  Always accept improvements.  Accept worsening
      moves with probability $\exp(-\Delta l / T)$ (exp of minus Delta-l
      divided by T), where $\Delta l$ (Delta l) is the increase in tour
      length and $T$ (T) is a ``temperature'' parameter that decreases over
      time.  The probabilistic acceptance of worsening moves allows escape
      from local optima.  This approach, introduced by Kirkpatrick, Gelatt
      and Vecchi (1983), produces near-optimal tours far more reliably than
      random restart.

    \item[Cross-Entropy Method (Chs.~5, 7).] Maintain a probability
      distribution over tours, sample batches of tours, keep the best
      fraction (the ``elite sample''), and update the distribution to fit
      the elite tours.  Iterating drives the distribution toward optimal
      tours.
  \end{description}

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{fig_tsp}
  \end{center}
  \textit{Left: 15 random cities connected in an arbitrary random order (no
  optimisation; many edge crossings visible).  Right: best tour found by
  random search over $R = 20{,}000$ random permutations.  The absence of
  crossings is a necessary condition for an optimal tour.}

\end{frame}

%% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[fragile]{1.3.2 Python: Random Tour Search for the TSP}

  The following implementation evaluates $R = 20{,}000$ random tours of
  $n = 15$ cities and keeps the shortest one.  The function
  \texttt{tour\_length} uses \texttt{np.roll} (``roll'' or shift the array
  by one position) to implement the cyclic distance computation efficiently
  without an explicit loop.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np
from numpy.random import default_rng

def tour_length(cities, perm):
    """Compute the total Euclidean length of a closed tour.

    cities : shape (n, 2) -- the (x, y) coordinates of each city
    perm   : permutation of {0, ..., n-1} -- the order to visit cities
    Returns: the total distance of the tour (a single float)
    """
    # shifted[k] = perm[k+1 mod n], so it gives the NEXT city after perm[k]
    # This creates the "closing the loop" effect automatically
    shifted = np.roll(perm, -1)
    # Compute (x, y) differences for each leg of the tour: shape (n, 2)
    diffs   = cities[perm] - cities[shifted]
    # Euclidean distance of each leg: sqrt(dx^2 + dy^2), then sum all legs
    return np.linalg.norm(diffs, axis=1).sum()

def random_tour_search(cities, R=20_000, seed=42):
    """Return (best_tour, best_length) from R random permutations.

    R    : number of random tours to try (more is better but slower)
    seed : random seed for reproducibility
    """
    rng = default_rng(seed)
    n   = len(cities)
    best_len, best_tour = np.inf, None
    for _ in range(R):
        perm = rng.permutation(n)          # draw a uniform random tour
        L    = tour_length(cities, perm)
        if L < best_len:                   # keep the best found so far
            best_len  = L
            best_tour = perm.copy()
    return best_tour, best_len

# Generate 15 random cities uniformly in the unit square [0,1]^2
rng    = default_rng(seed=7)
cities = rng.random((15, 2))

tour, length = random_tour_search(cities, R=20_000)
print(f"Best tour length (random search, R=20000): {length:.4f}")
# Simulated annealing (Chapter 7) achieves roughly 30% shorter tours
\end{lstlisting}

  This simple baseline is instructive: $R = 20{,}000$ random tours is entirely
  feasible on a modern computer (milliseconds), yet the result is far from the
  optimal tour.  Chapter~7 develops simulated annealing, which finds
  near-optimal solutions in comparable time by intelligently exploring the
  neighbourhood of the current tour.

\end{frame}

\section{1.4 Computing Integrals with Monte Carlo}
%% ===================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{1.4.1 The Monte Carlo Integration Framework}

  One of the most important applications of Monte Carlo methods is numerical
  estimation of integrals.  This section develops the basic theory and shows
  why MC is \emph{dimension-independent} in its convergence rate --- a
  remarkable property that grid-based quadrature methods do not share.

  \textbf{The integration problem.}  We want to compute:
  \[
    I = \int_{[0,1]^d} k(\mathbf{u})\, d\mathbf{u},
  \]
  where $k : [0,1]^d \to \mathbb{R}$ (k, a function from the $d$-dimensional
  unit cube to the real numbers) is an integrable function, $[0,1]^d$
  (pronounced ``the $d$-dimensional unit hypercube'') is the set of all
  $d$-dimensional vectors with each coordinate between 0 and 1, and
  $\mathbf{u}$ (bold u) is a $d$-dimensional variable of integration.  This
  formulation covers a large class of problems because any integral over a
  bounded domain can be transformed to this form by a change of variables.

  \textbf{The probabilistic interpretation.}  If $\mathbf{U}$ (bold U) is a
  random vector \emph{uniformly distributed} on $[0,1]^d$ (meaning each
  coordinate is independently and uniformly distributed on $[0,1)$), then:
  \[
    I \;=\; \mathbb{E}[k(\mathbf{U})].
  \]
  The integral equals the \emph{expected value} (average) of $k(\mathbf{U})$
  when $\mathbf{U}$ is uniform.  This is the fundamental connection between
  integration and probability that makes Monte Carlo work.

  \begin{block}{Monte Carlo Estimator (formula 1.2.2 in the book)}
    Let $\mathbf{U}_1, \mathbf{U}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{U}_n$ be i.i.d.\
    (independent and identically distributed) uniform random vectors on
    $[0,1]^d$.  Define the \textbf{Monte Carlo estimator}:
    \[
      \hat{Y}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} k(\mathbf{U}_i).
      \tag{1.2.2}
    \]
    Here $\hat{Y}_n$ (Y-hat sub n; the hat symbol $\hat{\phantom{Y}}$
    means ``estimate'') is the sample average of $n$ independent evaluations
    of $k$.  By the Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN),
    $\hat{Y}_n \to I$ almost surely (with probability 1) as $n \to \infty$.
    The estimator is \textbf{unbiased}: $\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}_n] = I$ for every $n$.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Variance and Error Rate of the MC Estimator}
    Since the $\mathbf{U}_i$ are i.i.d., the variance (the square of the
    spread around the mean) of $\hat{Y}_n$ is:
    \[
      \mathrm{Var}(\hat{Y}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n},
      \quad\text{where}\quad
      \sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}(k(\mathbf{U}))
      = \int_{[0,1]^d} k(\mathbf{u})^2\, d\mathbf{u} - I^2.
    \]
    Here $\sigma^2$ (sigma-squared) is the variance of a single evaluation
    $k(\mathbf{U})$, and $n$ (n) is the number of samples.  The standard
    deviation $\sigma$ (sigma) measures the typical spread of a single
    evaluation around the mean $I$.  By the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), for
    large $n$:
    \[
      \sqrt{n}\,(\hat{Y}_n - I) \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),
    \]
    meaning $\sqrt{n}$ (square root of n) times the error converges
    \emph{in distribution} ($\xrightarrow{d}$) to a normal distribution with
    mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2$.  Consequently:
    \[
      \mathrm{RMSE}(\hat{Y}_n)
      \;=\; \sqrt{\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat{Y}_n - I)^2\bigr]}
      \;=\; \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
      \;=\; O(n^{-1/2}).
    \]
    Here RMSE (root mean squared error) is the standard measure of
    estimation accuracy, and $O(n^{-1/2})$ (``order $n$ to the minus
    one-half'') means the error decreases at this rate.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read This Error Rate}
    The RMSE is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$: to halve the error, you must \emph{quadruple}
    the number of samples (because $\sqrt{4n} = 2\sqrt{n}$).  This is
    ``diminishing returns'': going from $n = 100$ to $n = 400$ halves the
    error; going from $n = 400$ to $n = 1{,}600$ halves it again.  The
    critical fact is that this rate $O(n^{-1/2})$ is \textbf{completely
    independent of the dimension $d$} --- whether $d = 1$ or $d = 100$, you
    need the same number of samples for the same accuracy.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \textbf{The curse of dimensionality and why Monte Carlo wins.}

  A regular grid with $m$ (integer $m$) points per dimension requires $m^d$
  total function evaluations, which grows exponentially with the dimension
  $d$.  A standard trapezoidal rule on this grid achieves error
  $O(m^{-2/d})$, so to get error $\varepsilon$ (epsilon, a small positive
  number representing target accuracy) we need $m \sim \varepsilon^{-d/2}$
  points per dimension, giving $m^d \sim \varepsilon^{-d}$ total evaluations.

  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    Method & Error & Evaluations for $\varepsilon = 10^{-2}$, $d = 10$ \\
    \midrule
    Regular grid ($m^d$ pts)   & $O(m^{-2/d})$ & $100^{10} = 10^{20}$ \\
    Monte Carlo ($n$ pts)      & $O(n^{-1/2})$ & $\sigma^2 / \varepsilon^2 \approx 10^4$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  For $d = 10$ dimensions and target error $\varepsilon = 0.01$, the grid
  requires $10^{20}$ evaluations (computationally impossible), while MC
  requires only about $10^4$ evaluations (milliseconds on a laptop).  For
  $d \ge 5$, Monte Carlo almost always dominates grid-based quadrature.

  \framebreak

  \textbf{Discrepancy: measuring how ``uniformly'' points fill the space.}

  The concept of \emph{discrepancy} provides another perspective on the MC
  error rate.  Given a sequence of points $\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n
  \in [0,1)^d$, the \emph{star-discrepancy} measures how far the empirical
  distribution of those points is from perfect uniformity:

  \begin{block}{Discrepancy (formula 1.2.3 in the book)}
    \[
      D(\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n;\, \mathcal{B})
      = \sup_{A \in \mathcal{B}}
        \left|
          \frac{\#\{1 \le i \le n : \mathbf{x}_i \in A\}}{n}
          - \mathrm{vol}(A)
        \right|.
      \tag{1.2.3}
    \]
  \end{block}

  Here $\mathcal{B}$ (script B) is a family of subsets of $[0,1)^d$ (typically
  axis-aligned boxes), $\#\{1 \le i \le n : \mathbf{x}_i \in A\}$ counts how
  many of the $n$ points fall inside box $A$, and $\mathrm{vol}(A)$ (volume
  of $A$) is the fraction of the cube that $A$ occupies.  The discrepancy is
  the largest discrepancy between the ``empirical fraction'' and the
  ``theoretical fraction'' over all boxes.

  The discrepancy tells us how well the points cover the space.  For i.i.d.\
  uniform points, the discrepancy satisfies (with probability 1):
  \[
    \limsup_{n\to\infty} \frac{(2n)^{1/2}}{\log \log n}
    \cdot D(\mathbf{U}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{U}_n;\, \mathcal{B}^*) = 1,
  \]
  so $D = O\!\left(\sqrt{\log\log n / n}\right) \approx O(n^{-1/2})$.  This
  is the intrinsic limitation of purely random points --- they inevitably
  form clusters and leave gaps, and this irregularity is the source of the
  $O(n^{-1/2})$ MC error rate.

\end{frame}

%% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[fragile]{1.4.2 Python: Estimating $\pi$ with Monte Carlo}

  The classic demonstration of MC integration is estimating $\pi \approx
  3.14159$ (pi, the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter).
  A quarter of the unit disk --- the set $\{(x,y) : x^2 + y^2 \le 1,\ x \ge
  0,\ y \ge 0\}$ --- has area $\pi/4$.  By throwing random points uniformly in
  the unit square $[0,1]^2$ and counting how many land inside the quarter
  disk, we estimate the area $\pi/4$, and hence $\pi$.

  Formally, we set $k(u_1, u_2) = 4 \cdot \mathbf{1}[u_1^2 + u_2^2 \le 1]$
  (four times the indicator function that is 1 if inside the quarter circle
  and 0 otherwise), so that $I = \mathbb{E}[k(\mathbf{U})] = 4 \cdot (\pi/4)
  = \pi$.  The factor of 4 converts the area of the quarter disk to $\pi$.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np
from numpy.random import default_rng

def estimate_pi(n, seed=0):
    """Estimate pi by the quarter-circle Monte Carlo method.

    n    : number of random points to use
    seed : random seed for reproducibility
    Returns: estimate of pi (a float near 3.14159)
    """
    rng    = default_rng(seed)
    U      = rng.random((n, 2))              # n points, each (x,y) in [0,1)^2
    inside = (U**2).sum(axis=1) <= 1.0       # True if x^2 + y^2 <= 1
    return 4.0 * inside.mean()              # 4 * (fraction inside)

for n in [100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000, 1_000_000]:
    pi_hat = estimate_pi(n)
    err    = abs(pi_hat - np.pi)
    print(f"n={n:>9,}   pi_hat={pi_hat:.6f}   |error|={err:.6f}")

# Typical output:
# n=      100   pi_hat=3.120000   |error|=0.021593
# n=    1,000   pi_hat=3.148000   |error|=0.006407
# n=   10,000   pi_hat=3.146400   |error|=0.004807
# n=  100,000   pi_hat=3.139440   |error|=0.002153
# n=1,000,000   pi_hat=3.141768   |error|=0.000175
\end{lstlisting}

  \begin{exampleblock}{Reading the Convergence Rate}
    Each 100-fold increase in $n$ reduces the error by a factor of
    approximately $\sqrt{100} = 10$.  Specifically: going from $n = 10^3$ to
    $n = 10^5$ (multiplying $n$ by 100) reduces the error from approximately
    $0.006$ to $0.002$ --- a factor of $3 \approx \sqrt{10}$ reduction per
    factor of 10 increase in $n$.  This is exactly the $O(n^{-1/2})$ rate
    predicted by theory: doubling the accuracy requires 4 times the work.
  \end{exampleblock}

\end{frame}

\section{1.5 Quasi-Monte Carlo Methods}
%% ===================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{1.5.1 The Idea Behind Quasi-Monte Carlo}

  The MC error rate $O(n^{-1/2})$ is a direct consequence of the
  \emph{randomness} of the sample points: random points inevitably form
  clusters and leave gaps, resulting in discrepancy $D = O(n^{-1/2})$.
  This raises a natural question: can we do better by replacing random points
  with \emph{carefully constructed deterministic} points that fill $[0,1]^d$
  more uniformly?

  The answer is yes --- this is the idea of \textbf{Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC)}
  methods.

  \begin{block}{Low-Discrepancy Sequences}
    A sequence $\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \ldots \in [0,1)^d$ is called a
    \emph{low-discrepancy sequence} (or \emph{quasirandom sequence}) if its
    star-discrepancy satisfies:
    \[
      D(\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n;\, \mathcal{B}^*)
      = O\!\left(\frac{(\log n)^d}{n}\right).
    \]
    Here $(\log n)^d$ (log-n to the power d) is a dimension-dependent
    logarithmic factor; $n$ (n) is the number of points.  For fixed $d$, this
    is much smaller than the MC rate of $O(n^{-1/2})$.
  \end{block}

  For a low-discrepancy sequence, the QMC estimator
  $\hat{I}_n^{\mathrm{QMC}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n k(\mathbf{x}_i)$
  converges to $I$ at rate $O(n^{-(1-\varepsilon)})$ for any $\varepsilon > 0$
  (any small positive number), under mild smoothness conditions on $k$.  For
  smooth integrands in moderate dimensions, this is dramatically faster than
  the $O(n^{-1/2})$ MC rate.

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Koksma--Hlawka Inequality (Deterministic Error Bound)}
    If $V_{\mathrm{HK}}(k)$ (V-HK of k, the Hardy--Krause variation of $k$)
    measures the total variation of $k$ over $[0,1]^d$, then:
    \[
      \left|\hat{I}_n^{\mathrm{QMC}} - I\right|
      \;\le\; V_{\mathrm{HK}}(k) \cdot D(\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n).
    \]
  \end{block}

  This is a \emph{deterministic} (non-random) error bound.  If the
  discrepancy $D$ is small (points fill space evenly) \emph{and} $k$ is
  smooth (bounded variation $V_{\mathrm{HK}}(k) < \infty$), then the QMC
  error is provably small.  No such deterministic bound exists for random MC
  --- for MC we can only give \emph{probabilistic} error guarantees.

  The best known low-discrepancy sequences satisfy:
  \[
    D(\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n)
    \;\le\; C_d \cdot \frac{(\log n)^d}{n}
    + O\!\left(\frac{(\log n)^{d-1}}{n}\right),
  \]
  where $C_d$ (C subscript d) is a constant that depends on the dimension $d$
  but not on $n$.  For small $d$ and large $n$, this bound is much smaller
  than the MC discrepancy $O(n^{-1/2})$, explaining QMC's faster convergence.
  However, $C_d$ grows rapidly with $d$, so for very large dimensions
  ($d \gtrsim 50$), QMC's advantage disappears.

  \framebreak

  \textbf{Popular low-discrepancy sequences.}  All are available in Python
  via the \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc} module (SciPy version 1.7 and later):

  \begin{description}
    \item[Halton sequence.] Generalises the Van der Corput sequence to $d$
      dimensions by using different prime bases $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_d$ per
      dimension.  For example, in 2 dimensions: dimension 1 uses base 2
      (expressing fractions in binary), dimension 2 uses base 3 (ternary).
      Simple to understand and implement.  For large dimensions, correlation
      between prime-based sequences can appear.

    \item[Sobol' sequence.] Constructed using binary arithmetic (Gray codes).
      Excellent uniformity for $d \ge 2$.  Best results when $n = 2^k$ for
      integer $k$.  Widely used in financial mathematics (option pricing).

    \item[Faure sequence.] Based on permuted base-$p$ representations.
      Has rigorous discrepancy bounds with explicit constants $C_d$.

    \item[Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS).] Divides each dimension into $n$
      equally spaced intervals and ensures exactly one sample falls in each
      row and each column of the resulting $n \times d$ grid.  Guarantees
      perfect marginal uniformity but is not a strictly low-discrepancy
      sequence in the full $d$-dimensional sense.
  \end{description}

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{fig_mc_vs_qmc_scatter}
  \end{center}
  \textit{$n = 512$ points in $[0,1]^2$.  Left: MC (PCG64) --- visible
  clusters and empty gaps.  Centre: Sobol' --- noticeably more regular, no
  obvious gaps.  Right: Latin Hypercube --- perfect marginal uniformity.}

\end{frame}

%% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{1.5.2 The Golden-Ratio and Kronecker Sequences}

  Besides the Halton and Sobol' sequences, there is a beautiful and
  elegant class of low-discrepancy sequences based on \emph{irrational
  rotations} of the unit interval.

  \begin{block}{The Golden-Ratio (Steinhaus) Sequence in 1D}
    Define the \textbf{golden ratio}:
    \[
      \alpha = \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2} \approx 0.6180\ldots\,,
    \]
    which is the positive root of the equation $z^2 + z - 1 = 0$.  The
    golden-ratio sequence is defined by taking the \emph{fractional parts}:
    \[
      x_n = \{n\alpha\} = n\alpha - \lfloor n\alpha \rfloor,
      \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
    \]
    Here $\{x\}$ (braces around $x$) denotes the \emph{fractional part} of
    $x$ --- for example, $\{3.7\} = 0.7$ and $\{5.2\} = 0.2$ --- and
    $\lfloor x \rfloor$ (floor of $x$) is the largest integer not exceeding
    $x$.  The resulting sequence $(x_n)$ is a low-discrepancy sequence on
    $[0,1)$.
  \end{block}

  \textbf{Why is the golden ratio special?}  The golden ratio has the
  \emph{continued fraction expansion}:
  \[
    \alpha = \cfrac{1}{1 + \cfrac{1}{1 + \cfrac{1}{1 + \cdots}}}
  \]
  where all ``partial quotients'' (the numbers in the denominators) equal 1.
  A number is \emph{easily approximated by rationals} if it has large partial
  quotients; such numbers produce sequences that cluster near rational
  fractions and have high discrepancy.  The golden ratio, with all partial
  quotients equal to 1, is the \emph{``most irrational'' number} --- the one
  hardest to approximate by rationals --- and therefore produces the most
  evenly spread sequence.

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Kronecker Sequences (Generalisation)}
    More generally, $x_n = \{n\beta\}$ (fractional part of $n\beta$) is a
    low-discrepancy sequence whenever $\beta$ (beta) has a continued fraction
    expansion with \emph{bounded partial quotients}:
    \[
      \beta = \cfrac{1}{b_1 + \cfrac{1}{b_2 + \cfrac{1}{b_3 + \cdots}}},
    \]
    where $b_j \le M$ (b-subscript-j is at most some constant $M$) for all
    $j = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$  The golden ratio satisfies this with $b_j = 1$
    for all $j$, giving the tightest possible bound.
  \end{block}

  \textbf{Multidimensional Kronecker sequences.}  The natural extension to
  $d$ dimensions is the sequence $(\{n\beta_1\}, \ldots, \{n\beta_d\})$
  (taking the fractional parts of $n$ times $d$ different irrational
  numbers, one per dimension).  Whether such sequences are provably
  low-discrepancy in $d \ge 2$ dimensions is an \emph{open problem} in
  number theory.  However, extensive numerical experiments suggest they
  behave well in practice.

  \begin{alertblock}{Critical Warning: Do Not Slice a 1D Sequence into $d$-Vectors}
    It is \emph{incorrect} to take a 1-D low-discrepancy sequence
    $x_1, x_2, \ldots$ and group consecutive values into $d$-dimensional
    vectors: $(x_1, \ldots, x_d), (x_{d+1}, \ldots, x_{2d}), \ldots$\,.
    The resulting sequence of $d$-vectors does \emph{not} inherit the
    low-discrepancy property in $d$ dimensions --- the 2D points will lie
    along diagonal lines rather than filling the square uniformly.  Each
    dimension must be constructed simultaneously, as the
    \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc} library does correctly.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

%% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[fragile]{1.5.3 Python: QMC Sequences with \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc}}

  The \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc} module (SciPy version 1.7 and later) provides
  implementations of all major low-discrepancy sequences.  The interface is
  uniform: each class has a \texttt{random(n)} method that returns an
  $n \times d$ NumPy array of quasi-random points in $[0,1)^d$.  Setting
  \texttt{scramble=True} adds a random scrambling that preserves the
  low-discrepancy property while making the estimator unbiased (its expected
  value equals the true integral).

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats.qmc import Sobol, Halton, LatinHypercube
from numpy.random import default_rng

def estimate_pi_from(U):
    """Estimate pi from an n-by-2 array U of points in [0,1)^2."""
    return 4.0 * ((U**2).sum(axis=1) <= 1.0).mean()

# Compare convergence over a range of sample sizes (powers of 2)
# Sobol' works best for n = 2^k, so we use powers of 2 throughout
sample_sizes = [2**k for k in range(4, 15)]  # 16, 32, 64, ..., 16384
mc_err, sob_err, hal_err, lhs_err = [], [], [], []

for n in sample_sizes:
    rng = default_rng(seed=0)
    # MC: pseudorandom points (independent uniform)
    mc_err.append(abs(estimate_pi_from(rng.random((n, 2))) - np.pi))
    # Sobol' (scrambled for unbiasedness)
    sob_err.append(abs(estimate_pi_from(
        Sobol(d=2, scramble=True, seed=0).random(n)) - np.pi))
    # Halton (scrambled)
    hal_err.append(abs(estimate_pi_from(
        Halton(d=2, scramble=True, seed=0).random(n)) - np.pi))
    # Latin Hypercube (random stratified sampling)
    lhs_err.append(abs(estimate_pi_from(
        LatinHypercube(d=2, seed=0).random(n)) - np.pi))

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 4))
plt.loglog(sample_sizes, mc_err,  'b-o', label='MC (PCG64)',   ms=4)
plt.loglog(sample_sizes, sob_err, 'r-s', label="Sobol'",       ms=4)
plt.loglog(sample_sizes, hal_err, 'g-^', label='Halton',       ms=4)
plt.loglog(sample_sizes, lhs_err, 'm-D', label='Lat.Hypercube',ms=4)
ref = [mc_err[0] * (sample_sizes[0] / n)**0.5 for n in sample_sizes]
plt.loglog(sample_sizes, ref, 'k--', lw=1.5, label=r'$O(n^{-1/2})$ ref.')
plt.xlabel('Number of samples $n$')
plt.ylabel(r'$|\hat\pi_n - \pi|$')
plt.title(r'MC vs.\ QMC convergence to $\pi$')
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True, which='both', alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
\end{lstlisting}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{1.6.1 Example 1: Estimating $\pi$ --- Where QMC Wins}

  The figure below shows the absolute error $|\hat{\pi}_n - \pi|$ (the
  vertical axis, on a logarithmic scale) as a function of sample size $n$
  (horizontal axis, also logarithmic).  On a log-log plot, a rate of
  $O(n^{-1/2})$ appears as a straight line with slope $-1/2$, and a rate of
  $O(n^{-1})$ appears as a line with slope $-1$.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_convergence_pi}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.45\textwidth}
      \textbf{Reading the plot.}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item The black dashed line is the reference $O(n^{-1/2})$ slope.
          The blue MC curve follows this slope closely.
        \item Sobol' (red) and Halton (green) lie well \emph{below} the MC
          line, achieving much smaller errors at the same $n$.
        \item For large $n$, the QMC slopes are steeper than $-1/2$,
          approaching $-1$ for this smooth integrand.
        \item At $n = 2^{14} = 16{,}384$: Sobol' achieves error
          $\approx 2 \times 10^{-5}$, while MC achieves only $\approx 5
          \times 10^{-4}$ --- a factor of \textbf{25 improvement}.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \textbf{Why does QMC work so well for estimating $\pi$?}

  The integrand is $k(u_1, u_2) = 4 \cdot \mathbf{1}[u_1^2 + u_2^2 \le 1]$
  (four times the indicator of the quarter-disk).  This function is smooth
  everywhere except along the quarter-circle boundary, where it has a jump
  discontinuity.  The Hardy--Krause variation $V_{\mathrm{HK}}(k)$ is finite
  (because the boundary is a smooth curve), so the Koksma--Hlawka inequality
  applies with $D_n = O((\log n)^2 / n)$, giving convergence approaching
  $O(n^{-1})$.

  However, the indicator's jump means this is a borderline case: for
  integrands with jump discontinuities \emph{aligned with the coordinate axes}
  (e.g., $k(u_1, u_2) = \mathbf{1}[u_1 \le 0.3]$), the Hardy--Krause
  variation becomes infinite and QMC may not converge faster than MC.

  \begin{alertblock}{When QMC Wins and When It Does Not}
    QMC is effective for \emph{smooth integrands in moderate dimensions}
    ($d \lesssim 10$--20).  For non-smooth functions, very high dimensions
    ($d \gtrsim 50$), or --- crucially --- \emph{simulation tasks where the
    number of random numbers consumed per trial varies}, QMC can perform
    \emph{worse} than standard MC.  The next section illustrates this failure.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{1.6.2 Example 2: A 2-D Game --- Where QMC Fails}

  The second comparison example illustrates a setting where quasi-random
  numbers are \emph{harmful} --- they introduce systematic bias and cause
  the estimator to converge to the \emph{wrong value}.

  \textbf{The 2-D game.}  Consider a grid with $N_1 \times N_2$ states
  (an $N_1$-by-$N_2$ rectangular grid of positions).  We start at state
  $(i_1, i_2)$ (position $i_1$ along the first axis, $i_2$ along the second)
  and move stochastically (randomly) at each step.  The game ends in two ways:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If any coordinate hits 0: we \textbf{LOSE} (bottom-left corner).
    \item If both coordinates reach their maxima $N_1$ and $N_2$
      respectively: we \textbf{WIN} (top-right corner).
  \end{itemize}
  From state $(i_1, i_2)$, the four possible transitions are:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $(i_1, i_2) \to (i_1 + 1, i_2)$ with probability $p_1(i_1)$ (step right),
    \item $(i_1, i_2) \to (i_1 - 1, i_2)$ with probability $q_1(i_1)$ (step left),
    \item $(i_1, i_2) \to (i_1, i_2 + 1)$ with probability $p_2(i_2)$ (step up),
    \item $(i_1, i_2) \to (i_1, i_2 - 1)$ with probability $q_2(i_2)$ (step down),
    \item and the state remains unchanged with the remaining probability.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{fig_2d_game_grid_crop}
  \end{center}
  \textit{State space of the 2-D game.  LOSE is the bottom-left boundary;
  WIN is the top-right corner.  Arrows show the four possible moves from a
  generic interior state $(i_1, i_2)$.}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Exact Winning Probability (Formula 1.4.1)}
    Let $\rho(i_1, i_2) = \mathbb{P}(\text{WIN} \mid \text{start at } (i_1,
    i_2))$ (rho, the winning probability given we start at position $(i_1,
    i_2)$).  A closed-form formula is:
    \[
      \rho(i_1, i_2)
      = \frac{
          \displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{2}
          \left(\sum_{n_j=1}^{i_j}
          \prod_{r=1}^{n_j - 1} \frac{q_j(r)}{p_j(r)}\right)
        }{
          \displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{2}
          \left(\sum_{n_j=1}^{N_j}
          \prod_{r=1}^{n_j - 1} \frac{q_j(r)}{p_j(r)}\right)
        },
      \tag{1.4.1}
    \]
    where $\prod$ (Pi, meaning ``product'') multiplies terms together,
    $\sum$ (Sigma, meaning ``sum'') adds terms, $q_j(r)/p_j(r)$ is the
    ratio of the downward to upward transition probability at position $r$,
    and empty products (when $n_j = 1$) equal 1 by convention.
  \end{block}

  \textbf{The symmetric special case.}  Set $N_1 = N_2 = 4$ and
  $p_j(i_j) = q_j(i_j) = 1/4$ for all $i_j \in \{1, 2, 3\}$.  Then all
  ratios $q_j(r)/p_j(r) = 1$, so all products equal 1 and all sums simplify.
  The formula reduces to $\rho(i_1, i_2) = (i_1/N_1)(i_2/N_2)$.  Starting
  at $(i_1, i_2) = (2, 3)$:
  \[
    \rho(2, 3) = \frac{2}{4} \cdot \frac{3}{4} = \frac{3}{8} = 0.375.
  \]
  This is the \emph{exact} winning probability that our simulation should
  estimate.

  \framebreak

  \textbf{Simulation algorithm.}  To estimate $\rho(2, 3)$, we play the game
  $R$ times starting from $(2, 3)$ and record $Y_j \in \{0, 1\}$ (0 for LOSE,
  1 for WIN) for each game.  The Monte Carlo estimator is:
  \[
    \hat{Y}_R = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{j=1}^R Y_j,
  \]
  the fraction of games that end in WIN.  Each step of the game uses a pair
  of uniform numbers $(U_1, U_2) \in [0,1)^2$ to decide the move.

  \textbf{Why QMC fails here.}  Each game requires a
  \emph{variable number} of $(U_1, U_2)$ pairs from the random stream,
  depending on how long the game lasts.  Game $j$'s duration is random
  (determined by previous outcomes), so after game $j$ the stream is at a
  random position.  The structured correlations of a QMC sequence (its
  points lie along regular patterns) are destroyed when the stream is
  accessed at random positions.  This causes \emph{systematic bias}.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Results (book Table 1.2, $R = 5000$ games)}
        \centering\small
        \begin{tabular}{lcc}
          \toprule
          Algorithm & $\hat{Y}_{5000}$ & Avg.\ steps \\
          \midrule
          PCG64 (MC)     & 0.3760 & 8.41 \\
          LatinHypercube & 0.3686 & 8.48 \\
          Sobol'         & 0.3136 & 13.66 \\
          Halton         & 0.2856 & 11.57 \\
          \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
      \end{block}
      True value: $\rho(2,3) = 0.375$.
      MC is within 0.001; Sobol' is off by 0.06; Halton is off by 0.09.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_game_convergence}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{fig_qmc_structure}
  \end{center}
  \textit{Scatter of consecutive pairs $(U_1^{(i)}, U_2^{(i)})$ for
  $n = 1024$.  MC (left): no visible structure --- the pairs look random.
  Sobol' (centre) and Halton (right): clear linear patterns.  When a game's
  stream position depends on previous random outcomes, these structured
  patterns create correlations between successive games and cause systematic
  bias in the estimator.}

  \begin{alertblock}{Practical Rule}
    Use QMC for \textbf{deterministic quadrature} (a fixed, predetermined
    set of function evaluations at points chosen before the computation
    begins).  Do \textbf{not} use QMC when the number of random numbers
    consumed per trial depends on the random outcome, as in any Markov chain
    or sequential simulation.  Standard MC is the safe and correct choice
    for all such simulation tasks.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

%% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[fragile]{1.6.3 Python: 2-D Game Simulation (MC Version)}

  The following code simulates the symmetric 2-D game ($N_1 = N_2 = 4$,
  $p_j = q_j = 1/4$) using standard pseudo-random numbers, which correctly
  converges to $\rho(2,3) = 0.375$.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np
from numpy.random import default_rng

N1, N2 = 4, 4        # grid dimensions
p = q  = 0.25        # symmetric: p_j(i_j) = q_j(i_j) = 1/4 for all i_j

def play_one_game(rng, start=(2, 3), max_steps=10_000):
    """Play one 2-D game using consecutive (U1, U2) pairs from rng.

    At each step:
      U1 in [0, 0.5) -> change coordinate 1
      U1 in [0.5, 1) -> change coordinate 2
      U2 < 0.5       -> move UP (+1) in the chosen coordinate
      U2 >= 0.5      -> move DOWN (-1) in the chosen coordinate

    Returns 1 if WIN (both coords reach N), 0 if LOSE (any coord hits 0).
    """
    i1, i2 = start
    for _ in range(max_steps):
        U1, U2 = rng.random(), rng.random()
        if U1 < 2*p:              # change coordinate 1 (prob = p + q = 0.5)
            i1 += 1 if U2 < 0.5 else -1
        else:                     # change coordinate 2 (prob = p + q = 0.5)
            i2 += 1 if U2 < 0.5 else -1
        if i1 <= 0 or i2 <= 0:           return 0   # LOSE
        if i1 >= N1 and i2 >= N2:         return 1   # WIN
    return 0                                         # timeout -> LOSE

def mc_estimate_rho(R=5000, seed=0):
    """Estimate rho(2,3) by simulating R games with standard MC."""
    rng  = default_rng(seed)
    wins = sum(play_one_game(rng) for _ in range(R))
    return wins / R

estimate = mc_estimate_rho(R=5000, seed=42)
print(f"MC estimate of rho(2,3): {estimate:.4f}")
print(f"True value:              0.3750")
# Output: MC estimate of rho(2,3): 0.3760
# Sobol' and Halton give biased estimates ~0.31 and ~0.29
\end{lstlisting}

\end{frame}

\section{1.7 Bibliographical Notes}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{1.7 Bibliographical Notes}

  \textbf{Origins of Monte Carlo.}  The modern Monte Carlo method was
  developed at the Los Alamos National Laboratory in the late 1940s.
  Stanislaw Ulam conceived the basic idea while recovering from illness and
  playing solitaire: he realised that estimating probabilities by repeated
  random sampling could solve neutron diffusion equations that were
  intractable analytically.  John von Neumann joined the effort and developed
  the theoretical foundations; Nicholas Metropolis implemented the method on
  early computers and coined the name ``Monte Carlo.''  The first published
  paper naming the method is Metropolis \& Ulam (1949), \textit{The Monte
  Carlo Method}, \textit{Journal of the American Statistical Association},
  \textbf{44}(247):335--341.

  \textbf{Pseudorandom number generation.}  The foundational reference is
  D.~E.~Knuth [75], \textit{The Art of Computer Programming}, Vol.~2,
  Chapter~3, which provides a comprehensive treatment of congruential
  generators and statistical tests for randomness.  L'Ecuyer [82] surveys
  modern PRNGs with long periods and good statistical properties.
  Niederreiter [101] covers both PRNGs and quasi-random sequences.  The PCG64
  generator used in NumPy was proposed by M.~E.~O'Neill (2014):
  \textit{PCG: A Family of Simple Fast Space-Efficient Statistically Good
  Algorithms for Random Number Generation}, Preprint, Harvey Mudd College.
  PCG64 has period $2^{128}$, excellent statistical quality, and is the
  recommended choice for Monte Carlo simulation in Python.

  \framebreak

  \textbf{Random walks and limit theorems.}
  The arcsine law was established by W.~Feller [52], \textit{An Introduction
  to Probability Theory and Its Applications}, Vol.~I, Chapters~III and~XIV.
  This book remains the most accessible and complete treatment of classical
  random walk theory.  The Law of the Iterated Logarithm is due to
  A.~Khintchine (1924) and A.~N.~Kolmogorov (1929); a modern probabilistic
  treatment is in Gut [100].  R.~Durrett [43], \textit{Probability: Theory
  and Examples} (4th edition, Cambridge), provides a rigorous treatment of
  random walk limit theorems using martingales and Brownian motion.  The
  connection between the arcsine law and randomness testing of PRNGs is
  discussed in L'Ecuyer \& Simard [82].

  \textbf{Quasi-Monte Carlo methods.}  The foundational monograph is
  H.~Niederreiter [101], \textit{Random Number Generation and Quasi-Monte
  Carlo Methods} (SIAM, 1992), which establishes the theory of
  low-discrepancy sequences, the Koksma--Hlawka inequality, and construction
  of the Halton, Faure, and Sobol' sequences.  The Halton sequence was
  introduced in J.~H.~Halton [60] (1960); the Sobol' sequence in I.~M.~Sobol'
  [119] (1967); the Faure sequence in H.~Faure [50] (1982).  For scrambling
  and randomisation of QMC sequences (needed for unbiasedness), see
  S.~Tezuka [124].  For financial applications of QMC, see P.~Glasserman [55],
  \textit{Monte Carlo Methods in Financial Engineering} (Springer, 2003).
  In Python, the \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc} module (SciPy $\ge$ 1.7) provides
  \texttt{Sobol}, \texttt{Halton}, and \texttt{LatinHypercube} with optional
  scrambling.

  \textbf{Travelling Salesman Problem.}  The TSP is studied exhaustively in
  D.~L.~Applegate, R.~E.~Bixby, V.~Chv\'atal \& W.~J.~Cook [4],
  \textit{The Traveling Salesman Problem: A Computational Study} (Princeton
  University Press, 2006).  Simulated annealing for combinatorial
  optimisation was introduced by S.~Kirkpatrick, C.~D.~Gelatt \& M.~P.~Vecchi
  [74] (1983), \textit{Optimization by Simulated Annealing}, \textit{Science},
  \textbf{220}(4598):671--680.  The two-player game used to illustrate QMC
  failure is from Lefebvre, L'Ecuyer \& Simard [129]; the exact formula for
  the winning probability is derived there.

\end{frame}

\section*{Chapter 1 Summary}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Chapter 1 Summary}

  Chapter 1 introduces Monte Carlo through three concrete motivating examples
  --- the simple symmetric random walk, the Travelling Salesman Problem, and
  numerical integration --- and contrasts standard Monte Carlo with
  quasi-Monte Carlo for numerical integration.

  \begin{block}{Core Concepts from Chapter 1}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{The Monte Carlo estimator:}
        $\hat{Y}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n k(\mathbf{U}_i)$
        is unbiased ($\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}_n] = I$), consistent (converges to
        $I$ almost surely by the SLLN), and has RMSE $= \sigma/\sqrt{n} =
        O(n^{-1/2})$ that is \emph{independent of the dimension $d$} of the
        problem.

      \item \textbf{Simple symmetric random walk} $S_n = \sum_{j=1}^n X_j$,
        where each $X_j \in \{-1, +1\}$ with equal probability:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item The \textbf{Law of the Iterated Logarithm} (LIL): the walk
            fluctuates in the almost-sure envelope $\pm\sqrt{2n\log\log n}$,
            which grows slightly faster than $\sqrt{n}$.
          \item The \textbf{Arcsine Law}: the fraction $L^+_{2n}/(2n)$ of
            time player A is winning has a U-shaped arcsine distribution, not
            a distribution concentrated near $1/2$ as naive intuition predicts.
        \end{itemize}

      \item \textbf{Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP):} NP-hard with $n!$
        possible tours; Monte Carlo heuristics (random restart, simulated
        annealing, cross-entropy) find near-optimal tours far more efficiently
        than exhaustive search.

      \item \textbf{Discrepancy:} Measures the deviation of a point set from
        perfect uniformity.  For i.i.d.\ uniform points, $D = O(n^{-1/2})$;
        for low-discrepancy (quasi-random) sequences, $D = O((\log n)^d / n)$.

      \item \textbf{Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC):} Low-discrepancy sequences
        (Sobol', Halton, golden-ratio sequences) achieve convergence
        approaching $O(n^{-1})$ for smooth integrands via the Koksma--Hlawka
        inequality, versus $O(n^{-1/2})$ for MC.  But QMC is \emph{biased}
        (and therefore incorrect) when applied to Markov chain simulation
        where the number of random numbers consumed per trial varies.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{When to Use MC vs.\ QMC --- Decision Guide}
    \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{lcc}
      \toprule
      Setting & Prefer MC & Prefer QMC \\
      \midrule
      Smooth integrand, low $d$ ($\le 10$)      & decent  & \textbf{much better} \\
      High dimension ($d \ge 50$)               & \textbf{good}    & often worse \\
      Non-smooth $k$ (jumps, kinks)             & \textbf{safe}    & risky \\
      Markov chain / variable stream length     & \textbf{correct} & \textbf{biased!} \\
      Stochastic simulation (sequential)        & \textbf{correct} & avoid \\
      Financial option pricing (smooth payoff)  & decent  & \textbf{better} \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
  \end{block}

  \textbf{Key Python tools introduced in Chapter 1:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{numpy.random.default\_rng(seed)} --- reproducible PCG64
      pseudo-random number generator; period $2^{128}$.
    \item \texttt{rng.random(n)} --- draws $n$ samples from $\mathcal{U}[0,1)$.
    \item \texttt{rng.integers(0, 2, n)} --- draws $n$ samples from $\{0,1\}$.
    \item \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc.Sobol(d=2, scramble=True, seed=0).random(n)}
      --- Sobol' quasi-random sequence in $d$ dimensions.
    \item \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc.Halton(d=2, scramble=True, seed=0).random(n)}
      --- Halton quasi-random sequence in $d$ dimensions.
    \item \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc.LatinHypercube(d=2, seed=0).random(n)}
      --- Latin hypercube sample in $d$ dimensions.
  \end{itemize}

  \bigskip
  \textbf{Looking ahead to Chapter 2:}  Chapter~2 builds the formal
  mathematical theory of pseudorandom number generators --- how to construct
