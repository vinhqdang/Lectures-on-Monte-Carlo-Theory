\section{8.1 Preliminaries}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.1 Preliminaries -- What Is a Queueing System?}

Simulation methods are indispensable for analyzing complex stochastic systems.
While mathematical models provide powerful abstractions for queueing networks or
telecommunication systems, closed-form solutions are often unavailable due to
real-world complexity.  Simulation therefore becomes the critical tool that enables
practitioners to study system behavior under various configurations and constraints.

This chapter focuses on the simulation of queueing models and related systems,
offering both theoretical insights and practical implementations.  Queueing systems
appear in a vast range of real-world applications: call center optimization,
inventory management, manufacturing lines, and telecommunications.  By simulating
these systems we can evaluate performance characteristics such as average waiting
times, system utilization, and throughput, even in cases where analytical solutions
are completely intractable.

\begin{block}{The General Model: Tasks, Arrivals, and Service}
A ``system'' is characterized by two ingredients:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tasks} (also called jobs, customers, packets, or calls) that arrive
        at the system according to some random mechanism.
  \item A \textbf{processing stage} in which tasks are served and eventually
        leave the system.
\end{itemize}
The quantity of interest is typically a \emph{performance characteristic} such
as the number of tasks currently waiting, the average time a task spends in the
system, or the fraction of time a server is busy.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\textbf{Two ways to track performance.}  Performance characteristics can be
observed in two complementary ways, depending on whether we index by customer
number or by continuous time.

First, one may observe a \emph{discrete-time sequence}: for example, the waiting
time $W_i$ (W sub i) of the $i$-th arriving task gives a sequence
$W_1, W_2, \ldots$ indexed by customer number.  Each $W_i$ is the time that
customer number $i$ must wait in the queue before service begins.

Second, one may observe a \emph{continuous-time process}: for example, the total
number of tasks $L(t)$ (L of t) present in the system at time $t$ is a
right-continuous step process that jumps up by 1 at each arrival and down by 1
at each departure.

\begin{block}{Time Average vs.\ Ensemble Average}
Two fundamentally different averages arise in simulation:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{time average} of a process $X(t)$ over the interval $[0,t]$:
        \[
          \hat{X}(t) = \frac{1}{t}\int_0^t X(s)\,ds.
        \]
        Here $X(s)$ is the process value at time $s$, and $\hat{X}(t)$
        (X-hat of t) is its running average up to time $t$.  This is what a
        \emph{single long simulation run} produces.
  \item The \textbf{ensemble average} at time $t$ is $\E[X(t)]$ (E of X of t),
        the expected value computed by averaging over all possible random
        trajectories.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

The central question of simulation output analysis is: \emph{when does
$\hat{X}(t)$ converge to a meaningful steady-state quantity as $t\to\infty$?}
The answer depends on whether the process is \emph{ergodically stable} -- a
concept developed in Section~8.7.

\begin{alertblock}{Key Takeaway}
The goal of steady-state simulation is to estimate $\E[X^*]$ (the stationary
mean) by running the system long enough that $\hat{X}(t)$ is a reliable proxy
for it.  How long is ``long enough'' is governed by the \emph{time-average
variance constant} $\varsigma^2$ (varsigma squared, also called TAVC), which
quantifies how strongly the process is autocorrelated over time.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{8.2 Modeling Arrival Streams}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.2 Modeling Arrival Streams -- Counting Processes}

Many queueing problems require modeling entities arriving at a system randomly.
We use \emph{counting processes} to formalize this.

\begin{block}{Counting Process $N(t)$}
A \textbf{counting process} $N(t)$, $t \ge 0$, records the total number of
arrivals up to time $t$ (not including time 0).  Formally:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $N(0) = 0$ (no arrivals before time zero).
  \item $N(t)$ is non-decreasing and right-continuous with unit upward jumps.
\end{itemize}
The notation $N(s,t] := N(t) - N(s)$ denotes the number of arrivals in the
half-open interval $(s,t]$.  Equivalently we can describe arrivals through the
sequence of \textbf{arrival times} $0 < A_1 < A_2 < \cdots$, giving:
\[
  N(t) = \#\{i \ge 1 : A_i \le t\}, \qquad
  \tau_1 = A_1,\quad \tau_i = A_i - A_{i-1} \;\;(i \ge 2).
\]
Here $\tau_i$ (tau sub i) is the $i$-th \textbf{inter-arrival time}, the waiting
time between consecutive arrivals.
\end{block}

The most important special case is the \textbf{Poisson process}, which arises
when inter-arrival times are independent and exponentially distributed.  The
exponential distribution $\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$ (Exp lambda, with rate
$\lambda$ > 0) has mean $1/\lambda$ and the \emph{memoryless property}: the
remaining time until the next arrival does not depend on how long we have
already waited.  Here $\lambda$ (lambda) is the \textbf{rate parameter},
measuring arrivals per unit time.

\end{frame}

% ── 8.2.1 ────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{8.2.1 Homogeneous Poisson Process}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.2.1 Homogeneous Poisson Process}

The \textbf{homogeneous Poisson process} is the most fundamental arrival model.
``Homogeneous'' means the arrival rate is constant over time.

\begin{block}{Definition 8.2.1 -- Homogeneous Poisson Process}
A counting process $N(t)$ is a \textbf{homogeneous Poisson process} with
\textbf{intensity} $\lambda > 0$ if its inter-arrival times
$\tau_1, \tau_2, \ldots$ are i.i.d.\ (independent and identically distributed)
random variables with distribution $\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$.
\end{block}

Arrivals occur at a constant average rate of $\lambda$ per unit time, and the
time until the next arrival is always exponentially distributed regardless of
the history -- this is the memoryless property.

\begin{block}{Proposition 8.2.2 -- Equivalent Characterization}
A counting process $N(t)$ is a Poisson process with intensity $\lambda$ if and
only if:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Numbers of arrivals in \emph{disjoint} time intervals are \emph{independent}.
  \item For every $t \ge 0$ and $h > 0$: $N(t, t+h] \sim \mathrm{Poi}(\lambda h)$,
        meaning $N(t, t+h]$ follows a Poisson distribution with mean $\lambda h$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}

From this, a useful approximation for small intervals $h$ is:
\[
  \Prob(N(t, t+h] = 1) = \lambda h\,e^{-\lambda h} \approx \lambda h,
\]
so the probability of exactly one arrival in a tiny window is approximately
$\lambda h$.  This is why $\lambda$ (lambda) is called the \textbf{intensity}
or \textbf{arrival rate}.

Furthermore, given that exactly $n$ arrivals occurred in $[0,t]$, their positions
are i.i.d.\ uniform on $[0,t]$.  This \emph{order-statistics property} leads to
a second simulation algorithm.

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Algorithm 73 -- PoiPro($\lambda$): Inter-arrival Method}
Simulate a Poisson process on $[0, t_{\max}]$ by generating one inter-arrival
time at a time.  Here $t_{\max}$ (t max) is the simulation horizon.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Create an empty list \texttt{A}.
  \item Draw $U \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$ (U uniform on 0 to 1); set
        $t = -\log(U)/\lambda$, giving $\tau_1 \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$.
  \item \textbf{While} $t \le t_{\max}$: append $t$ to \texttt{A};
        draw $U \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$; set $t \leftarrow t - \log(U)/\lambda$.
  \item Return \texttt{A}.
\end{enumerate}
The transformation $-\log(U)/\lambda$ converts a uniform random variable to an
exponential, using the inverse-CDF method.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Algorithm 74 -- PoiProSorted($\lambda$): Order-Statistics Method}
Simulate all arrivals simultaneously, using the uniform distribution.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Sample $K \sim \mathrm{Poi}(\lambda\,t_{\max})$.  Here $K$ is the
        total number of arrivals, with mean $\lambda t_{\max}$.
  \item Generate $K$ i.i.d.\ uniform random variables on $[0, t_{\max}]$.
  \item Sort and return them as $(A_{(1)} \le \cdots \le A_{(K)})$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}

Both algorithms produce the same distribution.  Algorithm 73 is a sequential
loop, suitable when we do not know $K$ in advance.  Algorithm 74 generates all
arrivals at once, which is often faster when $\lambda t_{\max}$ is large.

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Poisson Process}
A larger $\lambda$ means more frequent arrivals.  The number of arrivals in any
fixed interval of length $h$ is always Poisson with mean $\lambda h$, regardless
of what happened before -- this is independent increments.  Real examples:
phone calls to a call center, particle emissions in a radiation detector,
customer arrivals at a supermarket.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Python: Homogeneous Poisson Process (Algorithms 73 \& 74)}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np

def simulate_poisson(lam, t_max, rng=None):
    """Algorithm PoiPro: simulate Poisson(lambda) on [0, t_max]."""
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng()
    arrivals = []
    t = -np.log(rng.uniform()) / lam       # first inter-arrival ~ Exp(lam)
    while t <= t_max:
        arrivals.append(t)
        t += -np.log(rng.uniform()) / lam  # next inter-arrival
    return np.array(arrivals)

def simulate_poisson_sorted(lam, t_max, rng=None):
    """Algorithm PoiProSorted: simulate via order-statistics."""
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng()
    K = rng.poisson(lam * t_max)           # total number of arrivals
    arrivals = rng.uniform(0, t_max, size=K)
    return np.sort(arrivals)

# Example: lambda=2, simulate on [0,10]; expected ~20 arrivals
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
A = simulate_poisson(lam=2.0, t_max=10.0, rng=rng)
print(f"Inter-arrival method: {len(A)} arrivals")  # expect ~20
B = simulate_poisson_sorted(lam=2.0, t_max=10.0, rng=rng)
print(f"Sorted method:        {len(B)} arrivals")
\end{lstlisting}
\end{frame}

\subsection{8.2.2 Non-homogeneous Poisson Process}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.2.2 Non-homogeneous Poisson Process (NHPP)}

A homogeneous Poisson process has the same arrival rate $\lambda$ at all times.
This is inadequate for many real-world systems: call centers have peak hours,
retail stores have rush periods, and network traffic varies throughout the day.
A \textbf{non-homogeneous Poisson process} (NHPP) generalizes this by allowing
the intensity to be a function of time, $\lambda(t)$.

\begin{block}{Definition 8.2.3 -- Non-homogeneous Poisson Process}
A counting process $N(t)$ is an \textbf{NHPP} with \textbf{intensity function}
$\lambda(t) \ge 0$ if:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Numbers of arrivals in disjoint intervals are independent.
  \item $N(s, t]$ has a Poisson distribution with mean $\int_s^t\lambda(u)\,du$
        (integral of lambda from $s$ to $t$).
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}

Here $\lambda(t)$ (lambda of t) is the local arrival rate at time $t$: the
expected number of arrivals in a tiny window $[t, t+dt]$ equals $\lambda(t)\,dt$.
When $\lambda(t) = \lambda$ is constant, the NHPP reduces to the homogeneous case.

\framebreak

\textbf{Thinning Algorithm (Lewis-Shedler).}  The main simulation technique for
NHPPs is \textbf{thinning}.  The idea is: first generate arrivals at the maximum
possible rate $\lambda_{\max}$ (an upper bound on $\lambda(t)$), then randomly
\emph{discard} each proposed arrival with probability proportional to how much
the true rate falls short of the maximum.

\begin{block}{Proposition 8.2.4 -- Thinning}
Let $\lambda_{\max} \ge \lambda(t)$ for all $t \in (0, t_{\max})$.  Generate a
homogeneous Poisson process at rate $\lambda_{\max}$ to produce candidate
arrivals.  Retain each candidate arrival at time $A_i$ independently with
probability
\[
  p(A_i) = \frac{\lambda(A_i)}{\lambda_{\max}}.
\]
Here $p(A_i)$ (p of A sub i) is the \textbf{acceptance probability} at time $A_i$,
and $\lambda_{\max}$ (lambda max) is the upper bound on the intensity.  The
retained process is an NHPP with intensity $\lambda(t)$.
\end{block}

At times when $\lambda(t) = \lambda_{\max}$, every candidate is accepted
($p=1$); at quiet times where $\lambda(t) = 0.1\,\lambda_{\max}$, only 10\%
are kept.

\begin{block}{Algorithm 75 -- NH-Poi-thinning($\lambda(t)$)}
\textbf{Require:} $\lambda_{\max} = \max_{t \in [0,t_{\max}]}\lambda(t)$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Create empty list \texttt{A}; draw $U \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$;
        set $t = -\log(U)/\lambda_{\max}$.
  \item \textbf{While} $t \le t_{\max}$: compute $p = \lambda(t)/\lambda_{\max}$;
        draw $U_1 \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$; if $U_1 \le p$, append $t$ to \texttt{A};
        draw $U_2 \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$ and set $t \leftarrow t -
        \log(U_2)/\lambda_{\max}$.
  \item Return \texttt{A}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}

\begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{nhpp_thinning}
\end{center}

\framebreak

\textbf{Inversion Method (Algorithm 76).}  An alternative approach uses the
\emph{integrated intensity function} $h(t) = \int_0^t \lambda(s)\,ds$ (h of t,
the cumulative intensity up to time $t$).

\begin{block}{Algorithm 76 -- NH-Poi($\lambda(t)$): Inversion Method}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Sample $K \sim \mathrm{Poi}\!\left(h(t_{\max})\right)$, where
        $h(t_{\max}) = \int_0^{t_{\max}}\lambda(s)\,ds$.
  \item Compute the c.d.f.\ $F(s) = h(s)/h(t_{\max})$ and its generalized
        inverse $F^{\leftarrow}$ (the quantile function).
  \item Draw $U_1,\ldots,U_K$ i.i.d.\ $\mathcal{U}(0,1)$; set
        $A_i = F^{\leftarrow}(U_i)$.
  \item Return sorted values $(A_{(1)},\ldots,A_{(K)})$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}

Algorithm 76 requires computing $F^{\leftarrow}$ analytically or numerically,
which is extra work not needed by thinning (Algorithm 75).  The thinning approach
is therefore more widely used in practice.

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: Sinusoidal Call Center}
Suppose $\lambda(t) = 10(0.5 + 0.5\sin(2\pi t/24 - \pi/2))$ models a call
center with a 24-hour daily cycle.  At midnight ($t=0$): $\lambda(0) \approx 0$
(no calls).  At noon ($t=12$): $\lambda(12) = 10$ (peak 10 calls/hour).  So
$\lambda_{\max} = 10$.  In thinning: propose arrivals at rate 10, accept each at
time $t$ with probability $p(t) = \lambda(t)/10$.  Expected total calls per day:
$\int_0^{24}\lambda(t)\,dt = 10 \times 12 = 120$.
\end{exampleblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Python: NHPP Thinning (Algorithm 75)}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np

def nhpp_thinning(lam_func, lam_max, t_max, rng=None):
    """NH-Poi-thinning: simulate NHPP with intensity lam_func(t).
    lam_func must satisfy lam_func(t) <= lam_max for all t in [0,t_max]."""
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng()
    A = []
    t = -np.log(rng.uniform()) / lam_max   # first candidate ~ Exp(lam_max)
    while t <= t_max:
        p = lam_func(t) / lam_max          # acceptance probability
        if rng.uniform() <= p:             # accept with prob p
            A.append(t)
        t += -np.log(rng.uniform()) / lam_max   # next candidate
    return np.array(A)

# Sinusoidal daily pattern: peaks at noon (t=12), quiet at midnight
lam_func = lambda t: 10 * (0.5 + 0.5 * np.sin(2*np.pi*t/24 - np.pi/2))
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
A = nhpp_thinning(lam_func, lam_max=10.0, t_max=24.0, rng=rng)
print(f"Arrivals in one day: {len(A)}")   # expected ~120
print(f"First five times (hours): {np.array(A[:5]).round(2)}")
\end{lstlisting}
\end{frame}

\subsection{8.2.3 Poisson Process on General Spaces}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.2.3 Poisson Process on General Spaces}

The Poisson process generalizes naturally to any measurable space
$E \subset \R^d$ (R to the power d, meaning $d$-dimensional Euclidean space),
such as a two-dimensional spatial region (e.g., locations of customers in a city).

\begin{block}{Definition 8.2.6 -- Poisson Point Process on $E \subset \R^d$}
Let $\Lambda$ (Lambda, capital lambda) be an \textbf{intensity measure} on $E$.
A point process $N$ on $E$ is a \textbf{Poisson point process} with intensity
measure $\Lambda$ if:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For all disjoint sets $B_1, \ldots, B_n \subset E$, the random
        variables $N(B_1), \ldots, N(B_n)$ are independent.
  \item $N(B) \sim \mathrm{Poi}(\Lambda(B))$ for every measurable $B \subset E$.
\end{enumerate}
Here $N(B)$ denotes the number of points falling inside the region $B$.
\end{block}

Special cases:
\begin{itemize}
  \item On $E = [0, t_{\max}]$ with $\Lambda(dt) = \lambda\,dt$: the homogeneous
        Poisson process.
  \item With $\Lambda(dt) = \lambda(t)\,dt$: the NHPP.
  \item On a rectangle $E \subset \R^2$ with $\Lambda(d\mathbf{x}) = \lambda\,d\mathbf{x}$:
        a spatial Poisson process.
\end{itemize}

When $\Lambda(E) < \infty$ (total intensity is finite), simulation is easy:

\begin{block}{Algorithm 77 -- Poisson Process on $E$ with Finite $\Lambda(E)$}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Generate $T \sim \mathrm{Poi}(\Lambda(E))$.  Here $T$ (capital T) is the
        total number of points; its expected value is $\Lambda(E)$.
  \item Draw $\xi_1, \ldots, \xi_T$ (xi sub 1 to T) independently from $E$
        with probability distribution $\Lambda(d\mathbf{x})/\Lambda(E)$.
  \item Return the random set $\{\xi_1, \ldots, \xi_T\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}

\section{8.3 Birth and Death Processes / Markovian Queues}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.3 Birth-and-Death Processes and Markovian Queues}

The simplest queueing models have the Markov property: given the current number
of tasks $L(t) = i$ in the system at time $t$, the future behavior of the queue
depends only on $i$ and not on the entire history of arrivals and departures.
These models are called \textbf{birth-and-death (B\&D) processes}.

``Birth'' means an arrival (queue length increases by 1); ``death'' means a
departure (queue length decreases by 1).

\begin{block}{Birth-and-Death Process}
A continuous-time Markov process $X(t)$ on state space $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$
is a \textbf{birth-and-death process} with birth rates $(\lambda_i)_{i\ge 0}$
(lambda sub i) and death rates $(\mu_i)_{i\ge 1}$ (mu sub i, with $\mu_0 = 0$)
if, when in state $i$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A \emph{birth} (jump to state $i+1$) occurs at rate $\lambda_i$.
  \item A \emph{death} (jump to state $i-1$) occurs at rate $\mu_i$
        (only if $i \ge 1$).
  \item The sojourn time before the next event is
        $\tau \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda_i + \mu_i)$, i.e., exponential with
        rate equal to the total event rate.
  \item Given that an event occurs, it is a birth with probability
        $\lambda_i/(\lambda_i + \mu_i)$ and a death with probability
        $\mu_i/(\lambda_i + \mu_i)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Algorithm 78 -- BD: Simulate B\&D Process on $[0, t_{\max}]$}
\textbf{Require:} Parameters $(\lambda_k, \mu_k)_{k\ge 0}$; initial state
$y_0 = i$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Create empty list JS; set $t = 0$.
  \item \textbf{While} $t \le t_{\max}$:
        append $[t, i]$ to JS;
        generate $U_1, U_2 \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$;
        if $U_1 \le \lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\mu_i)$ then $\xi = +1$, else $\xi = -1$;
        draw $\tau = -\log(U_2)/(\lambda_i + \mu_i)$; set $t = t + \tau$;
        set $i = (i + \xi)_+$ (the next state, floored at 0).
  \item Return JS.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Kendall Notation: A/S/c}
Standard queueing systems are described by three symbols:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A} = arrival (inter-arrival time) distribution:
        \textbf{M} = Markovian (exponential), \textbf{G} = general,
        \textbf{GI} = i.i.d.\ general, \textbf{D} = deterministic.
  \item \textbf{S} = service time distribution: same codes.
  \item \textbf{c} = number of servers; $c = \infty$ means no waiting possible.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

The \textbf{M/M/1 queue} is the fundamental single-server queue: Poisson
arrivals at rate $\lambda$ (lambda) and exponential service times with rate $\mu$
(mu), so mean service time $= 1/\mu$.  It is a B\&D process with $\lambda_i = \lambda$
and $\mu_i = \mu$ for all valid $i$.

The \textbf{traffic intensity} $\rho = \lambda/\mu$ (rho, Greek letter ``rho'')
captures stability: $\rho < 1$ for stability, $\rho \ge 1$ for instability.

\framebreak

\begin{block}{M/M/1 Steady-State Distribution}
When $\rho < 1$, the stationary distribution of queue length $L^*$ (L-star) is
geometric:
\[
  \pi_i = \Prob(L^* = i) = (1-\rho)\,\rho^i, \qquad i = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
\]
Here $\pi_i$ (pi sub i) is the probability of finding exactly $i$ tasks in the
system at steady state.  The steady-state mean number of tasks:
\[
  \bar{l} = \E[L^*] = \sum_{i=0}^\infty i\,(1-\rho)\rho^i = \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}
          = \frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda}.
\]
Here $\bar{l}$ (l-bar) is the long-run average queue length.
When $\rho \ge 1$, the queue is unstable and $L(t) \to \infty$ almost surely.
\end{block}

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: M/M/1 Queue Length}
Take $\lambda = 0.5$ arrivals per unit time, $\mu = 1$ service per unit time.
Then $\rho = 0.5/1 = 0.5$.  Mean queue length: $\bar{l} = 0.5/(1-0.5) = 1$.
The server is idle with probability $\pi_0 = 1 - \rho = 50\%$.

Now take $\lambda = 8/9$, $\mu = 1$, so $\rho = 8/9 \approx 0.889$.  Then
$\bar{l} = (8/9)/(1/9) = 8$.  Going from $\rho = 0.5$ to $\rho = 0.889$
increased the mean queue length 8-fold!  This illustrates the extreme sensitivity
of queuing systems to traffic intensity near 1.
\end{exampleblock}

To estimate $\bar{l}$ from a simulation run on $[0, t_{\max}]$ with jump times
$0 = a_0 < a_1 < \cdots < a_k \le t_{\max}$ and state $y_j = L(a_j)$:
\[
  \hat{l}(t_{\max}) = \frac{1}{t_{\max}}\int_0^{t_{\max}} L(s)\,ds
                    = \frac{1}{t_{\max}}\sum_{j=0}^{k} y_j\,(a_{j+1}-a_j).
\]

import numpy as np

def simulate_bd(lam_func, mu_func, t_max, y0=0, rng=None):
    """Algorithm 78: Simulate a birth-and-death process on [0, t_max].
    lam_func(i) = birth rate from state i, mu_func(i) = death rate."""
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng()
    t, y = 0.0, y0
    JS = [(0.0, y0)]
    while t < t_max:
        lam_i = lam_func(y)
        mu_i  = mu_func(y)
        rate  = lam_i + mu_i
        if rate == 0:
            break
        t += rng.exponential(1.0 / rate)
        if t >= t_max:
            break
        u = rng.uniform()
        y = y + 1 if u < lam_i / rate else max(y - 1, 0)
        JS.append((t, y))
    JS.append((t_max, y))
    times  = np.array([e[0] for e in JS])
    states = np.array([e[1] for e in JS])
    l_hat  = np.sum(states[:-1] * np.diff(times)) / t_max
    return l_hat, JS

# M/M/1: lambda=0.5, mu=1.0; theoretical mean = rho/(1-rho) = 1.0
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
l_hat, _ = simulate_bd(lambda i: 0.5, lambda i: 1.0 if i > 0 else 0.0,
                        t_max=1e4, rng=rng)
print(f"Estimated mean queue length: {l_hat:.4f}  (theory: 1.0000)")
\end{lstlisting}

\section{8.4 Basic Concepts of Queueing Theory}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.4 Basic Concepts of Queueing Theory}

We now set up general notation for single- and multi-server queues.  For the
$i$-th task:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $A_i$ = arrival time (when the task joins the system).
  \item $S_i$ = service time (how long the task takes to process, once started).
  \item $W_i$ = waiting time (time spent in the queue before service starts).
  \item $D_i = A_i + W_i + S_i$ = departure time (when the task leaves).
  \item Sojourn time = $W_i + S_i$ (total time from arrival to departure).
\end{itemize}

The process $L(t) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \mathbf{1}(A_i \le t < D_i)$ is the number
of tasks in the system at time $t$ (including those being served).  Here
$\mathbf{1}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function, equal to 1 if the condition holds.

We say that arrival times $A_i$ follow a \textbf{renewal process} if the
inter-arrival times $\tau_i = A_i - A_{i-1}$ are i.i.d.  For example, GI/G/1
refers to a single-server system with general i.i.d.\ inter-arrival times and
general i.i.d.\ service times.

\begin{columns}
  \column{0.55\textwidth}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{queue_diagram}
  \column{0.43\textwidth}
  \textbf{Fig.\ 8.5 \& 8.6:} Two standard configurations.  Left: one shared
  queue feeding into $c$ servers (each arriving task joins a single waiting line).
  Right: $c$ separate queues, one per server.  In the shared-queue model, each
  task goes to the next free server.
\end{columns}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Traffic Intensity $\varrho$}
The \textbf{traffic intensity} (also called the \textbf{load} or
\textbf{utilization}) is
\[
  \varrho = \frac{\E[S_1]}{\E[\tau_1]} = \lambda \E[S_1],
\]
where $\E[S_1]$ (E of S sub 1) is the mean service time, $\E[\tau_1]$ is the
mean inter-arrival time, and $\lambda = 1/\E[\tau_1]$ (lambda) is the arrival
rate.  The symbol $\varrho$ (varrho, a variant of ``rho'') measures how much
work arrives per unit time relative to capacity.  For a $c$-server system,
stability requires $\varrho < c$; with one server, stability requires $\varrho < 1$.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Little's Law}
Under mild conditions, for \emph{any} stable queueing system:
\[
  \bar{l} = \lambda\,\bar{w},
\]
where $\bar{l}$ (l-bar) is the steady-state mean number of tasks in the system,
$\lambda$ (lambda) is the arrival rate, and $\bar{w}$ (w-bar) is the steady-state
mean \emph{sojourn time} (total time from arrival to departure).  This connects
the three most important performance measures: occupancy ($\bar{l}$), throughput
($\lambda$), and latency ($\bar{w}$), and holds regardless of distributions.
\end{block}

\begin{columns}
  \column{0.52\textwidth}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{littles_law}
  \column{0.46\textwidth}
  \textbf{Application to M/M/1:}
  We know $\bar{l} = \rho/(1-\rho)$.  Applying Little's Law:
  $\bar{w} = \bar{l}/\lambda = 1/(\mu - \lambda)$.
  With $\lambda=0.5$, $\mu=1$: $\bar{l}=1$ task on average, and
  $\bar{w}=2$ time units sojourn per task.
\end{columns}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Lindley Recursion for GI/G/1 -- Equation (8.4.1)}
Under First Come First Served (FCFS) discipline with one server, the waiting
times satisfy a simple recursion.  Starting with $W_1 = 0$ (first task finds
the system empty):
\[
  W_{i+1} = (W_i + S_i - \tau_{i+1})_+, \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots
\]
Here $(x)_+ = \max(x, 0)$ is the positive part of $x$, $W_i$ (W sub i) is the
waiting time of task $i$, $S_i$ (S sub i) is its service time, and $\tau_{i+1}$
(tau sub i plus 1) is the inter-arrival time before task $i+1$.
\end{block}

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Lindley Recursion}
When task $i+1$ arrives, the server has $W_i + S_i$ total remaining work.
Subtracting the inter-arrival time $\tau_{i+1}$ gives the remaining workload
when task $i+1$ arrives.  If positive, task $i+1$ waits; if negative, it finds
the server idle, so $W_{i+1} = 0$.  This recursion is valid for any sample path.
\end{alertblock}

\begin{block}{Pollaczek-Khinchin (PK) Formula for M/G/1}
When arrivals are Poisson at rate $\lambda$ and service times have general
distribution with mean $\E[S_1] = 1/\mu$ and $\E[S_1^2] < \infty$:
\[
  \tilde{w} = \frac{\lambda \E[S_1^2]}{2(1 - \lambda\E[S_1])}.
\]
Here $\tilde{w}$ (w-tilde) is the steady-state mean waiting time in queue.
$\E[S_1^2]$ (E of S sub 1 squared) captures the variability of service times:
more variable service leads to longer waiting even with the same average rate.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Virtual Waiting Time (Workload) Process -- Equation (8.4.4)}
The \textbf{virtual waiting time} (or \emph{workload}) $V(t)$ is the amount of
work the server would face if a new task arrived at time $t$:
\[
  V(t) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty (W_i + S_i - t)_+ \mathbf{1}(A_i \le t < A_{i+1}),
  \quad t \in [0, t_{\max}].
\]
It is a piecewise linear process: it decreases at rate 1 between arrivals (as the
server processes work) and jumps up by $S_i$ at each arrival time $A_i$.  It is
directly computable from the simulated sequence $(S_i)$, $(A_i)$, and $(W_i)$.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{M/G/$\infty$ System: Infinite Servers, No Waiting}
In an infinite-server system, each arriving task immediately starts service.
With Poisson arrivals (rate $\lambda$) and i.i.d.\ service times $S \sim G$
with mean $\E[S] = 1/\mu$ and load $\varrho = \lambda/\mu$:
\[
  \Prob(L^* = k) = \frac{\varrho^k}{k!}e^{-\varrho}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots
\]
The steady-state distribution is Poisson with mean $\varrho$, and crucially this
holds for \emph{any} service distribution $G$ -- only the mean service time
$\E[S]$ matters.  Equations (8.4.5)--(8.4.8) confirm that the stationary
Poisson distribution also gives:
$\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_0^t L(s)\,ds = \varrho$ a.s.
\end{block}

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: GI/G/1 Waiting Times (Table 8.1)}
Five tasks: $(\tau_1,S_1)=(1,2.5)$, $(\tau_2,S_2)=(1.5,2.5)$,
$(\tau_3,S_3)=(4.5,0.5)$, $(\tau_4,S_4)=(1,5)$, $(\tau_5,S_5)=(1,1)$.\\[0.2em]
Arrival times: $A_1=1$, $A_2=2.5$, $A_3=7$, $A_4=8$, $A_5=9$.
Applying the Lindley recursion with $W_1=0$:
\begin{align*}
  W_2 &= (0+2.5-1.5)_+ = 1.0 \quad W_3 = (1+2.5-4.5)_+ = 0.0 \\
  W_4 &= (0+0.5-1.0)_+ = 0.0 \quad W_5 = (0+5-1.0)_+ = 4.0
\end{align*}
Departures: $D_1=3.5$, $D_2=6$, $D_3=7.5$, $D_4=13$, $D_5=14$.
\end{exampleblock}

\section{8.5 Discrete-Event Simulations}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.5 Discrete-Event Simulations -- The General Framework}

Many processes change state only at specific \emph{event times} -- arrivals,
departures, failures, repairs.  Between consecutive events, the system state
remains constant.  A \textbf{discrete-event simulation (DES)} exploits this
structure by advancing the simulation clock directly to the next event rather
than stepping through time in small fixed increments.

\begin{block}{Core Components of a Discrete-Event Simulation}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Simulation clock} $t$: the current simulated time.
  \item \textbf{Event list} (EL): a sorted list of pending events, each with
        a scheduled time and event type.  The simulation always processes the
        event with the smallest time first.
  \item \textbf{State variables}: e.g., $n$ = current number of tasks in the
        system, queue contents, server status (busy/idle).
  \item \textbf{Output list} JS: records $(t, n)$ pairs after each event for
        post-processing and estimation.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}

The DES approach is far more efficient than time-stepping because it skips all
the ``idle'' time between events.

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Example 8.5.1 -- The On-Off Process}
The \textbf{on-off process} $K(t)$ takes values in $\{0, 1\}$ (off or on),
alternating between state 1 (\emph{on}, e.g., a server is active) and state 0
(\emph{off}, idle).  \emph{On}-time durations $T_1^{\mathrm{on}}, T_2^{\mathrm{on}},
\ldots$ are i.i.d.\ with distribution $F_{\mathrm{on}}$ (mean $\E T^{\mathrm{on}}$);
\emph{off}-time durations are i.i.d.\ with distribution $F_{\mathrm{off}}$
(mean $\E T^{\mathrm{off}}$).

The on-off process $K(t)$ is formally defined as:
\[
  K(t) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{for } S_{n-1} \le t < S_n \text{ and } n \text{ odd},\\
                       0 & \text{for } S_{n-1} \le t < S_n \text{ and } n \text{ even},
          \end{cases}
\]
where $S_0=0$ and $S_n = S_{n-1} + T_k^{\mathrm{on}}$ or $T_k^{\mathrm{off}}$
alternately.  The steady-state probability of being \emph{on} is:
\[
  p = \frac{\E T^{\mathrm{on}}}{\E T^{\mathrm{on}} + \E T^{\mathrm{off}}},
\]
a consequence of the renewal reward theorem.  To estimate $p$ from simulation:
\[
  \hat{p}(t_{\max}) = \frac{1}{t_{\max}}\int_0^{t_{\max}} K(s)\,ds.
\]
\end{block}

\end{center}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Example 8.5.2 -- Repairman Problem (Algorithm 79)}
A system has $N$ elements (machines) and $M$ repairmen.  Each working element
fails after a random failure time $\tau^f \sim F$; once failed, it waits for a
repairman, then is repaired in time $\tau^r \sim G$.  The state $k(t)$ is the
number of \emph{working} elements at time $t$.

Algorithm 79 uses two event types in the event list EL:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Failure} event for element $i$: reduce working count by 1.  If
        a repairman is free ($s < M$), assign one immediately and schedule a
        Repair\_Completion; otherwise, add element $i$ to the repair queue Q.
  \item \textbf{Repair\_Completion} event for element $i$: increase working count
        by 1; generate a new failure time for $i$.  If elements are waiting in Q,
        assign the freed repairman to the next one and schedule its completion.
\end{itemize}
State variables: $k$ = number of working elements, $s$ = number of busy
repairmen, $q$ = number of elements waiting for repair.
\end{block}

The time-averaged estimators:
\[
  \hat{l} = \frac{1}{t}\int_0^t k(s)\,ds \;\text{(mean working)}, \qquad
  \hat{w} = N - \hat{l} - \hat{m} \;\text{(mean waiting for repair)}.
\]
where $\hat{m} = \frac{1}{t}\int_0^t (N - k(s))\,ds - \hat{w}$ is the mean
number under repair.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Python: On-Off Discrete-Event Simulation}

import numpy as np

def simulate_onoff(F_on, F_off, t_max, rng=None):
    """Discrete-event simulation of on-off process.
    F_on / F_off: callables(rng) returning one sample."""
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng()
    t = 0.0; state = 1   # start in ON state
    integral = 0.0        # accumulate integral of K(t)
    while t < t_max:
        duration = F_on(rng) if state == 1 else F_off(rng)
        new_t = min(t + duration, t_max)
        integral += state * (new_t - t)  # K(s)=state on [t, new_t]
        t = new_t
        if t < t_max:
            state = 1 - state            # flip state
    return integral / t_max              # time-averaged estimator p_hat

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
# Exp on/off: E[T^on]=3, E[T^off]=1, theory p = 3/(3+1) = 0.75
p_hat = simulate_onoff(
    F_on  = lambda r: r.exponential(3.0),
    F_off = lambda r: r.exponential(1.0),
    t_max = 50000, rng=rng)
print(f"p_hat = {p_hat:.5f}  (theory = 0.75000)")
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{8.5.1 GI/G/c Queue Simulation}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.5.1 GI/G/1 Queue Simulation (Algorithm 81)}

For a GI/G/1 queue, we can estimate $\tilde{w}$ (the steady-state mean waiting
time) by iterating the Lindley recursion to generate $W_1, W_2, \ldots$ and
computing their sample mean.  To also estimate $\bar{l}$ (mean tasks in system),
we need the full discrete-event simulation.

\begin{block}{Algorithm 81 -- Simulation of a Single-Server Queue}
State variables: $t_A$ (time of next scheduled arrival) and $t_D$ (time of next
departure; set to $\emptyset$ when server is idle).  $n$ = number in system.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Initialize: set $t_A = 0$; generate $\tau \sim F$; set $t_A := \tau$;
        EL $= [t_A, \emptyset]$; JS $= [[0,0]]$.
  \item \textbf{While} $t_A < t_{\max}$: switch on EL:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item EL $=[t_A, \emptyset]$ (server idle): $n := 1$; generate $S \sim G$,
            $t_D = t_A + S$; generate $\tau \sim F$, $t_A := t_A + \tau$;
            update EL$=[t_A, t_D]$; append $[t_A, n]$ to JS.
      \item $t_A < t_D$ (arrival before departure): $n := n+1$; append to JS;
            generate $\tau \sim F$, $t_A := t_A + \tau$.
      \item $t_A \ge t_D$ (departure first): $n := n-1$; append to JS;
            if $n > 0$: generate $S \sim G$, $t_D := t_D + S$; else EL$=[t_A,\emptyset]$.
    \end{itemize}
  \item Flush final departure; append $[t_{\max}, n]$ to JS.
\end{enumerate}
Here $F$ = inter-arrival distribution, $G$ = service distribution.
\end{block}

The estimated mean queue length from JS is the time-weighted average:
$\hat{l} = \frac{1}{t_{\max}}\sum_j n_j (t_{j+1} - t_j)$.

\begin{block}{GI/G/c Queue: Workload Vector Recursion (Equation 8.5.2)}
For a \textbf{GI/G/c queue} ($c$ servers), maintain a sorted vector of server
workloads $\mathbf{V}_i = (V_{i,1} \le V_{i,2} \le \cdots \le V_{i,c})$ just
before the $i$-th task arrives.  Here $V_{i,j}$ (V sub i comma j) is the
remaining work at server $j$ when task $i$ arrives.

The waiting time of task $i$ is $W_i = V_{i,1}$ (the minimum workload; it goes
to the least-loaded server).  The workload vector updates as:
\[
  \mathbf{V}_{i+1} = \mathrm{sort}\!\left((V_{i,1} + S_i - \tau_{i+1})_+,\;
                     (V_{i,2} - \tau_{i+1})_+,\; \ldots,\;
                     (V_{i,c} - \tau_{i+1})_+\right).
\]
All workloads decrease by $\tau_{i+1}$ (time passes between arrivals), and task
$i$ adds $S_i$ to the minimum workload.  For $c=1$ this reduces to the Lindley
recursion.  If $\varrho = \E[S]/\E[\tau] < c$, the sequence $\mathbf{V}_i$ has
a limiting distribution and:
$\tilde{w} = \lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k V_{i,1}$ a.s.
\end{block}

\end{frame}

import numpy as np

def gig1_waiting_times(n_tasks, F_interarr, G_service, rng=None):
    """Lindley recursion: generate waiting times W_1,...,W_n for GI/G/1."""
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng()
    tau = F_interarr(n_tasks, rng)   # inter-arrival times
    S   = G_service(n_tasks, rng)    # service times
    W   = np.zeros(n_tasks)
    for i in range(1, n_tasks):
        W[i] = max(W[i-1] + S[i-1] - tau[i], 0.0)
    return W

    rng        = rng)
print(f"Estimated mean waiting time: {np.mean(W):.4f}  (PK theory: 1.0000)")

\section{8.6 Slotted ALOHA}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.6 Slotted ALOHA and Random Access Protocols}

\textbf{Slotted ALOHA} is a canonical model of a shared wireless communication
channel, proposed in the 1970s.  Time is divided into fixed-length slots.  Each
slot, one or more users may attempt to transmit a packet.  If exactly one user
transmits, the packet gets through (success).  If two or more transmit
simultaneously, a \emph{collision} occurs and all packets must be retransmitted.

The three main retransmission strategies:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Slotted ALOHA}: after any collision, retransmit in the next slot
        with a fixed probability $h$ (h, $0 < h < 1$).
  \item \textbf{Exponential Back-off}: retransmit with probability $2^{-i}$ after
        $i$ unsuccessful attempts.  Here $2^{-i}$ decreases geometrically in $i$.
  \item \textbf{Ethernet (Finite Exponential Back-off)}: exponential back-off up
        to a maximum of $K$ attempts, after which the packet is discarded.
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Slotted ALOHA System Model -- Equation (8.6.1)}
Let $X_n$ (X sub n) be the number of packets \emph{waiting} at the beginning of
slot $n$.  New arrivals in slot $n$: $A_n \sim \mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)$ (Poisson
with rate $\lambda$), i.i.d.\ across slots.  Retransmission attempts:
$Y_n \sim \mathrm{Bin}(X_n, h)$ (Binomial with $X_n$ trials and probability $h$).
Total transmissions in slot $n$: $Z_n = A_n + Y_n$ (Z sub n).

Queue update equation:
\[
  X_{n+1} = X_n + A_n - \mathbf{1}(Z_n = 1), \qquad n = 0, 1, \ldots
\]
The indicator $\mathbf{1}(Z_n = 1)$ equals 1 only when exactly one packet
transmits (a success), removing one packet from the queue.
\end{block}

\framebreak

The \textbf{local throughput} at slot $n$ and the \textbf{overall throughput}:
\[
  \gamma_n = \frac{N_n^s}{n}, \qquad \gamma = \lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_n,
\]
where $N_n^s = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}(Z_i=1)$ is the total number of
successful transmissions up to slot $n$.

\begin{block}{Success Probability and Conditional Drift}
Given $X_n = k$ waiting packets, the success probability is:
\[
  \Prob(Z_n=1 \mid X_n=k)
  = e^{-\lambda}\cdot kh(1-h)^{k-1} + \lambda e^{-\lambda}(1-h)^k.
\]
The \textbf{conditional drift} (expected change in queue length per slot) is:
\[
  \E[X_{n+1} - X_n \mid X_n = k]
  = \lambda - e^{-\lambda}\bigl(kh(1-h)^{k-1} + \lambda(1-h)^k\bigr).
\]
As $k \to \infty$, both terms $kh(1-h)^{k-1} \to 0$ and $(1-h)^k \to 0$, so
the drift converges to $\lambda > 0$.  This positive drift is the key instability
result: for large queues, retransmissions dominate and collisions overwhelm
the system.
\end{block}

\begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Slotted ALOHA Is Inherently Unstable}
For any fixed retransmission probability $h$ and any arrival rate $\lambda > 0$,
the chain $(X_n)$ is \textbf{transient}: the queue length drifts to infinity with
probability 1.  The theoretical channel capacity of Slotted ALOHA is $1/e \approx
0.368$ packets per slot (maximum achievable throughput), but even for arrival
rates well below this, the system is unstable under a fixed $h$.  This is a
classic example of a ``metastable'' system: it looks stable for many slots, then
suddenly jams.
\end{alertblock}

import numpy as np

def simulate_aloha(lam, h, n_slots, X0=0, rng=None):
    """Simulate Slotted ALOHA with Poisson(lam) arrivals per slot.
    h = retransmission probability. Returns queue lengths and throughputs."""
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng()
    X = X0
    Xs = [X0]; successes = 0; throughputs = [0.0]
    for n in range(1, n_slots + 1):
        A = rng.poisson(lam)                       # new arrivals
        Y = rng.binomial(X, h) if X > 0 else 0    # retransmission attempts
        Z = A + Y                                  # total transmissions
        success = int(Z == 1)                      # 1 iff exactly one
        successes += success
        X = max(X + A - success, 0)
        Xs.append(X)
        throughputs.append(successes / n)
    return np.array(Xs), np.array(throughputs)

# lambda=0.31 is well below capacity 1/e~0.368, yet system is unstable
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
Xs, gammas = simulate_aloha(lam=0.31, h=0.1, n_slots=10000, rng=rng)
print(f"Final queue length: {Xs[-1]}")
print(f"Final throughput:   {gammas[-1]:.4f}  (initial rate was ~0.31)")
\end{lstlisting}

\section{8.7 Ergodically Stable Processes}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.7 Ergodically Stable Processes}

To justify using a time average from a single simulation run as an estimator for
a steady-state quantity, we need the concept of \emph{ergodic stability}.

\begin{block}{Stationary Processes}
A stochastic process $(X^*(t))_{t\ge 0}$ (X-star of t) is \textbf{stationary}
if for all $d \ge 1$ and all time shifts $0 < h_1 < \cdots < h_d$, the joint
distribution of $(X^*(t+h_1), \ldots, X^*(t+h_d))$ does not depend on $t$.

Key consequences:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The marginal distribution $\Prob(X^*(t) \in B)$ is the same for all $t$;
        so $\E[X^*(t)]$ and $\Var[X^*(t)]$ are constant over time.
  \item The covariance function $R(h) = \mathrm{Cov}(X^*(t), X^*(t+h))$ (R of h)
        depends only on the \emph{lag} $h$, not on $t$.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Ergodic Processes -- Equation (8.7.2)}
A stationary process $(X^*(t))$ is \textbf{ergodic} if for any measurable
function $g$ with $\E[|g(X^*(h_1),\ldots,X^*(h_d))|] < \infty$:
\[
  \lim_{t\to\infty} \frac{1}{t}\int_0^t g(X^*(h_1+s), \ldots, X^*(h_d+s))\,ds
  = \E[g(X^*(h_1), \ldots, X^*(h_d))], \quad \text{a.s.}
\]
In particular, with $g(x) = x$: time averages converge to ensemble averages:
$\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_0^t X^*(s)\,ds = \E[X^*(0)]$ a.s.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Ergodically Stable Processes -- Equation (8.7.5)}
A stochastic process $(X(t))$ (not necessarily stationary) is
\textbf{ergodically stable} if there exists a stationary and ergodic process
$(X^*(t))$ such that for all suitable functions $g$:
\[
  \lim_{t\to\infty} \frac{1}{t}\int_0^t g(X(h_1+s), \ldots, X(h_d+s))\,ds
  = \E[g(X^*(h_1), \ldots, X^*(h_d))], \quad \text{a.s.}
\]
Time averages of the actual (non-stationary) process $X(t)$ converge to ensemble
averages of the stationary background process $X^*(t)$, regardless of the initial
state.  In particular (Equation 8.7.1):
\[
  \hat{X}(t) = \frac{1}{t}\int_0^t X(s)\,ds \;\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}\; I
  = \E[X^*(0)] \quad \text{a.s.}
\]
\end{block}

\textbf{Key examples:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Any irreducible positive recurrent continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC)
        is ergodically stable.  The intensity matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ (Q, whose
        $(i,j)$ entry is the jump rate from state $i$ to state $j$) determines
        the stationary distribution via $\boldsymbol{\pi}\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{0}$
        (pi times Q equals the zero vector).
  \item The on-off process with $\E T^{\mathrm{on}} < \infty$ and
        $\E T^{\mathrm{off}} < \infty$ is ergodically stable.
  \item The M/M/1 queue with $\rho < 1$ is ergodically stable.
\end{itemize}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Central Limit Theorem for Ergodic Processes -- Equation (8.7.4)}
For an ergodic process $(X^*(t))$ satisfying the CLT:
\[
  \sqrt{t}\bigl(\hat{X}^*(t) - \E[X^*(0)]\bigr) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}
  \mathcal{N}(0, \varsigma^2),
\]
where $\varsigma^2$ (varsigma squared) is the \textbf{time-average variance
constant (TAVC)}, or \emph{asymptotic variance}.  Here $\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}$
means convergence in distribution (the distribution of the left side approaches
that of the right side as $t \to \infty$), and $\mathcal{N}(0, \varsigma^2)$ is
a Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and variance $\varsigma^2$.
\end{block}

The fundamental precision-vs-run-length relationship (Equation 8.7.10):
\[
  b = z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{\varsigma}{\sqrt{t}},
\]
where $b$ (b) is the half-width of the confidence interval, $\alpha$ (alpha) is
the significance level (e.g., $\alpha=0.05$ for 95\% confidence),
$z_{1-\alpha/2}$ (z sub 1 minus alpha over 2) is the corresponding normal quantile
(e.g., $z_{0.975} \approx 1.96$), and $t$ is the simulation run length.
To achieve error $b$ with confidence $1-\alpha$:
\[
  t \ge \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}^2\,\varsigma^2}{b^2}.
\]

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Run-Length Formula}
Longer runs give smaller errors, but with diminishing returns: halving the error
requires quadrupling the run length (because $b \propto 1/\sqrt{t}$).  A more
autocorrelated process (larger $\varsigma^2$) requires proportionally longer
runs.  This is the fundamental tradeoff in steady-state simulation.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\subsection{8.7.1 Formula for Asymptotic Variance (TAVC)}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.7.1 A Formula for the Asymptotic Variance (TAVC)}

We derive the TAVC $\varsigma^2$ in terms of the covariance function
$R(h) = \mathrm{Cov}(X^*(t), X^*(t+h))$.

\begin{block}{Lemma 8.7.5 -- TAVC Formula (Equations 8.7.8--8.7.9)}
Suppose $\int_0^\infty |R(v)|\,dv < \infty$ and $\int_0^\infty R(s)\,ds \ne 0$.
Then as $t \to \infty$:
\[
  \mathrm{Var}\!\left(\hat{X}^*(t)\right) \sim \frac{2}{t}\int_0^\infty R(v)\,dv,
\]
and hence the TAVC is:
\[
  \boxed{\varsigma^2 = 2\int_0^\infty R(v)\,dv.}
\]
Here $R(v)$ (R of v) is the autocovariance at lag $v$ -- the covariance between
$X^*(t)$ and $X^*(t+v)$ -- and the integral sums all these covariances over all
positive lags.
\end{block}

\textbf{Proof sketch:}  Writing $\mathrm{Var}\bigl(\int_0^t X^*(s)\,ds\bigr)$
as a double integral over $(w,v) \in [0,t]^2$, using
$\E[X^*(w)X^*(v)] = R(|v-w|)$ (by stationarity with zero mean), one obtains
$2\int_0^t\int_0^v R(v-w)\,dw\,dv$, which grows like $2t\int_0^\infty R(u)\,du$
for large $t$.  Dividing by $t^2$ gives $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{X}^*(t)) \approx
\varsigma^2/t$.

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the TAVC Formula}
$\varsigma^2 = 2\int_0^\infty R(v)\,dv$ is twice the integral of the
autocovariance function.  If $R(v) \to 0$ quickly (the process decorrelates
rapidly), $\varsigma^2$ is small and fewer simulation steps are needed.  If
$R(v)$ decays slowly (as in heavy-traffic queues), $\varsigma^2$ is large and
much longer runs are required for the same precision.
\end{alertblock}

\begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{tavc_illustration}
\end{center}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{TAVC for the On-Off Process -- Kopocinski Formula (Eq.\ 8.7.11)}
For the on-off process with on-times $T^{\mathrm{on}}$ and off-times
$T^{\mathrm{off}}$ (both with finite means), the asymptotic variance of
$\hat{p}(t)$ (the estimator of $p$) is:
\[
  \varsigma^2 = \frac{(\E T^{\mathrm{on}})^2 \mathrm{Var}[T^{\mathrm{off}}]
                    + (\E T^{\mathrm{off}})^2 \mathrm{Var}[T^{\mathrm{on}}]}
                    {(\E T^{\mathrm{on}} + \E T^{\mathrm{off}})^3}.
\]
For exponential on/off times $T^{\mathrm{on}}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$ and
$T^{\mathrm{off}}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\mu)$ (so $\mathrm{Var} = 1/\lambda^2$ and
$1/\mu^2$ respectively), using $\rho = \lambda/\mu$ (Equation 8.7.12):
\[
  \varsigma^2 = \frac{2\lambda\mu}{(\lambda+\mu)^3} = \frac{2\rho}{\mu(1+\rho)^3}.
\]
\end{block}

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: TAVC of Exp/Exp On-Off Process}
Take $\lambda = 1/3$ (so $\E T^{\mathrm{on}} = 3$) and $\mu = 1$ (so
$\E T^{\mathrm{off}} = 1$).  Then $p = \E T^{\mathrm{on}}/(\E T^{\mathrm{on}} +
\E T^{\mathrm{off}}) = 3/4$ and $\rho = \lambda/\mu = 1/3$.

The TAVC is:
\[
  \varsigma^2 = \frac{2 \cdot (1/3) \cdot 1}{(1/3 + 1)^3}
              = \frac{2/3}{(4/3)^3} = \frac{2/3}{64/27} = \frac{9}{32} \approx 0.281.
\]
For target absolute error $b = 0.05$ at 95\% confidence ($z_{0.975}^2 \approx 4$):
\[
  t_{\min} = \frac{4 \times 0.281}{0.0025} \approx 450 \text{ time units.}
\]
\end{exampleblock}

\end{frame}

\subsection{8.7.2 TAVC for Other Systems}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.7.2 TAVC for Other Systems}

\begin{block}{Whitt-Burman Formula (Eq.\ 8.7.14) for Finite B\&D Processes}
Let $X(t)$ be a B\&D process on $\{0, 1, \ldots, n\}$.  The stationary
distribution satisfies $\pi_i = \pi_0\,\frac{\lambda_0 \cdots \lambda_{i-1}}{\mu_1 \cdots \mu_i}$.
For $Y(t) = f(X(t))$ with asymptotic mean $I_f = \sum_{i=0}^n f(i)\pi_i$, the
TAVC (attributed to D.Y.\ Burman via Whitt) is:
\[
  \varsigma_f^2 = 2\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\lambda_j\pi_j}
                 \left[\sum_{i=0}^{j}(f(i)-I_f)\pi_i\right]^2.
\]
Here $\lambda_j$ (lambda sub j) is the birth rate from state $j$, $\pi_j$ (pi
sub j) is the stationary probability of state $j$, and $f(i)$ is the function
of interest (e.g., $f(i)=i$ for queue length).
\end{block}

\textbf{M/M/1 Queue TAVC.}  For the M/M/1 queue ($\lambda_i = \lambda$,
$\mu_i = \mu$), the covariance function of $L(t)$ was derived by Reynolds using
a spectral method.  The result is:
\[
  \varsigma_L^2 = \frac{2\rho(1+\rho)}{\mu(1-\rho)^4}, \qquad \rho=\lambda/\mu.
\]
This formula shows that $\varsigma_L^2$ grows as $(1-\rho)^{-4}$ as $\rho
\nearrow 1$: near-critical queues require dramatically longer simulation runs.

\textbf{M/M/$n$ Queue.}  For $n$ servers with $\lambda_i = \lambda$ and
$\mu_i = \min(i,n)\mu$, the stationary distribution is the \emph{Erlang-C}
distribution and the TAVC can be computed from the Whitt-Burman formula.

\end{frame}

\section{8.8 Regenerative Processes}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.8 Regenerative Processes}

Regenerative processes are a broad class covering most continuous-time queueing
models.  Their key feature is that they contain random renewal times at which
the process probabilistically ``starts over'', enabling cycle-based estimation.

\begin{block}{Definition 8.8.1 -- Regenerative Process}
A stochastic process $(X(t), t \ge 0)$ is \textbf{regenerative} if there exist
random times $0 = T_0 \le T_1 < T_2 < \cdots$ (regeneration times) such that
the pairs $(Z_i, C_i)_{i \ge 0}$ are independent, where:
\[
  C_0 = T_1, \quad C_i = T_{i+1} - T_i \;\; (i \ge 1) \quad \text{(cycle lengths)},
\]
\[
  Z_i = \int_{T_i}^{T_{i+1}} X(s)\,ds \;\; (i \ge 1) \quad \text{(integrals over cycles)},
\]
and $(Z_i, C_i)_{i \ge 1}$ are i.i.d.  The process \emph{restarts
probabilistically} at each $T_i$.
\end{block}

\begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{regenerative_cycles}
\end{center}

\framebreak

\textbf{Examples of regenerative processes:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{On-off process:} regenerates each time the process transitions
        to state 1 (on).  One cycle $= T^{\mathrm{on}} + T^{\mathrm{off}}$.
  \item \textbf{M/M/1 queue:} $L(t)$ regenerates at times $T_i$ where the queue
        empties and a new arrival occurs.  One cycle = one idle + one busy period.
  \item \textbf{GI/G/1 queue:} regenerates at the start of each busy period
        (when the queue goes from 0 to 1 customer).  The virtual waiting time
        process $V(t)$ (defined in Eq.\ 8.4.4) is also regenerative, provided
        $\E S < \E T$ (i.e., $\rho < 1$).
  \item \textbf{M/G/$\infty$ queue:} regenerates when the system is empty and a
        new arrival occurs.
  \item Any irreducible positive recurrent Markov chain is regenerative with
        regeneration at return visits to any fixed state.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Non-delayed regenerative processes.}  When $T_1 = 0$ (the process starts
at a regeneration point), it is called \textbf{non-delayed} and denoted
$(X^o(t))$.  The cycles $(X^o(s), T^o_i \le s < T^o_{i+1})$, $i=1,2,\ldots$
are i.i.d.  For the on-off process starting in the on-state, the stationary
background process $K^*(t)$ has initial distribution given by:
\[
  \Prob(K^*(0)=1,\, A^*(0)>x) = \frac{\E T^{\mathrm{on}}}{\E T^{\mathrm{on}}+\E T^{\mathrm{off}}}
  \cdot \frac{1}{\E T^{\mathrm{on}}}\E(T^{\mathrm{on}}-x)_+.
\]

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Proposition 8.8.2 -- Renewal-Reward Theorem (Equation 8.8.1)}
If $\E[C_1] < \infty$ and $\E[|Z_1|] < \infty$, then with probability 1:
\[
  \hat{X}(t) = \frac{1}{t}\int_0^t X(s)\,ds \;\to\;
  I = \frac{\E[Z_1]}{\E[C_1]}.
\]
The steady-state mean equals the \emph{mean reward per cycle} divided by the
\emph{mean cycle length}.  If additionally $\E[C_1^2] < \infty$ and
$\mathrm{Var}[Z_1] < \infty$, a CLT holds (Equation 8.8.2):
\[
  \sqrt{t}(\hat{X}(t) - I) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}} \mathcal{N}(0,\,\varsigma_A^2),
\]
where $\varsigma_A^2 = \E[\zeta_1^2]/\E[C_1]$ and $\zeta_k = Z_k - I\,C_k$
(zeta sub k, the centered cycle contribution).
\end{block}

The renewal-reward theorem motivates the \textbf{regenerative ratio estimator}.
After simulating $k$ complete regeneration cycles (discarding cycle 0):

\begin{block}{Regenerative Ratio Estimator -- Equations (8.8.6)--(8.8.8)}
\[
  \hat{I}_k = \frac{Z_1 + \cdots + Z_k}{C_1 + \cdots + C_k}.
\]
By the LLN, $\hat{I}_k \to I$ a.s.  Under the conditions of Prop.\ 8.8.2
(Proposition 8.8.6): $\sqrt{k}(\hat{I}_k - I) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}
\mathcal{N}(0,\,\varsigma_B^2)$, where
$\varsigma_B^2 = \E[\zeta_1^2]/(\E[C_1])^2$.

The $1-\alpha$ confidence interval using $k$ cycles:
\[
  \hat{I}_k \pm \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\hat{\varsigma}_B}{\sqrt{k}},
\]
where $\hat{\varsigma}_B^2 = \hat{s}_A^2 / \hat{C}^2$ with
$\hat{C} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^k C_j$ (sample mean cycle length) and
$\hat{s}_A^2 = \frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^k (\zeta_j - \bar{\zeta})^2$ with
$\zeta_j = Z_j - \hat{I}_k C_j$.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\textbf{Comparing $\hat{X}(t)$ and $\hat{I}_k$:}  Both have the same asymptotic
confidence interval width (the CIs are identical in length to leading order).
The key advantage of $\hat{X}(t)$ is simplicity: no need to identify regeneration
times.  The key advantage of $\hat{I}_k$ is unbiasedness (once the first cycle
is discarded) and that it is directly constructible from cycle data.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.8 Simulation Results for On-Off Process (Example 8.8.7)}

We illustrate both estimators on the on-off process with true $p = 3/4$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Option 1 (Exp/Exp):} $T^{\mathrm{on}}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1/3)$,
        $T^{\mathrm{off}}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)$; exponential tails.
  \item \textbf{Option 2 (Par/Gamma):} $T^{\mathrm{on}}\sim\mathrm{Par}(2.5, 1.8)$
        (Pareto), $T^{\mathrm{off}}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(2, 1/2)$; heavier-tailed.
\end{itemize}

In both cases $\E T^{\mathrm{on}} = 3$ and $\E T^{\mathrm{off}} = 1$, giving
$p = 3/(3+1) = 0.75$.  Both estimators computed from the same simulation run
for $t \in [0, 1000]$.

import numpy as np

def regenerative_onoff(F_on, F_off, n_cycles, rng=None):
    """Regenerative ratio estimator for on-off process.
    Cycles begin at each entry into the on state.
    First cycle is discarded for unbiasedness."""
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng()
    Zs, Cs = [], []
    for _ in range(n_cycles + 1):   # +1 to discard first cycle
        T_on  = F_on(rng)
        T_off = F_off(rng)
        C = T_on + T_off   # cycle length = on + off
        Z = T_on            # integral of K(t)=1 over cycle = on-length
        Zs.append(Z); Cs.append(C)
    Zs, Cs = np.array(Zs[1:]), np.array(Cs[1:])  # discard first
    I_hat = np.sum(Zs) / np.sum(Cs)              # ratio estimator
    # Estimate TAVC: zeta_j = Z_j - I_hat * C_j
    zeta  = Zs - I_hat * Cs
    C_bar = np.mean(Cs)
    s2_B  = np.var(zeta, ddof=1) / (C_bar ** 2)
    k = len(Zs)
    ci_half = 1.96 * np.sqrt(s2_B / k)
    return I_hat, ci_half

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
I_hat, ci = regenerative_onoff(
    F_on  = lambda r: r.exponential(3.0),
    F_off = lambda r: r.exponential(1.0),
    n_cycles = 500, rng = rng)
print(f"I_hat = {I_hat:.5f}, 95% CI: ({I_hat-ci:.5f}, {I_hat+ci:.5f})")
print(f"True p = 0.75000")
\end{lstlisting}

\section{8.9 Error Analysis of M/M/1 and M/G/$\infty$}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.9 Error Analysis of M/M/1 Queue}

We apply the TAVC framework to the M/M/1 queue to determine how long to
simulate for a given precision requirement.

The queue length $L(t)$ is a regenerative ergodically-stable process.
Regeneration times are moments when the queue empties and a new arrival occurs:
\[
  T_1 = \inf\{t > 0 : L(t^-) = 0,\, L(t) = 1\},
  \quad T_i = \inf\{t > T_{i-1} : L(t^-) = 0,\, L(t) = 1\}.
\]
Each cycle = one exponential idle period (duration $\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$) plus
one busy period.  The mean busy period is $1/(\mu-\lambda)$, and its variance is
$\mathrm{Var[\text{Busy}]} = (1+\rho)/[\mu^2(1-\rho)^3]$.

\begin{block}{M/M/1 Steady-State Quantities ($\rho = \lambda/\mu < 1$)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Steady-state mean queue length: $\bar{l} = \E[L^*] = \dfrac{\rho}{1-\rho}$
        (Equation 8.9.2).
  \item Steady-state variance: $\sigma_L^2 = \mathrm{Var}[L^*] = \dfrac{\rho}{(1-\rho)^2}$.
  \item TAVC (Equation 8.9.3): $\varsigma_L^2 = \dfrac{2\rho(1+\rho)}{\mu(1-\rho)^4}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

The TAVC $\varsigma_L^2$ grows as $(1-\rho)^{-4}$ as $\rho \nearrow 1$.  This
means that in heavy traffic, the required simulation run length grows as the
\emph{fourth power} of $1/(1-\rho)$.

\framebreak

The SACV (Squared Asymptotic Coefficient of Variation) is:
\[
  \mathrm{SACV} = \frac{\varsigma_L^2}{\bar{l}^2}
               = \frac{2(1+\rho)}{\mu\rho(1-\rho)^2}.
\]
Setting a target relative error of 10\% ($b = 0.1\bar{l}$) at 95\% confidence
($z_{0.975}^2 \approx 4$):
\[
  t_{\max} = 4 \cdot \frac{\varsigma_L^2}{(0.1\bar{l})^2} = 400 \cdot \mathrm{SACV}.
\]

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example 8.9.1 (with $\mu = 1$)}
\textbf{Case (a): $\rho = 1/2$.}
$\bar{l} = 1$.
$\varsigma_L^2 = 2(1/2)(3/2)/[1 \cdot (1/2)^4] = (3/2)/(1/16) = 24$.
Required: $t_{\max} = 400 \cdot 24/1 = 9600$.
Simulation on $[0, 10^4]$ gave $\hat{l} = 0.9822$ (1.78\% error -- within 10\%).

\textbf{Case (b): $\rho = 8/9$.}
$\bar{l} = 8$.
$\varsigma_L^2 = 2(8/9)(17/9)/[1 \cdot (1/9)^4] \approx 22032$.
Required: $t_{\max} = 400 \cdot 22032/64 \approx 1.377\times 10^5$.
Simulation on $[0, 10^4]$ gave $\hat{l} = 6.67$ (16.6\% error).
Since $10^4 \ll 1.377\times 10^5$, this large error is entirely expected.
\end{exampleblock}

\begin{columns}
  \column{0.52\textwidth}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{run_length_vs_rho}
  \column{0.46\textwidth}
  \textbf{Heavy traffic scaling:} As $\rho \nearrow 1$:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item For \emph{absolute} error $b$:
          $t_{\max} = O((1-\rho)^{-4})$.
          From $\rho=0.5$ to $\rho=0.95$: factor $100^2 = 10\,000$ more!
    \item For \emph{relative} error:
          $t_{\max} = O((1-\rho)^{-2})$.
          From $\rho=0.5$ to $\rho=0.95$: factor $100$ more.
  \end{itemize}
\end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{8.9 Error Analysis of M/G/$\infty$ Systems}

For the M/G/$\infty$ queue (infinite servers, Poisson arrivals at rate $\lambda$,
i.i.d.\ service times $S \sim G$ with mean $\E[S] = \mu^{-1}$), the covariance
function can be computed explicitly.

\begin{block}{M/G/$\infty$ Covariance Function and TAVC -- Equation (8.9.4)}
The covariance function of the stationary process $L^*(t)$ (Rolski et al.):
\[
  R(t) = \lambda\int_t^\infty(1-G(v))\,dv, \qquad t \ge 0.
\]
Here $G(v) = \Prob(S \le v)$ is the c.d.f.\ of the service time (G of v, the
probability that service completes within time $v$) and $(1-G(v))$ is the
probability that service time exceeds $v$.  The TAVC:
\[
  \varsigma^2 = 2\int_0^\infty R(t)\,dt = \lambda\E[S_1^2].
\]
This beautiful result says the TAVC equals $\lambda$ times the second moment of
the service time.  For exponential service ($\E[S^2] = 2/\mu^2$):
$\varsigma^2 = 2\lambda/\mu^2 = 2\rho$.
\end{block}

The behavior depends critically on the tail of $G$.  For Pareto service
$S = cX$, $X \sim \mathrm{Par}(\alpha)$ ($\Prob(X > x) = (1+x)^{-\alpha}$):
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\alpha > 3$: $\E[S^2] < \infty$, $\varsigma^2 < \infty$, CLT holds.
        Convergence at the normal $O(1/\sqrt{t})$ rate.
  \item $2 < \alpha \le 3$: $\E[S^2] = \infty$, $\varsigma^2 = \infty$.
        The CLT for $\hat{L}(t)$ breaks down; a non-standard stable limit theorem
        applies.
  \item $1 < \alpha \le 2$: $\E[S] < \infty$ but $\E[S^2] = \infty$.  The
        estimator $\hat{L}(t)$ converges to $\varrho$ (consistent) but
        extremely slowly.
\end{itemize}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Algorithm 82 -- Simulation of M/G/$\infty$ Systems}
Each task $i$ arrives at time $A_i$ (generated as a Poisson process) and departs
at $D_i = A_i + S_i$.  The integral $\int_0^{t_{\max}} L(s)\,ds$ equals
$\sum_i [\min(t_{\max}, A_i + S_i) - A_i]$ (Equation 8.9.5), because each task
$i$ contributes the portion of its sojourn before $t_{\max}$.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Initialize $t_{\max}$, $\lambda$; set $L = 0$, $T = 0$.
  \item \textbf{While} arrival times $A_i \le t_{\max}$:
        generate $A_i \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$; generate $S_i \sim G$.
        If $A_i + S_i \le t_{\max}$: $L \leftarrow L + S_i$; else:
        $L \leftarrow L + (t_{\max} - A_i)$.
  \item Compute $\hat{l} = L / t_{\max}$; return $\hat{l}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}

import numpy as np

def simulate_mginf(lam, G_sample, t_max, rng=None):
    """Algorithm 82: Simulate M/G/inf and estimate steady-state mean.
    Uses: hat_l = sum_i [min(t_max, A_i+S_i) - A_i] / t_max"""
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng()
    arrivals = []
    t = rng.exponential(1.0 / lam)
    while t < t_max:
        arrivals.append(t)
        t += rng.exponential(1.0 / lam)
    arrivals = np.array(arrivals)
    if len(arrivals) == 0:
        return 0.0
    service    = G_sample(len(arrivals), rng)
    departures = arrivals + service
    # Each task i contributes min(t_max, D_i) - A_i to the integral
    contribution = np.minimum(departures, t_max) - arrivals
    return np.sum(contribution) / t_max

rng = np.random.default_rng(99)
# Exponential service: theory rho=lambda/mu=5/1=5
l_hat = simulate_mginf(5.0, lambda n, r: r.exponential(1.0, n), 1e4, rng)
print(f"M/M/inf (Exp, sigma^2=10):     l_hat={l_hat:.4f} (theory=5.0)")

# Pareto(3.2) service, scaled to mean 1
l2 = simulate_mginf(5.0, lambda n, r: r.pareto(3.2, n)*(2.2/(3.2-1)), 1e4, rng)
print(f"M/Par/inf (alpha=3.2, s^2=18.3): l_hat={l2:.4f} (theory=5.0)")
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Chapter 8 Summary}

\begin{columns}
  \column{0.50\textwidth}
  \textbf{Arrival modeling:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Homogeneous Poisson: i.i.d.\ $\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$ inter-arrivals;
          simulate via sequential draws (Alg.~73) or order statistics (Alg.~74).
    \item Non-homogeneous Poisson: time-varying rate $\lambda(t)$; use thinning
          (Alg.~75) or inversion (Alg.~76).
    \item General spaces: Alg.~77 draws $K \sim \mathrm{Poi}(\Lambda(E))$
          points from the normalized intensity measure.
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Queue models:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item M/M/1, M/M/$c$: B\&D processes; stable iff $\rho < 1$ (or $\rho < c$);
          stationary mean $\bar{l} = \rho/(1-\rho)$; Little's Law: $\bar{w} =
          \bar{l}/\lambda$.
    \item GI/G/1: Lindley recursion $W_{i+1} = (W_i + S_i - \tau_{i+1})_+$.
    \item GI/G/$c$: workload vector recursion (Eq.\ 8.5.2).
    \item M/G/$\infty$: no waiting; $L^* \sim \mathrm{Poi}(\varrho)$.
    \item Slotted ALOHA: inherently unstable for any fixed $h$; drift
          $\to \lambda > 0$ for large queues.
  \end{itemize}

  \column{0.48\textwidth}
  \textbf{Output analysis:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Time average $\hat{X}(t) \to I = \E[X^*]$ for ergodically stable
          processes (Eq.\ 8.7.1).
    \item TAVC: $\varsigma^2 = 2\int_0^\infty R(v)\,dv$ (Lemma 8.7.5).
    \item Run length: $t_{\max} = z_{1-\alpha/2}^2\,\varsigma^2/b^2$.
    \item M/M/1 TAVC: $\varsigma_L^2 = 2\rho(1+\rho)/[\mu(1-\rho)^4]$.
    \item M/G/$\infty$ TAVC: $\varsigma^2 = \lambda\E[S^2]$.
    \item Heavy traffic: required run grows as $(1-\rho)^{-4}$ for absolute
          error.
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Regenerative estimation:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Ratio estimator $\hat{I}_k = \sum Z_j / \sum C_j$.
    \item Same asymptotic CI width as $\hat{X}(t)$.
    \item Unbiased once first cycle discarded.
    \item TAVC estimated from cycles via $\zeta_j = Z_j - \hat{I}_k C_j$.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{alertblock}{Central Message}
  The interplay between traffic intensity $\rho$, TAVC $\varsigma^2$, and
  required run length $t_{\max}$ governs the cost of steady-state simulation.
  Heavy-traffic queues ($\rho \nearrow 1$) require dramatically longer runs.
  Service time variability (via $\E[S^2]$) is equally critical for M/G/$\infty$
  and M/G/1 systems.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{columns}
