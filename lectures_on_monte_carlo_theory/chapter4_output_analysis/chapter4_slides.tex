\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Introduction: Why Output Analysis?}

Every time you run a Monte Carlo simulation, each result you get is a
\emph{random number} --- it will be slightly different if you run the
simulation again.  Suppose you estimate $I = \pi$ (pi, approximately
3.14159) by simulating $R = 1{,}000$ random points and obtain the
estimate $\hat{I} = 3.14$.  That single number tells you almost nothing
on its own.  How close is 3.14 to the truth?  Could the true value be
3.10 or 3.20 instead?  How many more runs would you need to be confident
your answer is correct to two decimal places?

\textbf{Output analysis} gives scientific, quantitative meaning to
simulation results.  Without it, a simulation study produces numbers but
not knowledge.  With it, we can make rigorous statements like:
``I am 95\% confident that the true value lies between 3.12 and 3.16.''

The two central goals of output analysis in this chapter are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Build a \emph{confidence interval} (CI) that brackets the true
        value $I$ with a specified probability $1 - \alpha$ (one minus
        alpha), for example 95\% when $\alpha = 0.05$.
  \item Determine how many replications $R$ are needed to achieve a
        desired half-width $b$ (the ``radius'' of the interval around
        the estimate) for that confidence level.
\end{enumerate}

The mathematical foundation is the \textbf{Central Limit Theorem (CLT)},
which guarantees that the average of many independent random variables
is approximately normally distributed regardless of the individual
distribution.  This allows us to use well-known properties of the
normal distribution to build confidence intervals.

\begin{block}{The Central Goal of Chapter 4}
  Everything in this chapter flows from the \textbf{fundamental
  relationship}:
  \[
    b \;=\; \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\,D}{\sqrt{R}}.
  \]
  Reading each symbol in plain English: $b$ is the CI half-width
  (how precise the estimate is --- smaller is better); $R$ is the number
  of simulation replications (how many independent runs we perform);
  $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ (z sub one-minus-alpha-over-two) is the standard
  normal quantile at level $1 - \alpha/2$, which for 95\% confidence
  equals $z_{0.975} = 1.96$, and for 99\% confidence equals
  $z_{0.995} = 2.576$; and $D$ is a variability constant that depends
  on the specific estimation problem (it captures how ``spread out'' the
  simulation outputs are).  Any two of these three quantities ($b$, $R$,
  $D$) determine the third.
\end{block}

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read This Formula}
  The formula says that the error $b$ shrinks as $1/\sqrt{R}$ --- the
  square root of the number of replications.  This means that to halve
  the error you must \emph{quadruple} the number of runs.  Going from
  $b = 0.1$ to $b = 0.01$ (ten times more precise) requires
  \emph{one hundred times} as many replications.  This
  ``diminishing returns'' property is a fundamental fact about Monte
  Carlo estimation that every practitioner must understand.
\end{alertblock}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{General Problem Setup}
  Throughout Chapter 4, we wish to estimate a quantity that can be
  written as an expectation:
  \[
    I \;=\; \mathbb{E}Y,
  \]
  where $\mathbb{E}$ (blackboard-bold E) denotes the \emph{expectation}
  (the theoretical long-run average, also called the mean), and $Y$ is a
  random variable that we can simulate on a computer.  We require that
  $Y$ has finite variance: $\mathbb{E}Y^2 < \infty$ (this rules out
  pathological distributions with infinite spread).  We draw $R$
  independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) copies $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots,
  Y_R$ --- each one being a fresh, independent simulation run --- and
  form their sample mean $\hat{Y}_R$ to estimate $I$.
\end{block}

Two fundamentally different notions of error will appear throughout this
chapter, and they behave very differently.

\textbf{Absolute error:} The absolute error is $|\hat{I} - I|$, the raw
numerical distance between the estimate and the truth.  We want this to
be at most some tolerance $\varepsilon$ (epsilon) with high probability.
For estimating $I = \pi$, we might ask for absolute error at most 0.01,
meaning the estimate lies within the interval $(3.131, 3.151)$.

\textbf{Relative error:} The relative error is $|\hat{I}/I - 1| =
|\hat{I} - I|/I$, the discrepancy expressed as a fraction of the true
value.  A relative error of 1\% means the estimate is within 1\% of the
truth.  Relative error is much more informative when $I$ itself is very
small --- for example, when $I$ is the probability of a rare event like
$I = 10^{-6}$.  An absolute error of 0.01 would be completely useless
there (it would allow the estimate to be anywhere from 0 to 0.0100001),
whereas a relative error of 1\% would mean the estimate is between
$0.99 \times 10^{-6}$ and $1.01 \times 10^{-6}$ --- genuinely informative.

\begin{block}{Error Control Conditions (for $\varepsilon, \delta \in (0,1)$)}
  We want our estimator $\hat{I}$ to satisfy one of the following with
  probability at least $1 - \delta$ (delta, the allowed failure
  probability; for 99\% confidence, $\delta = 0.01$):
  \begin{align*}
    \text{(E1) Absolute error: } &
      \mathbb{P}\!\left(|\hat{I} - I| \geq \varepsilon\right) \leq \delta, \\[4pt]
    \text{(E2) Relative error: } &
      \mathbb{P}\!\left(|\hat{I}/I - 1| \geq \varepsilon\right) \leq \delta.
  \end{align*}
  Condition (E2) is strictly harder than (E1) whenever $I < 1$, because
  it demands that the error be small \emph{relative to the true value},
  not just in absolute terms.
\end{block}

\begin{alertblock}{Key Takeaway}
  This chapter focuses primarily on absolute error control for Sections
  4.1 through 4.3, building up the machinery of confidence intervals for
  means, transformed means, and quantiles.  Section 4.4 then returns to
  relative error, showing it is dramatically harder when $I$ is small ---
  which motivates the variance reduction techniques of Chapter 5.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.1.1 The Sample Mean Estimator}

We want to estimate $I = \mathbb{E}Y$.  The most natural estimator is
simply the average of $R$ independent simulation outputs --- one output
per run.  This estimator is so fundamental that it is worth studying in
careful detail, because all the ideas carry over to more complex
situations.

\begin{block}{Sample Mean Estimator}
  Given $R$ independent replications $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_R$ (each one
  is an independent simulation run with the same distribution as $Y$),
  the sample mean estimator is:
  \[
    \hat{Y}_R \;=\; \frac{1}{R} \sum_{j=1}^{R} Y_j.
  \]
  Here $\sum_{j=1}^{R}$ (capital Sigma, meaning ``sum'') adds up the
  simulation outputs from run number $j=1$ to run number $j=R$.
  The symbol $R$ (capital R) is the total number of replications, and
  $Y_j$ (Y sub j) is the output of the $j$-th run.
\end{block}

This estimator has two fundamental properties, which together guarantee
that it is a trustworthy way to estimate $I$.

\textbf{Unbiasedness.}  On average, the sample mean equals the truth.
Taking the expectation of both sides of the formula:
\[
  \mathbb{E}\hat{Y}_R
    = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{R}\sum_{j=1}^R Y_j\right]
    = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{j=1}^R \mathbb{E}Y_j
    = \frac{1}{R} \cdot R \cdot \mathbb{E}Y
    = I.
\]
Since each $Y_j$ has the same distribution as $Y$, we have
$\mathbb{E}Y_j = I$ for each $j$.  The result $\mathbb{E}\hat{Y}_R = I$
means the estimator does not systematically over- or under-estimate ---
it does not ``lean'' in any direction.

\textbf{Strong consistency.}  By the Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN),
as we run more and more replications, the sample mean converges to the
true value with probability 1:
\[
  \hat{Y}_R \;\longrightarrow\; I \quad \text{with probability 1, as } R \to \infty.
\]
In plain terms: if we could run infinitely many simulations, the average
would be exactly right.  For any fixed gap $b > 0$, this implies
\emph{weak consistency}: the probability of the estimate being wrong by
more than $b$ vanishes as $R$ grows:
$\mathbb{P}(|\hat{Y}_R - I| \geq b) \to 0$.

Weak consistency in turn guarantees that for any desired accuracy $b > 0$
and any failure probability $\alpha \in (0,1)$, there exists a sample
size $R_0$ such that for all $R \geq R_0$:
\[
  \mathbb{P}\!\left(\hat{Y}_R - b < I \leq \hat{Y}_R + b\right) \geq 1 - \alpha.
  \tag{1.2}
\]
This is the key promise: for large enough $R$, the random interval
$(\hat{Y}_R - b,\; \hat{Y}_R + b)$ brackets the true $I$ with high
probability.

\framebreak

The guarantee in (1.2) is existential --- it tells us such $R_0$ exists
but gives no formula for how large it is.  Two tools give us quantitative,
computable bounds.

\begin{block}{Chebyshev's Inequality-Based Bound (Remark 4.1.1)}
  Chebyshev's inequality states that for any random variable $X$ with
  finite variance and any $t > 0$:
  \[
    \mathbb{P}(|X - \mathbb{E}X| \geq t) \;\leq\; \frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{t^2}.
  \]
  Here $\mathrm{Var}(X)$ is the variance of $X$ (how spread out $X$ is
  around its mean), and $t$ is the threshold distance.  Since
  $\hat{Y}_R$ has variance $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{Y}_R) = \sigma_Y^2/R$
  (where $\sigma_Y^2 = \mathrm{Var}(Y)$ is the variance of a single
  observation), applying Chebyshev with $t = b$:
  \[
    \mathbb{P}(|\hat{Y}_R - I| \geq b) \;\leq\; \frac{\sigma_Y^2/R}{b^2}.
  \]
  Setting this bound equal to $\alpha$ (alpha, the significance level,
  e.g.\ $\alpha = 0.05$ for 95\% confidence) and solving for the
  half-width $b$ gives the \emph{Chebyshev half-width}:
  \[
    b_R^{\mathrm{Ch}} \;=\; \frac{\sigma_Y}{\alpha^{1/2}\,\sqrt{R}}.
    \tag{1.3}
  \]
  Here $\sigma_Y = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}$ (sigma sub Y) is the
  standard deviation of a single simulation output --- how spread out
  one run's result is.  Note that the error decreases as $1/\sqrt{R}$,
  which we call the \emph{Monte Carlo rate}.
\end{block}

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Chebyshev Bound}
  This bound is valid for \emph{any} distribution of $Y$ and any
  sample size $R$ (it is non-asymptotic, meaning it does not require
  $R$ to be large).  However, it is quite conservative: it ignores
  the shape of the distribution and relies only on the variance.  In
  practice, the true probability of error is much smaller than the
  Chebyshev bound suggests.  The CLT-based bound (next) is far sharper,
  but it requires $R$ to be reasonably large.
\end{alertblock}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)}
  Let $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_R$ be i.i.d.\ with mean $I = \mathbb{E}Y$
  and standard deviation $\sigma_Y = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(Y)} < \infty$
  (sigma sub Y, a finite positive number).  As the number of replications
  $R$ grows without bound, the standardized sample mean converges in
  distribution to the standard normal:
  \[
    \frac{\hat{Y}_R - I}{\sigma_Y / \sqrt{R}}
      \;=\;
    \frac{\sqrt{R}\,(\hat{Y}_R - I)}{\sigma_Y}
      \;\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\;
      \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
  \]
  The notation $\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}$ (converges in distribution)
  means: for large $R$, the left-hand side behaves like a standard
  normal variable.  Equivalently, $\hat{Y}_R$ is approximately normally
  distributed with mean $I$ and variance $\sigma_Y^2 / R$:
  \[
    \hat{Y}_R \;\approx\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(I,\; \frac{\sigma_Y^2}{R}\right)
    \quad \text{for large } R.
  \]
\end{block}

Let $z_{1-\alpha/2} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2)$ denote the
$(1-\alpha/2)$-quantile of the standard normal distribution, where
$\Phi$ (Phi) is the standard normal CDF (cumulative distribution
function, i.e.\ the probability of being at or below a value).
For $\alpha = 0.05$ (95\% confidence): $z_{0.975} = 1.96$.
For $\alpha = 0.01$ (99\% confidence): $z_{0.995} = 2.576$.
Rearranging the CLT to isolate $I$ gives:

\begin{block}{The Fundamental Relationship (Eq.\ 1.6)}
  \[
    b \;=\; z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sqrt{R}},
    \tag{1.6}
  \]
  where $b$ is the CI half-width (how wide the interval is on each
  side of the estimate), $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ is the normal quantile
  (for 95\% CI: $z_{0.975} = 1.96$), $\sigma_Y$ is the standard
  deviation of a single output (how variable individual runs are),
  and $R$ is the number of replications.
\end{block}

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Fundamental Relationship}
  The half-width $b$ shrinks proportionally to $1/\sqrt{R}$ as we
  add more replications.  This means doubling accuracy --- halving
  $b$ --- requires \emph{quadrupling} the number of runs.  To get
  ten times more precise (reduce $b$ by a factor of 10), you need
  one hundred times as many runs.  This ``diminishing returns'' law
  governs all Monte Carlo estimation and is why variance reduction
  (Chapter 5) is so valuable: if we can lower $\sigma_Y^2$ by a
  factor of 10, we need only one-tenth as many replications for the
  same accuracy.
\end{alertblock}

\framebreak

Solving the fundamental relationship for $R$ tells us how many
replications to plan for:

\begin{block}{Required Number of Replications (Eq.\ 1.7)}
  To achieve half-width $b$ at confidence level $1 - \alpha$, we need:
  \[
    R \;=\; z_{1-\alpha/2}^2\,\frac{\sigma_Y^2}{b^2}.
    \tag{1.7}
  \]
  For 95\% confidence ($\alpha = 0.05$, $z_{0.975} = 1.96$):
  $R = 1.96^2 \cdot \sigma_Y^2 / b^2 \approx 3.84\,\sigma_Y^2/b^2$.
  For 99\% confidence ($z_{0.995} = 2.576$):
  $R \approx 6.63\,\sigma_Y^2/b^2$ --- about 73\% more runs for 99\%
  vs 95\% confidence.
\end{block}

\begin{exampleblock}{CLT vs.\ Chebyshev: Quantitative Savings (Remark 4.1.5)}
  The ratio of the CLT half-width to the Chebyshev half-width, at the
  same nominal level $\alpha$, is:
  \[
    \frac{b^{\mathrm{CLT}}}{b^{\mathrm{Ch}}}
      = \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma_Y/\sqrt{R}}{\sigma_Y/(\alpha^{1/2}\sqrt{R})}
      = z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\alpha^{1/2}
      = 1.96 \times \sqrt{0.05}
      \approx 0.438 \quad (\alpha = 0.05).
  \]
  Since required sample size $R \propto b^{-2}$, the CLT-based formula
  requires only $(0.438)^2 \approx 19\%$ as many replications as the
  Chebyshev formula to achieve the same nominal confidence level.  That
  is roughly a $5\times$ reduction in computational cost.  The price of
  using the CLT is that it holds only approximately, for large $R$.
  The Chebyshev result is exact but extremely conservative.
\end{exampleblock}

\begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: The Monte Carlo Rate is Dimension-Free}
  The error $b \propto R^{-1/2}$ is completely independent of the
  dimension $d$ of the problem.  If we want to estimate a
  100-dimensional integral, the same formula applies as for a
  1-dimensional integral.  This is the crucial advantage of Monte
  Carlo over deterministic numerical integration methods, which suffer
  from a \emph{curse of dimensionality}: their error grows
  exponentially with $d$, making them impractical for high dimensions.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.1.1 Confidence Intervals in Practice}

In practice, the standard deviation $\sigma_Y = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}$
is unknown --- we do not know the distribution of $Y$ a priori (if we did,
we would not need to simulate).  We must estimate $\sigma_Y^2$ from the
same simulation data we use to estimate $I$.

\begin{block}{Sample Variance (Two Definitions)}
  Given observations $Y_1, \ldots, Y_R$ with sample mean $\hat{Y}_R$,
  we have two common estimators of the variance $\sigma_Y^2 =
  \mathrm{Var}(Y)$:
  \begin{align*}
    \hat{S}_R^2 &= \frac{1}{R-1} \sum_{j=1}^{R} (Y_j - \hat{Y}_R)^2
      \quad \text{(unbiased: $\mathbb{E}\hat{S}_R^2 = \sigma_Y^2$),} \\[6pt]
    \hat{\sigma}_R^2 &= \frac{1}{R} \sum_{j=1}^{R} (Y_j - \hat{Y}_R)^2
      \quad \text{(biased, but slightly simpler).}
  \end{align*}
  In both formulas, $(Y_j - \hat{Y}_R)^2$ (Y sub j minus Y-hat sub R,
  squared) is the squared deviation of the $j$-th observation from the
  sample mean.  Summing these squared deviations and dividing gives
  the average squared deviation --- the sample variance.

  The divisor $R - 1$ in $\hat{S}_R^2$ is called \emph{Bessel's
  correction}.  It compensates for the fact that we computed the
  deviations around $\hat{Y}_R$ (which was itself estimated from the
  data) rather than around the true mean $I$.  For large $R$, both
  definitions are nearly identical, but $\hat{S}_R^2$ is preferred
  for its exact unbiasedness.
\end{block}

Substituting $\hat{S}_R$ in place of $\sigma_Y$ in the fundamental
relationship (1.6) remains valid asymptotically.  This is justified by
Slutsky's theorem: since $\hat{S}_R \to \sigma_Y$ as $R \to \infty$,
the ratio $(\hat{Y}_R - I)/(\hat{S}_R/\sqrt{R})$ still converges to
a standard normal.  This gives us the practical confidence interval:

\begin{block}{$(1-\alpha)$-Confidence Interval (Eq.\ 1.9)}
  \[
    \left(\hat{Y}_R - z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{\hat{S}_R}{\sqrt{R}},\;\;
           \hat{Y}_R + z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{\hat{S}_R}{\sqrt{R}}\right).
    \tag{1.9}
  \]
  This interval is centered at the sample mean $\hat{Y}_R$, with
  half-width $z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\hat{S}_R/\sqrt{R}$.  The quantity
  $\hat{S}_R/\sqrt{R}$ is called the \emph{standard error of the
  mean} (SEM) --- it measures how precisely $\hat{Y}_R$ estimates $I$.
  Larger $R$ shrinks the SEM; larger $\hat{S}_R$ (more variable outputs)
  widens the CI.  For 95\% confidence ($z_{0.975} = 1.96$):
  the CI is $\hat{Y}_R \pm 1.96\,\hat{S}_R/\sqrt{R}$.
\end{block}

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Confidence Interval --- A Common Misconception}
  The statement ``the 95\% CI is $(3.12,\; 3.16)$'' does \emph{NOT}
  mean ``there is a 95\% probability that $I$ lies in $(3.12, 3.16)$.''
  The true value $I$ is a fixed (though unknown) constant --- it either
  lies in that specific interval or it does not.  The correct
  interpretation is: \emph{if we repeated this entire experiment many
  times, 95\% of the resulting intervals would contain the true $I$.}
  The randomness is in the interval, not in $I$.
\end{alertblock}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Online (Recursive) Update Formulas (Eqs.\ 1.11--1.12)}
  When $R$ is very large, storing all $R$ simulation outputs just to
  compute the mean and variance is wasteful.  These recursive formulas
  update both statistics using only the most recent observation, requiring
  $O(1)$ memory (constant memory, independent of $R$).  After observing
  $Y_{R+1}$:
  \begin{align}
    \hat{Y}_{R+1} &= \hat{Y}_R + \frac{Y_{R+1} - \hat{Y}_R}{R+1},
    \tag{1.11} \\[6pt]
    \hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^2 &= \hat{\sigma}_R^2
      + (\hat{Y}_R)^2 - (\hat{Y}_{R+1})^2
      + \frac{(Y_{R+1})^2 - \hat{\sigma}_R^2 - (\hat{Y}_R)^2}{R+1}.
    \tag{1.12}
  \end{align}
  Formula (1.11) says: the new mean is the old mean, adjusted by a
  fraction $1/(R+1)$ of the gap between the new observation $Y_{R+1}$
  and the old mean $\hat{Y}_R$.  When $R$ is large, this fraction is
  tiny, so the mean barely moves with each new observation --- which
  makes intuitive sense.
\end{block}

\textbf{Worked example of recursive update.}
Suppose we have run 1 replication with result $Y_1 = 3.0$, so
$\hat{Y}_1 = 3.0$ and $\hat{\sigma}_1^2 = 0$.  We then observe
$Y_2 = 5.0$.

Applying (1.11): $\hat{Y}_2 = 3.0 + (5.0 - 3.0)/2 = 3.0 + 1.0 = 4.0$.
The new mean is exactly the midpoint of 3.0 and 5.0 --- exactly
as expected.  No need to store $Y_1$ once $\hat{Y}_1$ is known.

\textbf{Practical workflow using a pilot simulation.}
Since we need $\sigma_Y^2$ to plan the required $R$, but $\sigma_Y^2$
is unknown, the standard approach is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Run a \emph{pilot} simulation of size $m$ (typically $m = 200$
        to $500$) to get an initial estimate $\hat{S}_m^2$ of the variance.
  \item Compute the required main sample size:
        $R = \lceil z_{1-\alpha/2}^2 \hat{S}_m^2/b^2 \rceil$
        (rounding up to the next integer).
  \item Run $R$ independent replications; compute $\hat{Y}_R$ and
        $\hat{S}_R$ from scratch (not combined with pilot runs, to ensure
        independence).
  \item Report the point estimate $\hat{Y}_R$ together with the
        confidence interval (1.9).
\end{enumerate}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.1.1 Examples: Estimating $\pi$ (pi)}

These two examples both estimate $I = \pi \approx 3.14159$, yet they
define different random variables $Y$ with $\mathbb{E}Y = \pi$.  The
variance of $Y$ directly controls how many replications are needed ---
so choosing a better $Y$ directly saves computation, even before we
apply any sophisticated variance reduction techniques.

\begin{block}{Example 4.1.6 (Asmussen \& Glynn) --- Hit-or-Miss Estimator}
  Imagine throwing a dart uniformly at random at the unit square
  $[0,1]^2$.  The dart falls inside the quarter-circle of radius 1
  (centered at the origin, covering the quarter of the unit disk in the
  first quadrant) if and only if $U_1^2 + U_2^2 \leq 1$, which happens
  with probability $\pi/4$ (the area of the quarter-circle).

  We define the random variable $Y = 4 \cdot \mathbf{1}(U_1^2 + U_2^2 \leq 1)$,
  where $\mathbf{1}(\cdot)$ (indicator function) equals 1 if the
  condition is true and 0 otherwise, and $(U_1, U_2)$ are independent
  uniform random variables on $[0,1]$.  Then:
  \[
    \mathbb{E}Y = 4 \cdot \mathbb{P}(U_1^2 + U_2^2 \leq 1) = 4 \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = \pi.
  \]
  Since $\mathbf{1}(U_1^2 + U_2^2 \leq 1)$ is a Bernoulli random
  variable with parameter $p = \pi/4$, we have
  $\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{1}(\cdot)) = p(1-p) = (\pi/4)(1 - \pi/4)$, so:
  \[
    \mathrm{Var}(Y) = 16 \cdot \frac{\pi}{4}\!\left(1 - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)
      = 16 \times 0.7854 \times 0.2146 \approx 2.697.
  \]
  For second-digit accuracy ($b = 0.05$), formula (1.7) gives:
  $R \approx 1.96^2 \times 2.697 / 0.05^2 \approx 4{,}144$ replications.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Example 4.1.7 (Madras) --- Smooth Integral Estimator}
  The identity $\pi/4 = \int_0^1 \sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx$ (integrating the
  upper boundary of the unit circle) suggests the estimator
  $Y = 4\sqrt{1 - U^2}$ where $U \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]$ (U uniform
  on the interval $[0,1]$).  Then:
  \[
    \mathbb{E}Y = \int_0^1 4\sqrt{1-u^2}\,du = 4 \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = \pi.
  \]
  The variance requires computing $\mathbb{E}Y^2 = \int_0^1 16(1-u^2)\,du = 16(1 - 1/3) = 32/3$:
  \[
    \mathrm{Var}(Y) = \frac{32}{3} - \pi^2 \approx 10.667 - 9.870 = 0.797.
  \]
  For second-digit accuracy ($b = 0.05$):
  $R \approx 1.96^2 \times 0.797 / 0.0025 \approx 1{,}224$ replications.
  This is approximately $4{,}144 / 1{,}224 \approx \mathbf{3.4\times}$ more efficient.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Variance Reduction Through Better Problem Formulation}
  Both estimators are perfectly unbiased for $\pi$ --- they are both
  mathematically correct.  Yet the smooth estimator requires roughly
  3.4 times fewer replications for the same accuracy.  The only
  difference is the \emph{choice of random variable $Y$}: the smooth
  estimator uses $Y = 4\sqrt{1-U^2}$, which is a smoothly varying
  function of $U$; the hit-or-miss estimator uses $Y = 4\cdot\mathbf{1}(\text{inside})$,
  which jumps between 0 and 4.  Smoother functions of the random input
  tend to have lower variance.  This is \emph{variance reduction in its
  simplest form}: simply choosing a better representation of $I$ as
  $\mathbb{E}Y$.  Chapter 5 develops systematic, powerful techniques
  for this.
\end{alertblock}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.1.1 Crude Monte Carlo and Hit-or-Miss Integration}

A core application of Monte Carlo is computing integrals.  Suppose we
want to evaluate:
\[
  I = \int_B k(x)\,dx,
\]
where $k : B \to \mathbb{R}$ is an integrable function (we can compute
$k(x)$ for any given $x$) and $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ is a bounded
region with volume $\mathrm{Vol}(B) = 1$ (we can always rescale so that
the volume equals 1; if $B = [0,1]^d$, it is already the case).  We
cannot compute this integral analytically, but we can evaluate $k$ at
any point.

\begin{block}{Crude Monte Carlo (CMC) Estimator (Eq.\ 1.13)}
  Generate $V_1, \ldots, V_R$ uniformly and independently from $B$
  (written $V_j \sim \mathcal{U}(B)$).  Set $Y = k(V)$, so that:
  \[
    \mathbb{E}Y = \mathbb{E}[k(V)] = \int_B k(v)\,\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(B)}\,dv = \int_B k(v)\,dv = I.
  \]
  The CMC estimator averages the function values at these random points:
  \[
    \hat{Y}_R^{\mathrm{CMC}} = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{j=1}^R k(V_j).
    \tag{1.13}
  \]
  More generally, if $X$ has density $f$ on $B$ and $Y = k(X)/f(X)$, then
  $\mathbb{E}Y = \int_B k(x)\,dx = I$, allowing us to use any convenient
  sampling distribution $f$.
\end{block}

\begin{exampleblock}{Example 4.1.10 (Madras) --- Estimating $I = \int_0^1 k(x)\,dx$}
  Let $k(x) = (e^x - 1)/(e - 1)$, where $e \approx 2.71828$ is Euler's
  number.  Setting $Y = k(U)$ with $U \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]$:
  \begin{align*}
    I = \mathbb{E}Y &= \int_0^1 \frac{e^x - 1}{e - 1}\,dx
      = \frac{1}{e-1}\!\left[e^x - x\right]_0^1
      = \frac{e - 1 - 1}{e - 1} = \frac{e - 2}{e - 1} \approx 0.418. \\
    \mathbb{E}Y^2 &= \int_0^1 k^2(x)\,dx \approx 0.2567. \\
    \sigma_Y^2 &= \mathbb{E}Y^2 - (\mathbb{E}Y)^2 = 0.2567 - (0.418)^2 \approx 0.0820.
  \end{align*}
  To achieve half-width $b$: $R = 1.96^2 \times 0.0820 / b^2 \approx 0.328/b^2$.
  For $b = 0.01$ (two decimal places): $R \approx 3{,}280$ replications.
\end{exampleblock}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Hit-or-Miss (HoM) Estimator (Example 4.1.11)}
  Assume $0 \leq k(x) \leq 1$ for all $x \in [0,1]$.  Generate two
  independent uniform random variables $U_1, U_2 \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]$
  and define $Y' = \mathbf{1}(k(U_1) > U_2)$ (the indicator that the
  point $(U_1, U_2)$ falls below the curve $y = k(x)$).  Then:
  \[
    \mathbb{E}Y' = \int_0^1 \int_0^{k(u_1)} du_2\,du_1 = \int_0^1 k(u_1)\,du_1 = I.
  \]
  The HoM estimator averages these indicators:
  $\hat{Y}_R^{\mathrm{HoM}} = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{j=1}^R Y_j'$.

  However, $Y'$ is a Bernoulli random variable with parameter $I$, so
  $\mathrm{Var}(Y') = I(1-I)$.  For the function $k(x) = (e^x-1)/(e-1)$
  with $I \approx 0.418$:
  $\mathrm{Var}(Y') \approx 0.418 \times 0.582 \approx 0.2433$.
  This is about $0.2433 / 0.0820 \approx 2.97\times$ the CMC variance.
  So HoM requires about 3 times as many replications as CMC for the
  same accuracy, and it also uses \emph{two} uniform random numbers
  instead of one.  The hit-or-miss method is simple to visualize
  but is statistically inefficient.
\end{block}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.1.1 Bounding the Variance Without Knowing $I$}

To plan a simulation study using formula (1.7), we need $\sigma_Y^2 =
\mathrm{Var}(Y)$.  But $\mathrm{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}Y^2 - (\mathbb{E}Y)^2 =
\mathbb{E}Y^2 - I^2$ involves the unknown quantity $I$ that we are trying
to estimate!  Sometimes we can bypass this circular problem by bounding
$\sigma_Y^2$ from above using only known upper and lower bounds on the
function $k$.

\begin{block}{Variance Bound for CMC (Remark 4.1.12)}
  For the CMC estimator $Y = k(X)$ with $X \sim f$ on $B$, the variance is
  $\sigma_Y^2 = \int_B k^2(x)\,f(x)\,dx - I^2$.  If we know constants
  $k_{\mathrm{up}}$ (an upper bound: $k(x) \leq k_{\mathrm{up}}$ for all $x$)
  and $k_{\mathrm{down}}$ (a lower bound: $I \geq k_{\mathrm{down}}$), then:
  \[
    \sigma_Y^2 \;=\; \int_B k^2(x)\,f(x)\,dx - I^2
      \;\leq\; k_{\mathrm{up}}^2 - k_{\mathrm{down}}^2.
  \]
  Using this upper bound in formula (1.7) gives a conservative (slightly
  too large) required sample size $R$, but guarantees the desired
  confidence level without knowing $I$.
\end{block}

\begin{exampleblock}{Bounding $\mathrm{Var}(Y)$ for $k(x) = (e^x - 1)/(e-1)$ on $[0,1]$}
  We know $k(0) = 0$ and $k(1) = 1$, so $0 \leq k(x) \leq 1$ for
  $x \in [0,1]$.  We also know $I = \mathbb{E}Y = (e-2)/(e-1) \approx 0.418 \geq 0$.

  A more careful analysis using $e \approx 2.71828$ gives:
  $k_{\mathrm{down}} \approx 0.4157$ and $\int_0^1 k^2(x)\,dx \leq 0.2622$.
  Therefore:
  \[
    \sigma_Y^2 \;\leq\; 0.2622 - (0.4157)^2 \approx 0.0894.
  \]
  The true value is $\sigma_Y^2 \approx 0.0820$, so the bound is about
  9\% too large.  Using this bound, the required $R$ is only 9\% more
  than strictly necessary --- a negligible price for not needing to know
  $I$ in advance.
\end{exampleblock}

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def estimate_pi_ci(R, alpha=0.05, seed=42):
    """Estimate pi via hit-or-miss MC and report a (1-alpha) CI."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    U = rng.random((R, 2))
    # Y_j = 4 * 1(U1^2 + U2^2 <= 1); E[Y_j] = pi
    Y = 4.0 * (U[:, 0]**2 + U[:, 1]**2 <= 1).astype(float)

    Y_bar = Y.mean()            # sample mean -- estimator of pi
    S2    = Y.var(ddof=1)       # unbiased sample variance (ddof=1 => divide by R-1)
    S     = np.sqrt(S2)

    z      = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)   # z_{1-alpha/2}; for alpha=0.05 => 1.96
    margin = z * S / np.sqrt(R)              # half-width b

    return Y_bar, Y_bar - margin, Y_bar + margin

# Example: R=10000, alpha=0.05
Y_bar, lo, hi = estimate_pi_ci(R=10_000)
print(f"Estimate: {Y_bar:.4f}")
print(f"95% CI : ({lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f})")
print(f"True pi: {np.pi:.4f}")
# Typical output:
# Estimate: 3.1464
# 95% CI : (3.1142, 3.1786)
# True pi: 3.1416
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.1.2 The Multidimensional Case}

Sometimes the quantity of interest is not a single number but a vector
$\mathbf{I} = (I^{(1)}, \ldots, I^{(d)})^T = \mathbb{E}\mathbf{Y}$,
where $\mathbf{Y} = (Y^{(1)}, \ldots, Y^{(d)})^T$ is a $d$-dimensional
random vector (each component is a separate simulation output of
interest).  The superscript $T$ denotes the transpose (turning a row
into a column).

For instance, we might simultaneously estimate the mean weight
$I^{(1)} = \mathbb{E}[\text{weight}]$ and mean height
$I^{(2)} = \mathbb{E}[\text{height}]$ of a population ($d = 2$),
or compute $d$ different integrals in a single simulation campaign.

\begin{block}{Vector Sample Mean Estimator}
  Given i.i.d.\ replications $\mathbf{Y}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{Y}_R$
  (each $\mathbf{Y}_j$ is a $d$-vector of simulation outputs from run $j$):
  \[
    \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R \;=\; \frac{1}{R}\sum_{j=1}^R \mathbf{Y}_j
      \;=\; \bigl(\hat{Y}_R^{(1)}, \ldots, \hat{Y}_R^{(d)}\bigr)^T,
  \]
  where each component $\hat{Y}_R^{(k)}$ (Y-hat sub R superscript k)
  is the sample mean of the $k$-th coordinate across all $R$ runs.
  This estimator is unbiased: $\mathbb{E}\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R = \mathbf{I}$.
\end{block}

Let $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}} = \mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{I})(\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{I})^T]$
(bold Sigma sub Y) denote the $d \times d$ \emph{covariance matrix} of
$\mathbf{Y}$.  Its $(i,j)$-entry is
$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}(i,j) = \mathrm{Cov}(Y^{(i)}, Y^{(j)})$
--- the covariance between the $i$-th and $j$-th components.  The
diagonal entries are the individual variances.

\begin{block}{Multivariate Central Limit Theorem (Eq.\ 1.23)}
  As $R \to \infty$:
  \[
    \sqrt{R}\,(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R - \mathbf{I}) \;\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\;
      \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}\right).
  \]
  This means that for large $R$, the vector estimator $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R$
  is approximately distributed as a $d$-dimensional normal (Gaussian)
  with mean vector $\mathbf{I}$ and covariance matrix
  $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}/R$.  The covariance matrix tells us
  both how uncertain each component is and how the uncertainties are
  correlated with each other.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Proposition 4.1.13 --- Key Properties of the Multivariate Normal}
  Let $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$
  where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ (bold mu) is the mean vector and
  $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T$ (with $\mathbf{A}$
  a square matrix, e.g.\ the Cholesky factor of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$).
  Then:
  \begin{enumerate}[(a)]
    \item $\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})
      \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{Id}_d)$, where $\mathbf{Id}_d$
      is the $d \times d$ identity matrix.  In words: the standardized
      vector $\mathbf{Z}$ has independent standard normal components
      (no correlations).
    \item $(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})
      \sim \chi_d^2$ (chi-squared, pronounced ``kai-squared'', with $d$
      degrees of freedom).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{block}

\textbf{Understanding part (b).}
The expression $(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})$
is the \emph{squared Mahalanobis distance} from $\mathbf{X}$ to its
mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$.  It measures how far $\mathbf{X}$ is from
its center in units of standard deviations, properly accounting for
correlations between components.  Part (b) says this distance follows
a chi-squared ($\chi^2$, chi-square) distribution with $d$ degrees of
freedom.

\textbf{Proof sketch of (b).}
From (a), $\mathbf{Z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{Id}_d)$
has i.i.d.\ standard normal components $Z_1, \ldots, Z_d$.  Then:
\[
  (\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})
  = \mathbf{Z}^T\mathbf{Z} = \sum_{i=1}^d Z_i^2,
\]
which is the sum of $d$ squared independent standard normals.
By definition, this sum has the $\chi_d^2$ distribution.

\textbf{Critical values of the chi-squared distribution.}
To build 95\% confidence regions, we need $q_{0.95}(\chi_d^2)$, the
value below which 95\% of the $\chi_d^2$ distribution falls:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{ccc}
    \toprule
    Dimension $d$ & $q_{0.95}(\chi_d^2)$ & Interpretation \\
    \midrule
    1   & 3.841 & Equivalent to $\pm 1.96$ in 1D (since $1.96^2 = 3.841$) \\
    2   & 5.991 & 2D confidence ellipse \\
    5   & 11.07 & 5D confidence ellipsoid \\
    10  & 18.31 & 10D confidence ellipsoid \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
As $d$ grows, the critical value grows, reflecting that a $d$-dimensional
normal vector is increasingly likely to be far from the origin in at
least one direction.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.1.2.2 Shapes of Confidence Regions}

For $\mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$,
we want to find a region $\mathcal{C} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ such that
$\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X} \in \mathcal{C}) \geq 1-\alpha$.  There are three
natural shapes for such regions, each with different trade-offs between
tightness and computational convenience.

\begin{block}{(1) Confidence Ellipsoid --- The Smallest Region (Eq.\ 1.15)}
  Using Proposition 4.1.13(b), the set:
  \[
    \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}} = \bigl\{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d
      : \mathbf{x}^T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x} \leq q_{1-\alpha}(\chi_d^2)\bigr\}
  \]
  satisfies $\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X} \in \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}) = 1-\alpha$
  exactly.  This is the minimum-volume region among all sets with coverage
  $1-\alpha$.

  If $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}^T$
  is the eigendecomposition (where $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}$ (Lambda) is
  the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_d$, and
  $\mathbf{F}$ is the matrix of corresponding eigenvectors $\mathbf{f}_1,
  \ldots, \mathbf{f}_d$), then the ellipsoid has principal axes of
  half-length $\sqrt{q_{1-\alpha}(\chi_d^2)\,\lambda_i}$ in direction
  $\mathbf{f}_i$.  Directions where the variance ($\lambda_i$) is large
  produce longer axes.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{(2) Confidence Parallelogram (Eq.\ 1.18)}
  Since $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu}) \sim
  \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{Id}_d)$ (a standardized normal vector),
  we can build a hypercube (box) $\mathcal{H} = (-z, z)^d$ in the
  standardized space, where
  $z = \Phi^{-1}\!\left((\sqrt[d]{1-\alpha}+1)/2\right)$.
  Transforming back to the original space gives a parallelogram:
  \[
    \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{par}} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathcal{H} + \boldsymbol{\mu}.
  \]
  This region has the same nominal coverage $1-\alpha$ but is generally
  larger than the ellipsoid (the ellipsoid is optimal).  The advantage of
  the parallelogram is that it is aligned with the principal axes of the
  covariance matrix.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{(3) Rectangular Confidence Region --- Conservative and Simple (Eq.\ 1.19)}
  The axis-aligned rectangle:
  \[
    \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{rec}} = [-c_1, c_1] \times \cdots \times [-c_d, c_d],
    \quad c_i = \sigma_i\,\Phi^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{\sqrt[d]{1-\alpha}+1}{2}\right),
  \]
  where $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i,i)}$ (sigma sub i) is the
  marginal standard deviation of coordinate $i$, satisfies
  $\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X} \in \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{rec}}) \geq 1-\alpha$
  by the \v{S}id\'ak inequality.  This region is \emph{conservative}:
  it generally achieves more than $1-\alpha$ coverage.  Its advantage is
  that it is simply $d$ independent confidence intervals --- one per
  component --- requiring no knowledge of the off-diagonal covariances.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{exampleblock}{Example 4.1.14: Covariance Matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 2 & 6\end{pmatrix}$, $\alpha = 0.05$}
  The eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ are found by solving
  $\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} - \lambda \mathbf{Id}) = 0$:
  $(1-\lambda)(6-\lambda) - 4 = 0 \Rightarrow \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 2 = 0$,
  giving $\lambda_{1,2} = (7 \pm \sqrt{41})/2$.  Numerically:
  $\lambda_1 \approx 6.7015$ and $\lambda_2 \approx 0.2984$.

  The corresponding eigenvectors (principal axes of the ellipsoid) are:
  $\mathbf{f}_1 \approx (-0.3310, -0.9436)^T$ and
  $\mathbf{f}_2 \approx (-0.9436, 0.3310)^T$.

  At $\alpha = 0.05$: $q_{0.95}(\chi_2^2) = 5.991$.

  \textbf{Ellipsoid:}  Principal axis half-lengths are
  $\sqrt{5.991 \times 6.7015} \approx 6.337$ (in direction $\mathbf{f}_1$)
  and $\sqrt{5.991 \times 0.2984} \approx 1.337$ (in direction $\mathbf{f}_2$).
  The ellipsoid is highly elongated: about 5 times longer in the
  $\mathbf{f}_1$ direction.

  \textbf{Parallelogram:}  The threshold is
  $z = \Phi^{-1}((\sqrt{0.95}+1)/2) = 2.2365$.
  The corners are $(\pm 2.2365, \pm 2.2365)$ in standardized space,
  mapped back through $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}$.

  \textbf{Rectangle:}  $c_1 = 1 \times 2.2365 = 2.2365$ (for the first
  coordinate with $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{1} = 1$) and
  $c_2 = \sqrt{6} \times 2.2365 \approx 5.478$ (for the second coordinate
  with $\sigma_2 = \sqrt{6}$).  The actual coverage of this rectangle is
  about 95.98\% --- slightly conservative, as expected.
\end{exampleblock}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.1.2.3 Confidence Regions for the Estimator $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R$}

In practice, we do not know $\mathbf{I}$ or $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}$
--- these are exactly what we are trying to learn from the simulation.
The multivariate CLT tells us that the estimator $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R$
is approximately $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{I}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}/R)$
for large $R$.  Replacing $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ by $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}/R$
in the formulae from the previous frame, the confidence ellipsoid for
the unknown mean $\mathbf{I}$ is centered at $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R$ with
principal axis half-lengths $\sqrt{q_{1-\alpha}(\chi_d^2)\,\lambda_i/R}$,
where $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}$
(Eq.\ 1.24).

Since $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}$ is unknown, we estimate it by
the \textbf{sample covariance matrix}:

\begin{block}{Sample Covariance Matrix (Eq.\ 1.25)}
  \[
    \hat{\mathbf{S}}_R^2 \;=\; \frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{k=1}^R
      \bigl(\mathbf{Y}_k - \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{Y}_k - \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R\bigr)^T.
  \]
  This is the unbiased estimator of the covariance matrix
  $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}$ (analogous to the scalar sample
  variance $\hat{S}_R^2$ in the one-dimensional case).  Its $(i,j)$-entry
  is the sample covariance between coordinates $i$ and $j$:
  $\hat{\mathbf{S}}_R^2(i,j) = \frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{k=1}^R
  (Y_k^{(i)} - \hat{Y}_R^{(i)})(Y_k^{(j)} - \hat{Y}_R^{(j)})$.
  Here $Y_k^{(i)}$ (Y sub k superscript i) is the $i$-th component of
  the $k$-th replication.
\end{block}

\textbf{Hotelling's $T^2$ (Remark 4.1.15).}
When the true covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}$ is replaced
by its estimate $\hat{\mathbf{S}}_R^2$, the statistic
$R(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R - \mathbf{I})^T(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_R^2)^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R - \mathbf{I})$
follows Hotelling's $T^2$-distribution (a multivariate generalisation of
the Student $t$-distribution).  For large $R$ this converges to the
$\chi_d^2$ distribution, justifying the use of chi-square quantiles in
constructing confidence regions when $R$ is large.

\framebreak

\begin{exampleblock}{Example 4.1.16 --- Heights and Weights ($R=1000$, $d=2$)}
  We use the SOCR HeightsWeights dataset (first 1000 entries) to
  illustrate the multivariate confidence ellipsoid.  Here
  $Y^{(1)}$ = weight (in lbs) and $Y^{(2)}$ = height (in inches).

  \textbf{Individual 95\% confidence intervals for each component:}

  For weight: sample mean $\hat{Y}_R^{(1)} = 127.48$ lbs,
  sample standard deviation $\hat{S}_R^{(1)} = 11.80$ lbs.
  Standard error of mean: $11.80/\sqrt{1000} \approx 0.373$.
  CI: $(127.48 - 1.96 \times 0.373,\; 127.48 + 1.96 \times 0.373) = (126.75,\; 128.21)$ lbs.

  For height: sample mean $\hat{Y}_R^{(2)} = 68.05$ inches,
  sample standard deviation $\hat{S}_R^{(2)} = 1.94$ inches.
  Standard error: $1.94/\sqrt{1000} \approx 0.061$.
  CI: $(67.93,\; 68.17)$ inches.

  \textbf{Joint confidence ellipsoid:}
  \[
    \hat{\mathbf{S}}_R^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 139.29 & 11.40 \\ 11.40 & 3.75 \end{pmatrix},
    \quad \lambda_1 = 140.24,\;\lambda_2 = 2.79.
  \]
  The dominant eigenvector $\mathbf{f}_1 \approx (0.9965, 0.0832)^T$
  points almost entirely in the weight direction --- weight varies much
  more than height between individuals.  With $q_{0.95}(\chi_2^2) = 5.991$
  and $R = 1000$, the ellipsoid axis half-lengths are
  $\sqrt{5.991 \times 140.24 / 1000} \approx 0.916$ lbs (weight direction)
  and $\sqrt{5.991 \times 2.79 / 1000} \approx 0.129$ inches (height direction).
  The confidence ellipse is highly elongated along the weight axis,
  reflecting the much greater variability in that dimension.
\end{exampleblock}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{4.1.2 Python: Multivariate Confidence Ellipsoid}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import chi2

def conf_ellipsoid(Y_samples, alpha=0.05):
    """Confidence ellipsoid for the mean of d-dimensional i.i.d. samples."""
    R, d = Y_samples.shape
    Y_bar = Y_samples.mean(axis=0)          # sample mean (d-vector)

    diff = Y_samples - Y_bar
    S2   = (diff.T @ diff) / (R - 1)        # sample covariance matrix (d x d)
    eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eigh(S2)   # eigendecomposition
    q = chi2.ppf(1 - alpha, df=d)           # chi-square quantile at level 1-alpha

    # Half-lengths of principal axes of the confidence ellipsoid
    # Each axis: sqrt(q * lambda_i / R) in direction eigvec[:,i]
    axis_lengths = np.sqrt(q * eigvals / R)
    return Y_bar, axis_lengths, eigvecs, S2

# Simulate 2D example: I = (2.0, 3.0), Sigma = [[1, 0.5],[0.5, 2]]
rng   = np.random.default_rng(42)
Sigma = np.array([[1., 0.5], [0.5, 2.]])
L     = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)           # Cholesky factor of Sigma
R     = 1000
Y     = (L @ rng.standard_normal((2, R))).T + np.array([2.0, 3.0])

print(f"Estimated mean       : {Y_bar}")
print(f"True mean            : [2.0, 3.0]")
print(f"Ellipsoid axis lengths: {axes}")

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.2 Estimation of $k(I)$: Motivation and Setup}

In many practical applications, the quantity we care about is not the
raw mean $I = \mathbb{E}Y$ but a nonlinear transformation of it.
Section 4.2 asks: if we can estimate $I$ well, how do we estimate
$z = k(I)$ for some prescribed smooth function $k$, and what is the
confidence interval?

Concrete examples where this arises:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Life insurance net premium:}  The premium for a whole-life
    policy at force of interest $r$ (r, a continuous interest rate) is
    $\Pi = r\,\mathbb{E}e^{-rT}/(1-\mathbb{E}e^{-rT}) = k(I)$, where
    $I = \mathbb{E}e^{-rT}$ and $T$ is the insured's future lifetime.
    We can simulate $T$ values and average $e^{-rT}$ to get $\hat{I}$,
    then plug into $k$ to get $\hat{\Pi}$.

  \item \textbf{Standard deviation:}  $k(I) = \sqrt{I}$ where
    $I = \mathbb{E}Y^2 - (\mathbb{E}Y)^2$ is the variance, estimated
    from simulation.

  \item \textbf{Ratio of means:}  $z = I^{(1)}/I^{(2)}$, e.g.\ expected
    total cost divided by expected throughput, where each expectation is
    estimated separately.
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Problem Setup and Natural Estimator}
  Let $k : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ be a prescribed continuous
  function (the transformation we apply), $\mathbf{I} = \mathbb{E}\mathbf{Y}$
  the true $d$-dimensional mean, and $\hat{\mathbf{X}}_R$ a strongly
  consistent estimator of $\mathbf{I}$ (meaning
  $\hat{\mathbf{X}}_R \to \mathbf{I}$ with probability 1).  The natural
  estimator of $z = k(\mathbf{I})$ is the \emph{plug-in estimator}
  $\hat{Z}_R = k(\hat{\mathbf{X}}_R)$.  The \textbf{asymptotic variance}
  of this estimator is defined as:
  \[
    \varsigma^2 = \lim_{R\to\infty} R\,\mathrm{Var}(\hat{Z}_R).
  \]
  Here $\varsigma$ (varsigma, a variant of sigma) is the asymptotic
  standard deviation constant, and $R\,\mathrm{Var}(\hat{Z}_R)$ is the
  variance scaled by $R$ --- which converges to a finite limit because
  variance decreases at rate $1/R$.
\end{block}

\textbf{Important caveat: the plug-in estimator is generally biased.}
Even when $\hat{X}_R$ is an unbiased estimator of $I$, the transformation
$k(\hat{X}_R)$ is generally \emph{biased} for $k(I)$ because of the
nonlinearity of $k$.  For example, with $k(y) = y^2$:
$\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}_R^2] = (\mathbb{E}Y)^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\hat{Y}_R)
= I^2 + \sigma_Y^2/R \neq I^2 = k(I)$.
The estimator systematically overestimates $I^2$.  However, this bias
is of order $O(R^{-1})$, while the standard deviation is $O(R^{-1/2})$,
so for large $R$ the bias is negligible compared to the uncertainty.
We can usually ignore the bias in practice.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.2.1 The Delta Method: 1D Case}

The \textbf{delta method} is the key mathematical tool for deriving the
confidence interval for $k(I)$.  The core idea is: if $k$ is smooth
and $\hat{Y}_R \approx I$ for large $R$, we can approximate $k(\hat{Y}_R)$
by a linear (first-order Taylor) expansion around $I$, because close to
$I$ the nonlinear function $k$ looks like a straight line.

\begin{block}{Taylor Expansion Argument}
  If $k$ is continuously differentiable near $I$ (meaning the derivative
  $k'$ exists and is continuous), then for $x$ near $I$:
  \[
    k(x) = k(I) + k'(I)(x - I) + o(x - I) \quad \text{as } x \to I.
  \]
  Here $o(x-I)$ (little-o notation) means a remainder term that is
  smaller than $|x-I|$ for small $|x-I|$ (it vanishes faster than
  linearly).  Substituting $x = \hat{Y}_R$ and multiplying both sides
  by $\sqrt{R}$:
  \[
    \sqrt{R}\bigl(k(\hat{Y}_R) - k(I)\bigr)
    = k'(I) \cdot \underbrace{\sqrt{R}\,(\hat{Y}_R - I)}_{\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\,\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_Y^2)}
    + \underbrace{\sqrt{R}\cdot o(\hat{Y}_R - I)}_{\to\,0\text{ in probability}}.
  \]
  The remainder vanishes: $\sqrt{R}\,|\hat{Y}_R - I|$ is bounded in
  probability (by the CLT), while the ratio $o(|\hat{Y}_R-I|)/|\hat{Y}_R-I| \to 0$.
  So the remainder $\sqrt{R}\cdot o(\hat{Y}_R - I) \to 0$ in probability.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Proposition 4.2.1 --- Delta Method, 1D Version (Eq.\ 2.2)}
  Let $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots$ be i.i.d.\ with mean $I = \mathbb{E}Y$,
  variance $\sigma_Y^2 = \mathrm{Var}(Y) < \infty$, and let $k$ be
  continuously differentiable at $I$ with $k'(I) \neq 0$.  Then:
  \[
    \sqrt{R}\,\bigl(k(\hat{Y}_R) - k(I)\bigr)
      \;\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\; \mathcal{N}(0, \varsigma^2),
    \quad \varsigma^2 = k'(I)^2\,\sigma_Y^2.
  \]
  Here $k'(I)$ (k prime of I, the derivative of $k$ evaluated at the
  true value $I$) acts as a magnification factor: where $k$ is steep
  (large $|k'(I)|$), a small error in estimating $I$ becomes a large
  error in $k(I)$; where $k$ is flat (small $|k'(I)|$), errors in $I$
  barely affect $k(I)$.  The factor $\sigma_Y^2$ is the variance of a
  single simulation output, measuring the intrinsic variability of
  the problem.
\end{block}

\framebreak

Since both $I$ and $\sigma_Y^2$ are unknown, we replace them by the
consistent estimators $\hat{Y}_R$ and $\hat{S}_R^2$.  By Slutsky's
theorem (continuous functions of consistent estimators are consistent),
this substitution preserves the asymptotic normal distribution, giving
us a practical, computable confidence interval:

\begin{block}{Practical CI for $k(I)$ at Level $1-\alpha$ (Eq.\ 2.3)}
  \[
    \left(
      k(\hat{Y}_R) - z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{\hat{S}_R\,|k'(\hat{Y}_R)|}{\sqrt{R}},\;\;
      k(\hat{Y}_R) + z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{\hat{S}_R\,|k'(\hat{Y}_R)|}{\sqrt{R}}
    \right). \tag{2.3}
  \]
  Here $k(\hat{Y}_R)$ (k of Y-hat sub R) is the point estimate of
  $k(I)$; $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ is the normal quantile (1.96 for 95\%);
  $\hat{S}_R$ is the sample standard deviation of the $Y_j$ values;
  $|k'(\hat{Y}_R)|$ (absolute value of k prime of Y-hat sub R) is the
  estimated derivative of $k$ at the estimated mean; and $\sqrt{R}$
  is the square root of the number of replications.

  The half-width $z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\hat{S}_R\,|k'(\hat{Y}_R)|/\sqrt{R}$
  is wide when $k$ is steep (large $|k'|$) and narrow when $k$ is flat
  (small $|k'|$).
\end{block}

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Delta Method CI}
  The confidence interval (2.3) has the same form as the basic CI (1.9)
  for a mean, but with the standard deviation $\hat{S}_R$ replaced by
  $\hat{S}_R\,|k'(\hat{Y}_R)|$.  The derivative factor $|k'(\hat{Y}_R)|$
  propagates the uncertainty in $\hat{Y}_R$ through the nonlinear
  transformation $k$.  A steep $k$ amplifies errors; a flat $k$
  suppresses them.  When $k'(I) = 0$ (the transformation is flat at the
  true value), the delta method gives a degenerate CI of zero width ---
  in that case higher-order terms matter and the method does not apply
  directly.
\end{alertblock}

\framebreak

\begin{exampleblock}{Example 4.2.2 --- Life Insurance Net Premium}
  The net premium for whole-life assurance at continuous interest rate
  $r$ (r, the force of interest, e.g.\ $r = 0.05$ for 5\% per year) is:
  \[
    \Pi = \frac{r\,\mathbb{E}e^{-rT}}{1 - \mathbb{E}e^{-rT}} = k(I),
  \]
  where $I = \mathbb{E}e^{-rT}$ and $T$ is the future lifetime of the
  insured (a random variable).  The factor $e^{-rT}$ (e to the power
  minus r times T) is the discount factor --- how much a payment of 1
  at time $T$ is worth today at interest rate $r$.

  We set $Y = e^{-rT}$, simulate many lifetimes $T_1, \ldots, T_R$,
  and average $e^{-rT_j}$ to get $\hat{Y}_R \approx I$.  The
  transformation is $k(x) = rx/(1-x)$, with derivative:
  $k'(x) = r/(1-x)^2$ (applying the quotient rule).

  The delta method CI (2.3) for the premium $\Pi = k(I)$ is:
  \[
    k(\hat{Y}_R) \;\pm\; \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\,r\,\hat{S}_R}{(1-\hat{Y}_R)^2\,\sqrt{R}}.
  \]
  Notice the $(1-\hat{Y}_R)^2$ term in the denominator.  As
  $\hat{Y}_R \to 1$ (which happens when $r$ is large or the expected
  lifetime $T$ is long, so $e^{-rT}$ is close to 1), the factor
  $(1-\hat{Y}_R)^2$ becomes very small, causing the CI to widen
  dramatically.  This reflects the mathematical fact that the premium
  $\Pi = rx/(1-x)$ is a rapidly increasing function near $x = 1$:
  a small uncertainty in $I$ translates into a large uncertainty in $\Pi$.
\end{exampleblock}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.2.1 The Multidimensional Delta Method}

When $\mathbf{I} = \mathbb{E}\mathbf{Y}$ is a $d$-dimensional vector
and $k : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ maps it to a scalar, the delta
method generalizes using the \emph{gradient} $\nabla k$ (nabla k,
also written grad k): the vector of all partial derivatives of $k$.

\begin{block}{Two-Dimensional Case (Proposition 4.2.3, Eq.\ 2.5--2.6)}
  Let $\mathbf{Y}_j = (Y_j^{(1)}, Y_j^{(2)})^T$ (a 2-component random
  vector), $\mathbf{I} = (I^{(1)}, I^{(2)})^T$, and $k(y_1, y_2)$
  continuously differentiable at $\mathbf{I}$.  The Taylor linearization
  becomes:
  \[
    k(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R) - k(\mathbf{I})
      \approx k'_{y_1}(\hat{Y}_R^{(1)} - I^{(1)}) + k'_{y_2}(\hat{Y}_R^{(2)} - I^{(2)}),
  \]
  where $k'_{y_i} = \partial k/\partial y_i\big|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{I}}$
  (the partial derivative of $k$ with respect to $y_i$, evaluated at the
  true value $\mathbf{I}$).  By the multivariate CLT and the delta method:
  \[
    \sqrt{R}\,(k(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R) - k(\mathbf{I}))
      \;\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\; \mathcal{N}(0, \varsigma^2),
  \]
  where the asymptotic variance $\varsigma^2$ is (Eq.\ 2.6):
  \[
    \varsigma^2 = (k'_{y_1})^2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}(1,1)
      + (k'_{y_2})^2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}(2,2)
      + 2k'_{y_1}k'_{y_2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}(1,2). \tag{2.6}
  \]
  Here $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}(i,j) = \mathrm{Cov}(Y^{(i)}, Y^{(j)})$
  is the $(i,j)$-entry of the covariance matrix.  The first two terms
  are the individual variance contributions (squared derivative times
  individual variance), and the third term is the cross-term arising
  from the correlation between components.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{General $d$-Dimensional Case (Proposition 4.2.8, Eq.\ 2.15)}
  For $k : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ continuously differentiable at
  $\mathbf{I}$, with gradient
  $\nabla k(\mathbf{I}) = (k^{(1)}(\mathbf{I}), \ldots, k^{(d)}(\mathbf{I}))$
  (the row vector of partial derivatives), the asymptotic variance is
  the quadratic form:
  \[
    \varsigma^2 = \nabla k(\mathbf{I})\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}\;(\nabla k(\mathbf{I}))^T
      = \sum_{i=1}^d\sum_{j=1}^d k^{(i)}(\mathbf{I})\,k^{(j)}(\mathbf{I})\,
        \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}(i,j). \tag{2.15}
  \]
  Here $k^{(i)}(\mathbf{I}) = \partial k/\partial x_i\big|_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{I}}$
  (partial derivative with respect to the $i$-th component, evaluated at
  $\mathbf{I}$), and the double sum accounts for all variances and
  covariances weighted by products of partial derivatives.
\end{block}

\framebreak

In practice we do not know $\mathbf{I}$ or $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}$,
so we substitute the consistent estimators $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R$ and
$\hat{\mathbf{S}}_R^2$.  The estimated asymptotic variance is (Eq.\ 2.16):
\[
  \hat{\varsigma}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^d\sum_{j=1}^d k^{(i)}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R)\,
    k^{(j)}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R)\,\hat{\mathbf{S}}_R^2(i,j).
\]
The practical confidence interval at level $1-\alpha$ for $k(\mathbf{I})$ is:
\[
  k(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R) \;\pm\; z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{\hat{\varsigma}}{\sqrt{R}}.
  \quad \text{(Eq.\ 2.8)}
\]

\begin{exampleblock}{Example 4.2.5 --- Ratio of Expectations $z = I^{(1)}/I^{(2)}$}
  We want to estimate $z = I^{(1)}/I^{(2)}$ (the ratio of two means),
  where both means are estimated from the same simulation runs as
  $\hat{Z}_R = \hat{Y}_R^{(1)}/\hat{Y}_R^{(2)}$.

  With $k(y_1, y_2) = y_1/y_2$, the partial derivatives are:
  $k'_{y_1} = 1/y_2$ and $k'_{y_2} = -y_1/y_2^2$.  Substituting
  into (2.6) and simplifying, the asymptotic variance becomes:
  \[
    \varsigma^2 = \frac{\mathbb{E}(Y^{(1)} - z\,Y^{(2)})^2}{(I^{(2)})^2}.
  \]
  This is estimated from data as (Eq.\ 2.13):
  \[
    \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{Z}_R)
      = \frac{1}{R^2\,(\hat{Y}_R^{(2)})^2}\sum_{i=1}^R
        (Y_i^{(1)} - \hat{Z}_R\,Y_i^{(2)})^2.
  \]
  The quantity $Y_i^{(1)} - \hat{Z}_R\,Y_i^{(2)}$ measures how much the
  $i$-th observation deviates from the estimated ratio --- small deviations
  mean the ratio is estimated precisely.  The CI is then:
  $\hat{Z}_R \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{Z}_R)}$.
\end{exampleblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.2.2 General Consistent Estimator and Asymptotic Bias}

The delta method extends beyond the sample mean to any strongly
consistent estimator $\hat{X}_R \to I$ satisfying two conditions:
\begin{enumerate}[(A1)]
  \item $\sqrt{R}(\hat{X}_R - \mathbb{E}\hat{X}_R)
    \xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$
    (asymptotically normal when centered at its \emph{own} mean,
    even if that mean is not exactly $I$).
  \item $\sqrt{R}(\mathbb{E}\hat{X}_R - I) \to 0$ as $R \to \infty$
    (the bias $\mathbb{E}\hat{X}_R - I$ vanishes faster than $1/\sqrt{R}$).
\end{enumerate}
Under (A1) and (A2) together, $\sqrt{R}(\hat{X}_R - I) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}
\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, and the delta method gives
$\sqrt{R}(k(\hat{X}_R) - k(I)) \xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}} \mathcal{N}(0, k'(I)^2\sigma^2)$.

\begin{block}{Asymptotic Bias of $\hat{Z}_R = k(\hat{X}_R)$ (Eq.\ 2.17)}
  Under the stronger assumption (A2$'$): $R(\mathbb{E}\hat{X}_R - I) \to \beta^X$
  (the bias of $\hat{X}_R$ is of exact order $1/R$ with constant
  $\beta^X$), the order-$1/R$ asymptotic bias of $\hat{Z}_R = k(\hat{X}_R)$
  is:
  \[
    \beta^Z = \beta^X\,k'(I) + \frac{\sigma^2\,k''(I)}{2}. \tag{2.17}
  \]
  Here $k''(I)$ (k double-prime of I) is the second derivative of $k$
  evaluated at the true value $I$.  The first term $\beta^X k'(I)$ simply
  propagates any existing bias of $\hat{X}_R$ through the transformation $k$.
  The second term $\sigma^2 k''(I)/2$ is the \emph{Jensen's inequality
  effect}: even if $\hat{X}_R$ is perfectly unbiased ($\beta^X = 0$),
  the curvature of $k$ (measured by $k''$) induces a bias in $k(\hat{X}_R)$.

  A bias-corrected estimator is $k(\hat{X}_R) - \hat{\beta}^Z/R$, but
  this correction is usually unnecessary because the bias $O(R^{-1})$
  is dominated by the standard deviation $O(R^{-1/2})$ for large $R$.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{4.2.3 Mean Square Error, Bias, and Variance (Eq.\ 2.19)}
  The \emph{mean square error} (MSE) of any estimator $\hat{X}_R$ of $I$
  decomposes into two parts:
  \[
    \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{X}_R)
      = \mathbb{E}(\hat{X}_R - I)^2
      = \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{X}_R)}_{\text{random fluctuation}}
        + \underbrace{(\mathbb{E}\hat{X}_R - I)^2}_{\text{squared systematic bias}}.
  \]
  Variance captures how much the estimator fluctuates randomly around
  its own mean.  Squared bias captures how far the estimator's mean is
  from the truth.  An unbiased estimator has MSE = Variance.  In
  general, there is a bias-variance tradeoff: techniques that reduce
  bias (like using $\hat{S}_R^2$ instead of $\hat{\sigma}_R^2$) may
  increase variance.
\end{block}

For large $R$, using $\lim_{R\to\infty} R\,\mathrm{Var}(\hat{X}_R) = \varsigma^2$
and $\lim_{R\to\infty} R(\mathbb{E}\hat{X}_R - I) = \beta$:
\[
  \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{X}_R) \approx \frac{\varsigma^2}{R} + \frac{\beta^2}{R^2}.
\]
The variance term is $O(R^{-1})$ and the squared bias is $O(R^{-2})$
--- the bias term is negligible for large $R$, justifying the common
practice of ignoring bias corrections.

\begin{exampleblock}{Example 4.2.9 --- $k(y) = y^2$, bias verification}
  Let $\hat{Z}_R = \hat{Y}_R^2$.  The derivatives are $k'(y) = 2y$
  and $k''(y) = 2$.  Since the sample mean $\hat{Y}_R$ is unbiased
  ($\beta^X = 0$), formula (2.17) gives:
  \[
    \beta^Z = 0 + \frac{\sigma_Y^2 \cdot 2}{2} = \sigma_Y^2.
  \]
  We can verify this directly:
  $\mathbb{E}(\hat{Y}_R)^2 = (\mathbb{E}\hat{Y}_R)^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\hat{Y}_R)
  = I^2 + \sigma_Y^2/R = k(I) + \sigma_Y^2/R$.
  So $\hat{Y}_R^2$ overestimates $I^2$ by $\sigma_Y^2/R$ on average ---
  this is the Jensen's inequality effect: $\mathbb{E}[X^2] \geq (\mathbb{E}X)^2$.
  The delta method asymptotic variance:
  $\varsigma^2 = (2I)^2\sigma_Y^2 = 4I^2\sigma_Y^2$.

  For 95\% CI: $\hat{Y}_R^2 \pm 1.96 \times 2|\hat{Y}_R|\,\hat{S}_R/\sqrt{R}$.
\end{exampleblock}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def delta_method_ci(Y_samples, k_func, k_prime, alpha=0.05):
    """
    Compute CI for k(I) using the delta method.
    Y_samples : 1D array of i.i.d. realizations of Y (E[Y]=I)
    k_func    : callable, the transformation k
    k_prime   : callable, derivative k' (evaluated at a scalar)
    """
    R     = len(Y_samples)
    Y_bar = Y_samples.mean()
    S     = Y_samples.std(ddof=1)    # sample std dev (ddof=1: divide by R-1)

    z_est     = k_func(Y_bar)                          # point estimate of k(I)
    asymp_std = abs(k_prime(Y_bar)) * S / np.sqrt(R)  # estimated half-width / z

    z_q = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)   # normal quantile; 1.96 for 95%
    return z_est, z_est - z_q * asymp_std, z_est + z_q * asymp_std

# Example: k(y) = y^2. Y ~ N(0.5, 0.3^2), so I=0.5 and k(I)=0.25.
# Delta method: varsigma^2 = (2*0.5)^2 * 0.09 = 0.09; std ~ 0.3/sqrt(R).
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
Y   = rng.normal(0.5, 0.3, size=1000)
est, lo, hi = delta_method_ci(
    Y, k_func=lambda y: y**2, k_prime=lambda y: 2*y)
print(f"k(I) estimate: {est:.4f}")
print(f"95% CI:        ({lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f})")
print(f"True k(I)=0.25. Bias: {est - 0.25:.4f}  (expect ~sigma^2/R = 0.09/1000)")
# Typical output:
# k(I) estimate: 0.2594
# 95% CI:        (0.2399, 0.2789)
\end{lstlisting}
\end{frame}

\section{4.3 Estimators of a Quantile}

%% ---- 4.3 intro & definition -----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.3 Estimating a Quantile: Motivation and Setup}

So far in this chapter we have estimated \emph{means}: $I = \mathbb{E}X$, the
theoretical average of a random variable.  A \textbf{quantile} is a fundamentally
different summary of a distribution.  The $p$-th quantile (for $p$ (p) in the
open interval $(0,1)$) is the value below which exactly a fraction $p$ of the
probability mass lies.

Quantiles arise everywhere in practice:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In \textbf{financial risk management}, the 99th percentile ($p = 0.99$)
    of the distribution of daily portfolio losses is called the
    \textbf{Value-at-Risk (VaR)}.  Banks are required by regulation to hold capital
    against this number: ``our loss will exceed this threshold on at most 1\% of
    trading days.''
  \item In \textbf{reliability engineering}, the 10th percentile ($p = 0.10$) of
    a component's lifetime distribution is the time by which 10\% of components
    will have failed --- a pessimistic but conservative design target.
  \item In \textbf{healthcare}, the 95th percentile of a biomarker distribution
    defines the upper limit of a ``normal'' reference range.
\end{itemize}

Because quantiles depend on the \emph{shape} of the entire distribution (not just
its mean), estimating them requires different tools than those developed in
Sections 4.1 and 4.2.

\begin{block}{Definition: The $p$-th Quantile (Eq.\ 3.1)}
  Let $X$ be a real-valued random variable with cumulative distribution function
  $F(x) = \mathbb{P}(X \leq x)$.  The \textbf{$p$-th quantile} (also called the
  $p$-th percentile when multiplied by 100) is the generalized inverse:
  \[
    q_p \;=\; F^{\leftarrow}(p) \;=\; \inf\!\bigl\{x \in \mathbb{R} : F(x) \geq p\bigr\}.
  \]
  The symbol $\inf$ means ``infimum'' (greatest lower bound).  For a
  \textbf{continuous} distribution function $F$ that is strictly increasing,
  $F^{\leftarrow}(p) = F^{-1}(p)$ is simply the unique solution to the equation
  $F(q_p) = p$.  In other words, $q_p$ is the value such that
  $\mathbb{P}(X \leq q_p) = p$ exactly.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Notation Explained}
  $F$ (capital F) --- the \textbf{cumulative distribution function} (CDF) of $X$;
  $F(x)$ gives the probability that $X$ does not exceed $x$.
  $p$ (p) --- the \textbf{quantile level}, a number strictly between 0 and 1;
  for example $p = 0.95$ means the 95th percentile.
  $q_p$ (q sub p) --- the \textbf{$p$-th quantile}: the threshold exceeded with
  probability $1-p$.
  $f(x)$ (lowercase f) --- the \textbf{probability density function} (PDF) of $X$;
  if it exists, $f(x) = F'(x)$ (the derivative of $F$).
\end{block}

\framebreak

Given an i.i.d.\ (independent and identically distributed) sample
$X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_R$ drawn from distribution $F$, we estimate $q_p$ using
the \textbf{sample quantile} (also called the \emph{empirical quantile}).

The first step is to \textbf{sort} the observations from smallest to largest:
\[
  X_{(1)} \;\leq\; X_{(2)} \;\leq\; \cdots \;\leq\; X_{(R)}.
\]
These sorted values are called \textbf{order statistics}.  Here $X_{(k)}$
(X sub (k) in parentheses) denotes the $k$-th smallest observation.
For instance, $X_{(1)}$ is the minimum and $X_{(R)}$ is the maximum.

\begin{block}{Sample Quantile Estimator}
  The empirical quantile function $\hat{F}_R^{\leftarrow}(p)$ is defined as:
  \[
    \hat{F}_R^{\leftarrow}(p) = \begin{cases}
      X_{(\lfloor pR \rfloor)} & \text{if } pR \text{ is a whole number,} \\
      X_{(\lfloor pR \rfloor + 1)} & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
  \]
  Here $\lfloor pR \rfloor$ (the \textbf{floor} of $pR$, read ``floor of p-times-R'')
  means the largest integer that does not exceed $pR$.  For example, if $R = 100$
  and $p = 0.9$, then $pR = 90$ is a whole number, so the estimator is $X_{(90)}$
  --- the 90th smallest observation.  If $R = 95$ and $p = 0.9$, then $pR = 85.5$
  and $\lfloor 85.5 \rfloor = 85$, so the estimator is $X_{(86)}$ --- the 86th
  smallest observation.
\end{block}

For the theoretical analysis that follows, we use the simplified estimator
$\hat{q}_p = X_{(\lfloor pR \rfloor)}$, which has the same asymptotic properties.

\begin{alertblock}{Intuition: Why the Order Statistic?}
  In a sample of $R$ observations, approximately $pR$ of them should fall below
  the true quantile $q_p$ (since each observation independently has probability $p$
  of lying below $q_p$).  So the $(pR)$-th smallest observation is a natural
  estimate of the value that separates the bottom fraction $p$ from the top
  fraction $1-p$ of the distribution.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

%% ---- 4.3.1 Asymptotic normality -------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.3.1 Asymptotic Normality of the Sample Quantile}

One of the most important results in statistics is that the sample quantile
$\hat{q}_p = X_{(\lfloor pR \rfloor)}$ is not only consistent (it converges to
the true $q_p$ as $R \to \infty$) but also \textbf{asymptotically normal}.  This
means that for large $R$, $\hat{q}_p$ has approximately a normal (bell-curve)
distribution centred at the true $q_p$.  This normality allows us to construct
confidence intervals using the same normal-quantile machinery we used for the
sample mean in Section 4.1.

\begin{block}{Proposition 4.3.1 --- Asymptotic Normality of the Sample Quantile (Eq.\ 3.2)}
  Assume the distribution $F$ has a density $f$ (lowercase f) that is continuous
  and strictly positive in an open neighbourhood of $q_p$ (i.e., $f(q_p) > 0$).
  Then:
  \begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item \textbf{Strong consistency:} $\hat{q}_p \to q_p$ with probability 1 as
      $R \to \infty$.  Running more simulations eventually pins down the quantile
      exactly.
    \item \textbf{Asymptotic normality:} as $R \to \infty$,
      \[
        \sqrt{R}\,(\hat{q}_p - q_p)
          \;\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\;
          \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\; \varsigma^2\right),
        \qquad \text{where } \varsigma^2 = \frac{p(1-p)}{f(q_p)^2}.
      \]
      The symbol $\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}$ means ``converges in distribution to.''
      $\mathcal{N}(0, \varsigma^2)$ (script N) is the normal distribution with
      mean 0 and variance $\varsigma^2$ (varsigma squared).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Notation for the Asymptotic Variance $\varsigma^2 = p(1-p)/f(q_p)^2$}
  $\varsigma^2$ (varsigma squared, sometimes written $\sigma_q^2$) --- the
  \textbf{asymptotic variance constant}; when we write $\sqrt{R}(\hat{q}_p - q_p)
  \to \mathcal{N}(0, \varsigma^2)$, the actual variance of $\hat{q}_p$ is
  approximately $\varsigma^2/R$ for large $R$.\\[4pt]
  $p$ (p) --- the quantile level in $(0,1)$.\\[4pt]
  $1-p$ (one minus p) --- the complementary probability; together, $p(1-p)$
  behaves like the variance of a Bernoulli($p$) random variable and is maximised
  at $p = 0.5$ (the median).\\[4pt]
  $f(q_p)$ (f of q-sub-p) --- the \textbf{density of $X$ evaluated at the
  quantile $q_p$}.  This is the height of the probability density curve at the
  point we are trying to estimate.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Formula $\varsigma^2 = p(1-p)/f(q_p)^2$}
  Think of $f(q_p)$ as measuring how ``crowded'' the distribution is near
  $q_p$.  When $f(q_p)$ is \textbf{large} (the density has a sharp peak near
  $q_p$, so many observations fall close to $q_p$), the denominator
  $f(q_p)^2$ is large and $\varsigma^2$ is small --- the sample quantile is
  easy to estimate accurately.

  When $f(q_p)$ is \textbf{small} (the distribution is spread out near $q_p$,
  with few observations falling close to it), $\varsigma^2$ is large and
  estimation is imprecise.  This happens especially in the tails of the
  distribution: at extreme quantiles like $p = 0.999$, the density $f(q_{0.999})$
  is very small, making the tail quantile hard to pin down.

  The numerator $p(1-p)$ is the binomial variance from asking ``did $X_i$ fall
  below $q_p$?'' (yes with probability $p$, no with probability $1-p$).  It
  peaks at the median ($p=0.5$, where $p(1-p) = 0.25$) and shrinks toward the
  tails, but the denominator $f(q_p)^2$ typically shrinks even faster, making
  tail quantiles harder overall.
\end{alertblock}

\framebreak

\textbf{Proof sketch of Proposition 4.3.1.}  The argument uses two steps.
Understanding these steps gives genuine insight into \emph{why} the formula is
what it is.

\medskip
\textbf{Step 1 --- Uniform order statistics (Lemma 4.3.2).}
If $U_1, U_2, \ldots, U_R$ are i.i.d.\ $\mathcal{U}[0,1]$ (uniform on the
interval $[0,1]$), the $k$-th order statistic $U_{(k)}$ (the $k$-th smallest
uniform value) follows a Beta$(k, R-k+1)$ distribution (Beta distribution with
parameters $k$ and $R-k+1$).  The Beta distribution has mean $k/(R+1)$ and
variance $k(R-k+1)/[(R+1)^2(R+2)]$.

Setting $k = \lfloor Rp \rfloor$ and applying the CLT to this Beta distribution
(using the fact that $\mathrm{Beta}(k, R-k+1)$ is the ratio of two Erlang sums,
which are themselves sums of exponential random variables):
\[
  \sqrt{R}\,(U_{(\lfloor Rp \rfloor)} - p)
    \;\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\;
    \mathcal{N}(0,\; p(1-p)).
\]
This says: the fraction of ``successes'' among order statistics is approximately
normal with variance $p(1-p)/R$ --- exactly the binomial variance we expect from
asking ``is $U_i \leq p$?''

\medskip
\textbf{Step 2 --- Transfer to a general distribution via the quantile transform.}
Any random variable $X$ with CDF $F$ can be written as $X \overset{d}{=} F^{-1}(U)$
where $U \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]$ (the \textbf{quantile transform}).  Therefore
$X_{(\lfloor Rp \rfloor)} \overset{d}{=} F^{-1}(U_{(\lfloor Rp \rfloor)})$.
Expanding $F^{-1}$ around $p$ using a Taylor (first-order) expansion:
\[
  F^{-1}(p + h) \;\approx\; F^{-1}(p) + h \cdot \frac{d}{dp}F^{-1}(p)
    = q_p + h \cdot \frac{1}{f(q_p)},
\]
where the last equality uses the fact that the derivative of the quantile function
$F^{-1}(p)$ equals $1/f(F^{-1}(p)) = 1/f(q_p)$ (this follows from
differentiating $F(F^{-1}(p)) = p$ with respect to $p$, giving
$f(F^{-1}(p)) \cdot (F^{-1})'(p) = 1$).

Setting $h = U_{(\lfloor Rp \rfloor)} - p$ and multiplying by $\sqrt{R}$:
\[
  \sqrt{R}(\hat{q}_p - q_p)
    \;\approx\;
    \frac{1}{f(q_p)} \cdot \underbrace{\sqrt{R}(U_{(\lfloor Rp \rfloor)} - p)}_{\to\,\mathcal{N}(0,\,p(1-p))}
    \;\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\;
    \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\; \frac{p(1-p)}{f(q_p)^2}\right).
\]
The $1/f(q_p)$ factor stretches the variance by $(1/f(q_p))^2$, yielding
$\varsigma^2 = p(1-p)/f(q_p)^2$. \qed

\framebreak

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Numerical Example: $X \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1)$, $p = 0.9$}
  The exponential distribution with rate 1 (mean 1) has:
  \[
    F(x) = 1 - e^{-x} \quad \text{and} \quad f(x) = e^{-x}, \quad x \geq 0.
  \]
  \textbf{Step 1 --- Find the true quantile $q_{0.9}$.}
  Solve $F(q_{0.9}) = 0.9$: $1 - e^{-q_{0.9}} = 0.9$, so
  $e^{-q_{0.9}} = 0.1$, giving
  $q_{0.9} = -\ln(0.1) = \ln(10) \approx 2.3026$.

  \textbf{Step 2 --- Evaluate the density at the quantile.}
  $f(q_{0.9}) = e^{-q_{0.9}} = e^{-\ln(10)} = 1/10 = 0.1$.

  \textbf{Step 3 --- Compute the asymptotic variance.}
  \[
    \varsigma^2 = \frac{p(1-p)}{f(q_p)^2}
      = \frac{0.9 \times 0.1}{(0.1)^2}
      = \frac{0.09}{0.01}
      = 9.
  \]

  \textbf{Step 4 --- Standard error and CI for $R = 1{,}000$ replications.}
  The standard error of $\hat{q}_{0.9}$ is approximately:
  $\varsigma/\sqrt{R} = 3/\sqrt{1000} \approx 0.0949$.

  For a 95\% confidence interval ($z_{0.975} = 1.96$):
  half-width $= 1.96 \times 0.0949 \approx 0.186$.
  CI: approximately $(2.3026 - 0.186,\; 2.3026 + 0.186) = (2.117,\; 2.489)$.

  \textbf{Interpretation.}  The asymptotic variance $\varsigma^2 = 9$ is large
  because the density at the 90th percentile ($f = 0.1$) is small --- the
  exponential distribution is quite spread out at its 90th percentile.  We need
  many observations to accurately locate this threshold.  By comparison, for the
  median ($p = 0.5$, where $q_{0.5} = \ln 2 \approx 0.693$ and $f(q_{0.5}) = 0.5$):
  $\varsigma^2 = 0.5 \times 0.5/0.5^2 = 1$ --- nine times smaller!  The median
  is much easier to estimate than the 90th percentile.
\end{exampleblock}

%% ---- 4.3.1 Estimating 1/f(q_p) via order statistics -----------------------
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.3.1 Building the Confidence Interval: Estimating the Density $f(q_p)$}

The asymptotic normality result (Proposition 4.3.1) gives us a confidence interval
for $q_p$, but only if we know the asymptotic standard deviation $\varsigma = \sqrt{p(1-p)}/f(q_p)$.
The numerator $\sqrt{p(1-p)}$ is known exactly because $p$ is specified by us.
The denominator $f(q_p)$ is unknown and must be estimated from the data.

The key mathematical insight is that the \textbf{quantile function} $Q(p) = F^{-1}(p)$
satisfies a beautiful relationship between its derivative and the density.
Differentiating $F(F^{-1}(p)) = p$ with respect to $p$ gives:
\[
  f(q_p) \cdot Q'(p) = 1
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  Q'(p) = \frac{1}{f(Q(p))} = \frac{1}{f(q_p)}.
\]
So the quantity we need, $1/f(q_p)$, is simply the \textbf{derivative of the
quantile function} $Q'(p)$ at the point $p$.

This turns the problem of density estimation into a problem of \textbf{derivative
estimation}, which can be done via a finite-difference approximation:
\[
  Q'(p) \approx \frac{Q(p + m/R) - Q(p - m/R)}{2m/R},
\]
for a small ``bandwidth'' $m$ (m, a positive integer).  We replace the true
quantile values $Q(p \pm m/R)$ with their empirical counterparts --- the
corresponding order statistics.

\begin{block}{Order-Statistic Estimator of $Q'(p) = 1/f(q_p)$ (Proposition 4.3.4, Eq.\ 3.3)}
  Given sorted observations $X_{(1)} \leq X_{(2)} \leq \cdots \leq X_{(R)}$,
  choose a bandwidth integer $m = m_R$ (m sub R, depending on $R$).  Define:
  \[
    \hat{Q}'_{p,R} \;=\; \frac{R}{2m}
      \Bigl(X_{(\lfloor pR \rfloor + m)} - X_{(\lfloor pR \rfloor - m + 1)}\Bigr).
      \tag{3.3}
  \]
  \textbf{How to read this:}
  The upper order statistic $X_{(\lfloor pR \rfloor + m)}$ estimates the
  quantile $Q(p + m/R)$ (the level slightly above $p$).
  The lower order statistic $X_{(\lfloor pR \rfloor - m + 1)}$ estimates
  $Q(p - m/R)$ (the level slightly below $p$).
  Dividing their difference by $2m/R$ approximates the derivative $Q'(p)$.
  The prefactor $R/(2m)$ equals $1/(2m/R)$, the reciprocal of the step size.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Notation}
  $m_R$ (m sub R) --- the \textbf{bandwidth}, a positive integer that controls
  the width of the finite-difference window; it must grow as $R$ grows, but
  more slowly than $R$ itself.\\[4pt]
  $X_{(\lfloor pR \rfloor + m)}$ --- the order statistic at index $\lfloor pR \rfloor + m$;
  this is $m$ positions to the \emph{right} (larger side) of the sample quantile.\\[4pt]
  $X_{(\lfloor pR \rfloor - m + 1)}$ --- the order statistic at index $\lfloor pR \rfloor - m + 1$;
  this is approximately $m$ positions to the \emph{left} (smaller side) of the
  sample quantile.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Consistency of the Estimator (Proposition 4.3.4)}
  If $f$ has a continuous derivative near $q_p$ (the density curve is smooth
  at the quantile) and the bandwidth satisfies:
  \[
    m_R \to \infty \quad \text{and} \quad m_R = o(R) \quad \text{as } R \to \infty,
  \]
  (meaning: $m_R$ grows without bound, but grows strictly slower than $R$),
  then $\hat{Q}'_{p,R} \to 1/f(q_p)$ with probability 1.
  In plain terms: as long as we use a window that grows but not too fast, the
  estimator converges to the true value of $1/f(q_p)$.
\end{block}

\textbf{Choosing the bandwidth $m_R$ --- two options:}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simple default rule: $m_R = \lfloor\sqrt{R}\rfloor$} (floor of the
    square root of $R$).  This satisfies both conditions automatically: $\sqrt{R} \to \infty$
    and $\sqrt{R}/R = 1/\sqrt{R} \to 0$.  For $R = 1{,}000$: $m = 31$.
    For $R = 10{,}000$: $m = 100$.  This is the recommended starting point.
  \item \textbf{MSE-optimal rule (Bloch--Gastwirth): $m_R \propto R^{4/5}$} (R to the
    power four-fifths).  This minimises the asymptotic Mean Squared Error (MSE)
    of $\hat{Q}'_{p,R}$, giving MSE $\sim C \cdot R^{-2/5}$.  The rate $R^{-2/5}$
    is slower than $R^{-1/2}$ (the usual Monte Carlo rate), reflecting the
    fundamental difficulty of density estimation from samples.  In practice, the
    constant $C$ depends on the unknown second derivative of $f$ near $q_p$, so
    this optimal bandwidth is mainly of theoretical interest.
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Confidence Interval for $q_p$ (combining Propositions 4.3.1 and 4.3.4)}
  Substituting $\hat{Q}'_{p,R}$ as our estimate of $1/f(q_p)$ into the asymptotic
  normality result:
  \[
    \hat{q}_p \;\pm\; z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{R}}\;\hat{Q}'_{p,R},
  \]
  where $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ (z sub one-minus-alpha-over-two) is the standard normal
  quantile (for 95\% CI: $z_{0.975} = 1.96$), $p(1-p)$ (p times one-minus-p) is
  the binomial variance factor, and $\hat{Q}'_{p,R}$ estimates $1/f(q_p)$.
  This confidence interval has coverage converging to $1-\alpha$ as $R \to \infty$.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Numerical Example: $X \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1)$, $p = 0.9$, $R = 1{,}000$}
  We have $R = 1000$ i.i.d.\ observations from $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$.  Sort them to
  get $X_{(1)} \leq \cdots \leq X_{(1000)}$.  Set $p = 0.9$ and use bandwidth
  $m = \lfloor\sqrt{1000}\rfloor = 31$.

  \textbf{Sample quantile index:} $\lfloor 0.9 \times 1000 \rfloor = 900$, so
  $\hat{q}_{0.9} = X_{(900)}$.

  \textbf{Estimate of $1/f(q_p)$:}
  $\hat{Q}'_{0.9,1000} = \frac{1000}{2 \times 31}\,(X_{(931)} - X_{(870)})
  = \frac{1000}{62}\,(X_{(931)} - X_{(870)})$.

  In a typical simulation, one might find $X_{(870)} \approx 2.21$ and
  $X_{(931)} \approx 2.42$, giving
  $\hat{Q}'_{0.9,1000} \approx \frac{1000}{62} \times 0.21 \approx 3.39$.
  (The true value is $1/f(q_{0.9}) = 1/0.1 = 10$; with $m=31$ the estimate
  is imprecise --- this is the inherent difficulty of density estimation ---
  but it improves as $R$ grows.)

  \textbf{CI half-width:}
  $1.96 \times \sqrt{0.9 \times 0.1/1000} \times 3.39
  \approx 1.96 \times 0.0300 \times 3.39 \approx 0.199$.

  \textbf{Confidence interval:} $(X_{(900)} - 0.199,\; X_{(900)} + 0.199)$.
  If $X_{(900)} \approx 2.30$, this gives approximately $(2.10,\; 2.50)$, which
  does contain the true value $\ln 10 \approx 2.303$.
\end{exampleblock}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{4.3 Python: Sample Quantile and Confidence Interval}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def quantile_ci(X_samples, p, m=None, alpha=0.05):
    """
    Estimate the p-quantile and construct a (1-alpha) confidence interval.

    Parameters
    ----------
    X_samples : 1D numpy array of i.i.d. observations from unknown CDF F
    p         : float in (0, 1), the desired quantile level
    m         : int, bandwidth for density estimate (default: floor(sqrt(R)))
    alpha     : float, significance level (default: 0.05 for a 95% CI)

    Returns
    -------
    q_hat : float -- sample quantile estimate
    lo    : float -- lower confidence limit
    hi    : float -- upper confidence limit
    """
    R        = len(X_samples)
    X_sorted = np.sort(X_samples)         # sort ascending: X_(1) <= ... <= X_(R)
    idx      = int(np.floor(p * R))       # index of sample quantile (0-based)
    q_hat    = X_sorted[idx]              # sample quantile = X_(floor(pR))

    if m is None:
        m = max(1, int(np.floor(np.sqrt(R))))   # simple bandwidth: sqrt(R)

    # Clamp indices to valid range [0, R-1]
    lo_idx = max(0, idx - m + 1)
    hi_idx = min(R - 1, idx + m)

    # Estimate 1/f(q_p) = Q'(p) via finite difference of order statistics
    # Q'(p) ~ (X_(idx+m) - X_(idx-m+1)) / (2m/R) = R/(2m) * (X_hi - X_lo)
    Q_prime_hat = R * (X_sorted[hi_idx] - X_sorted[lo_idx]) / (2 * m)

    z_q    = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)           # z_{1-alpha/2}
    margin = z_q * np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / R) * Q_prime_hat
    return q_hat, q_hat - margin, q_hat + margin

# ---- Demonstration: X ~ Exp(1), p = 0.9, R = 1000 -------------------------
# True quantile: q_{0.9} = -ln(0.1) = ln(10) ~ 2.3026
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
X   = rng.exponential(1.0, size=1000)

q_hat, lo, hi = quantile_ci(X, p=0.9)
print(f"q_0.9 estimate : {q_hat:.4f}")
print(f"95% CI         : ({lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f})")
print(f"True q_0.9     : {np.log(10):.4f}")

# Compare bandwidths m = sqrt(R) vs m = R^{4/5}
R = len(X)
for m_label, m_val in [("sqrt(R)", int(np.sqrt(R))),
                        ("R^0.8",  int(R**0.8))]:
    _, lo2, hi2 = quantile_ci(X, p=0.9, m=m_val)
    print(f"  m = {m_label} ({m_val:3d}):  CI = ({lo2:.4f}, {hi2:.4f})")
\end{lstlisting}

\section{4.4 More on Absolute vs Relative Errors}

%% ---- 4.4.1 The Chernoff Bound ---------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.4.1 The Chernoff Bound: Exponential Concentration}

In Section 4.1 we compared two ways to control absolute error: Chebyshev's
inequality and the CLT.  Now we study a third, sharper tool called the
\textbf{Chernoff bound}, which gives exponentially small error probabilities.
Unlike Chebyshev (which gives bounds that shrink as $1/R$, a ``polynomial'' rate),
the Chernoff bound gives bounds that shrink as $e^{-cR}$ (an ``exponential'' rate
in $R$).  For even moderately large $R$, this exponential decay is astronomically
smaller than any polynomial decay.

The Chernoff bound applies specifically when the random variables $Y_i$ are
Bernoulli (each $Y_i$ is either 0 or 1), which is the case for many Monte Carlo
estimators --- for example, the hit-or-miss estimator for computing areas or
probabilities.

\begin{block}{Setup: Bernoulli Sums}
  Let $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_R$ be i.i.d.\ Bernoulli$(p)$ random variables.
  This means each $Y_i$ equals 1 with probability $p$ (p, the success probability)
  and equals 0 with probability $1-p$.  The sum $S_R = Y_1 + Y_2 + \cdots + Y_R$
  counts the total number of ``successes'' in $R$ trials.  The expectation of $S_R$
  is $\mathbb{E}S_R = Rp$, and the sample mean $\hat{Y}_R = S_R/R$ estimates $p$.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Proposition 4.4.1 --- The Chernoff Bound (Eqs.\ 4.1--4.3)}
  For any $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$ (epsilon, the relative error tolerance):

  \textbf{Upper tail} (overestimation):
  \[
    \mathbb{P}(S_R \geq (1+\varepsilon)\,Rp)
      \;\leq\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^2 Rp}{2+\varepsilon}\right)
      \;\leq\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^2 Rp}{3}\right). \tag{4.1}
  \]
  \textbf{Lower tail} (underestimation):
  \[
    \mathbb{P}(S_R \leq (1-\varepsilon)\,Rp)
      \;\leq\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^2 Rp}{2}\right). \tag{4.2}
  \]
  \textbf{Two-sided} (combining both tails):
  \[
    \mathbb{P}\!\left(|S_R - Rp| \geq \varepsilon\,Rp\right)
      \;\leq\; 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^2 Rp}{3}\right). \tag{4.3}
  \]
  Here $\exp(x)$ (exponential function) means $e^x$ where $e \approx 2.718$.
  The condition $|S_R - Rp| \geq \varepsilon Rp$ is equivalent to
  $|\hat{Y}_R - p| \geq \varepsilon p$ --- a \textbf{relative error} of at
  least $\varepsilon$ in the estimate of $p$.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Notation for the Chernoff Bound}
  $\varepsilon$ (epsilon) --- the relative error tolerance; we want
  $|\hat{Y}_R/p - 1| \leq \varepsilon$.\\[4pt]
  $p$ (p) --- the true success probability, equal to $\mathbb{E}Y_i$.\\[4pt]
  $(1+\varepsilon)Rp$ (one-plus-epsilon times R-p) --- the threshold
  above which $S_R$ counts as a significant overestimate.\\[4pt]
  $e^{-\varepsilon^2 Rp/3}$ (e to the negative epsilon-squared-R-p-over-three) ---
  the bounding probability, which decays exponentially as $R$ or $p$ grows.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Chernoff Bound: Exponential vs Polynomial Decay}
  The key behaviour is in the factor $e^{-\varepsilon^2 Rp/3}$.  As $R$ grows by 1,
  the bound is multiplied by $e^{-\varepsilon^2 p/3}$, which is a number strictly
  less than 1.  This means the probability of error shrinks by a constant factor
  with each additional replication --- ``exponential decay.''

  In contrast, Chebyshev's bound is $\sigma^2/(R \cdot t^2)$, which shrinks only as
  $1/R$ --- ``polynomial decay.''  For large $R$, exponential decay is
  incomparably smaller.  For example, with $p = 0.5$ and $\varepsilon = 0.5$:
  at $R = 1{,}000$, Chebyshev gives $\sim 4/1000 = 0.004$, while Chernoff gives
  $\sim 2e^{-41.7} \approx 10^{-18}$.  That is 15 orders of magnitude smaller.
\end{alertblock}

\textbf{Proof sketch of the Chernoff bound (the ``moment generating function'' trick).}

The proof uses Markov's inequality in a clever way.  For any $s > 0$ (s, called
the ``tilting parameter''):
\[
  \mathbb{P}(S_R \geq x) = \mathbb{P}(e^{sS_R} \geq e^{sx})
  \;\leq\; \frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{sS_R}]}{e^{sx}},
\]
using Markov's inequality $\mathbb{P}(Z \geq c) \leq \mathbb{E}Z/c$ applied to
$Z = e^{sS_R} \geq 0$ and $c = e^{sx}$.  Now we compute $\mathbb{E}[e^{sS_R}]$
(the \textbf{moment generating function} of $S_R$):
\[
  \mathbb{E}[e^{sS_R}] = \prod_{i=1}^R \mathbb{E}[e^{sY_i}]
  = (pe^s + 1-p)^R
  \leq e^{Rp(e^s - 1)},
\]
where the inequality uses $1 + t \leq e^t$ for all $t$ (applied with $t = p(e^s-1)$).
Choosing $s = \ln(1+\varepsilon)$ to minimise the bound over $s$ and using the
algebraic inequality $\ln(1+\varepsilon) \geq \varepsilon^2/(2+\varepsilon)$
yields (4.1).  For the lower tail (4.2), apply the same trick to $e^{-sS_R}$.

\framebreak

\begin{block}{Corollary 4.4.2 --- Required Replications for Relative Error Control (Eq.\ 4.4)}
  We want $\mathbb{P}(|\hat{Y}_R - p| \geq \varepsilon p) \leq \delta$ (delta,
  the permitted failure probability).  Setting the Chernoff bound (4.3) equal
  to $\delta$ and solving for $R$:
  \[
    2\exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^2 Rp}{3}\right) \leq \delta
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    R \geq \frac{3\ln(2/\delta)}{\varepsilon^2 p}.
    \tag{4.4}
  \]
  Here $\ln(2/\delta)$ (natural logarithm of two-over-delta) is the log of the
  reciprocal of half the failure probability; $\varepsilon^2$ (epsilon squared)
  is the squared relative tolerance; and $p$ (p) is the true Bernoulli
  success probability.
\end{block}

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read Eq.\ (4.4): The $1/p$ Factor}
  Equation (4.4) says: for relative error control, the required sample size is
  \emph{inversely proportional to $p$}.  When $p = 0.1$ (a moderately rare event),
  we need $10\times$ more replications than when $p = 1$ (certain event).
  When $p = 10^{-6}$ (an extremely rare event), we need $10^6\times$ more
  replications.  This is the fundamental difficulty of rare-event simulation.
\end{alertblock}

\begin{exampleblock}{Numerical Comparison: Three Bounds for Bernoulli$(1/2)$, $\varepsilon = 1/2$}
  We compare $\mathbb{P}(|S_R - R/2| \geq R/4)$ (the event that the sample mean
  is off by more than 25\% from its true value $p = 1/2$) under three bounds,
  for $R = 1{,}000$:
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      Bound & Formula & Value at $R = 1{,}000$ \\
      \midrule
      \textbf{Chebyshev} & $4/R$ & $4/1{,}000 = 0.004$ \\
      \textbf{Chernoff} & $2e^{-R/24}$ & $2e^{-41.7} \approx 1.7 \times 10^{-18}$ \\
      \textbf{CLT approx.} & $\sqrt{8/(\pi R)}\,e^{-R/8}$ & $\approx 9 \times 10^{-55}$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  The Chebyshev bound (0.004) is 15 orders of magnitude worse than the Chernoff
  bound, which is itself $10^{36}$ times worse than the CLT approximation.
  For all practical purposes, once $R$ exceeds a few hundred, the probability of
  a 25\% relative error with $p = 1/2$ is negligibly small --- the CLT tells us
  this with great precision.
\end{exampleblock}

%% ---- 4.4.2 Absolute vs relative errors for Bernoulli -----------------------
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.4.2 Absolute vs Relative Error: A Striking Contrast}

We now compare the sample sizes required for absolute and relative error control,
using the concrete case of i.i.d.\ Bernoulli$(p)$ observations
($Y_i \in \{0, 1\}$, $\mathbb{E}Y_i = p$, $\mathrm{Var}(Y_i) = p(1-p)$).
We fix $\varepsilon = \delta = 0.01$ throughout (1\% error tolerance, 99\%
confidence).

\medskip
\textbf{Absolute error first.}
We want $\mathbb{P}(|\hat{Y}_R - p| \geq \varepsilon) \leq \delta$, i.e., the
absolute discrepancy between our estimate and the truth is at most 0.01 with
probability at least 99\%.

Using the CLT formula (Eq.\ 1.7) with $\sigma_Y^2 = \mathrm{Var}(Y) = p(1-p)$,
$z_{0.995} = 2.576$ (the 99.5th percentile of the standard normal, since
$1 - \delta/2 = 1 - 0.005 = 0.995$):
\[
  R^{\mathrm{abs}} = z_{1-\delta/2}^2 \cdot \frac{p(1-p)}{\varepsilon^2}
    = 2.576^2 \cdot \frac{p(1-p)}{(0.01)^2}
    \approx 6{,}634 \cdot p(1-p).
\]

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{cccc}
    \toprule
    $p = I$ (true probability) & $p(1-p)$ (variance) & $R^{\mathrm{abs}}$ & Interpretation \\
    \midrule
    $0.5$   & $0.250$  & $\approx 1{,}659$  & common probability \\
    $0.1$   & $0.090$  & $\approx 597$     & moderately rare \\
    $0.01$  & $0.0099$ & $\approx 66$      & rare \\
    $0.001$ & $\approx 0.001$ & $\approx 7$ & very rare \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

This is counterintuitive: \emph{smaller} $p$ requires \emph{fewer} replications
for absolute error control!  For $p = 0.001$, just 7 replications suffice
(theoretically) to ensure the estimate is within $\pm 0.01$ of the truth.

\framebreak

\textbf{Why absolute error control fails for rare events.}
Consider the extreme case $p = 0.001$ and a single replication ($R = 1$).
The estimator $\hat{Y}_1$ takes only two values: 0 (with probability 0.999)
or 1 (with probability 0.001).  The outcome $\hat{Y}_1 = 0$ (which happens
$99.9\%$ of the time) gives $|\hat{Y}_1 - p| = |0 - 0.001| = 0.001 \leq 0.01 = \varepsilon$.
So the absolute error condition is satisfied!  But the estimate (0) tells us
essentially nothing about the true value (0.001) --- we only know it is
``small,'' not by how much.

\begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Absolute Error Is Misleading for Rare Events}
  When $p$ is small, an estimate of 0 (``event never occurred'') is within
  $\varepsilon = 0.01$ of the truth in absolute terms.  But it gives zero
  information about the \emph{magnitude} of $p$.  The absolute error criterion
  (E1) is satisfied trivially, yet the estimate is practically useless.
  This is why we need relative error control for rare events.
\end{alertblock}

\medskip
\textbf{Relative error corrects this.}
We want $\mathbb{P}(|\hat{Y}_R/p - 1| \geq \varepsilon) \leq \delta$, i.e., the
estimate must be within $\pm 1\%$ of the true value \emph{in percentage terms}.
An estimate of 0 when $p = 0.001$ has \emph{100\% relative error} and fails
this condition catastrophically.

Using Corollary 4.4.2 (the Chernoff bound, Eq.\ 4.4) with $\varepsilon = \delta = 0.01$:
\[
  R \geq \frac{3\ln(2/0.01)}{(0.01)^2 \cdot p}
    = \frac{3\ln(200)}{0.0001 \cdot p}
    = \frac{3 \times 5.298}{0.0001 \cdot p}
    \approx \frac{158{,}940}{p}.
\]

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{cc}
    \toprule
    $p = I$ & Required $R$ for relative error ($\varepsilon = \delta = 0.01$) \\
    \midrule
    $0.5$       & $\approx 318{,}000$ \\
    $0.1$       & $\approx 1.59 \times 10^6$ \\
    $0.01$      & $\approx 1.59 \times 10^7$ \\
    $0.001$     & $\approx 1.59 \times 10^8$ \\
    $10^{-6}$   & $\approx 1.59 \times 10^{11}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\framebreak

The contrast between the two tables is dramatic.  For absolute error control with
$p = 0.001$: only 7 replications needed.  For relative error control with
$p = 0.001$: $1.59 \times 10^8$ replications needed --- an increase of
$\sim 23$ million times.

\begin{alertblock}{The Fundamental Hardness of Rare-Event Simulation}
  For relative error control of a Bernoulli$(p)$ estimator:
  required $R \propto 1/p$.  Halving $p$ doubles the required sample size.
  As $p \to 0$ (event becomes rarer), $R \to \infty$ proportionally.

  At $p = 10^{-6}$: even if each simulation takes $1$ nanosecond, $10^{11}$
  replications would take $10^{11}$ nanoseconds $= 100$ seconds.  At $p = 10^{-9}$,
  this becomes $10^{14}$ ns $= $ over a day.  For very rare events (e.g.,
  $p = 10^{-12}$ for catastrophic failure probabilities in aerospace engineering),
  naive Monte Carlo is completely impractical.

  The solution is \textbf{variance reduction} (Chapter 5).  Techniques such as
  \textbf{importance sampling} replace the original distribution with one that
  makes the rare event common, then correct for the change.  An efficient
  importance sampling estimator can achieve relative error control with $R$
  independent of $p$ --- breaking the $1/p$ bottleneck.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

%% ---- 4.4.3 Absolute vs relative for pi -------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.4.3 Absolute vs Relative Error for Estimating $\pi$ (pi)}

As a concrete bridge between the theory and practice, we now work through the
complete sample-size calculation for estimating $I = \pi$ (pi, the mathematical
constant $\approx 3.14159$).  Since $\pi$ is of order 1 (it is neither tiny
nor huge), we expect both error criteria to give manageable sample sizes ---
but relative error will still require somewhat more replications.

We use the \textbf{hit-or-miss estimator} from Example 4.1.6:
$Y_j = 4 \cdot \mathbf{1}(U_{2j-1}^2 + U_{2j}^2 \leq 1)$,
where $U_{2j-1}$ and $U_{2j}$ are i.i.d.\ $\mathcal{U}[0,1]$.
Here $\mathbf{1}(\cdot)$ (indicator function) equals 1 if the point
$(U_{2j-1}, U_{2j})$ lands inside the unit quarter-circle, 0 otherwise.
This gives $\mathbb{E}Y = \pi$ and $\mathrm{Var}(Y) = 4\pi(4-\pi) \approx 2.697$.

We fix $\varepsilon = \delta = 0.01$ (1\% accuracy, 99\% confidence throughout).

\begin{block}{Sample Size for Absolute Error via CLT (Eq.\ 1.7)}
  We want $\mathbb{P}(|\hat{Y}_R - \pi| \geq \varepsilon) \leq \delta$
  with $\varepsilon = 0.01$, i.e., the estimate is within $\pm 0.01$ of $\pi$.
  The CLT formula gives:
  \[
    R^{\mathrm{abs}} = z_{0.995}^2 \cdot \frac{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}{\varepsilon^2}
      = 2.576^2 \times \frac{2.697}{(0.01)^2}
      \approx 6.636 \times 26{,}970
      \approx 1.79 \times 10^5.
  \]
  So approximately \textbf{179,000 replications} are needed to guarantee that
  the estimate is within 0.01 of $\pi$ with 99\% confidence.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Sample Size for Relative Error via Chernoff (Eq.\ 4.4)}
  We want $\mathbb{P}(|\hat{Y}_R - \pi|/\pi \geq \varepsilon) \leq \delta$
  with $\varepsilon = 0.01$.  Using the indicator $Y'_j = \mathbf{1}(U_{2j-1}^2+U_{2j}^2\leq 1)$
  (which has $\mathbb{E}Y'_j = \pi/4 \approx 0.7854$), the Chernoff bound (4.4)
  with $p = \pi/4$ gives:
  \[
    R^{\mathrm{rel,1}} \geq \frac{3\ln(2/0.01)}{(0.01)^2 \cdot (\pi/4)}
      = \frac{3 \times 5.298}{0.0001 \times 0.7854}
      \approx \frac{15.894}{0.0000785}
      \approx 2.03 \times 10^5.
  \]
  The estimate of $\pi/4$ must be within $\pm 1\%$ of its true value.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Sample Size for Relative Error via CLT}
  Equivalently, we require $|\hat{Y}_R - \pi| \leq \varepsilon \pi = 0.01\pi \approx 0.03142$
  (making the absolute tolerance match 1\% relative).  Using the CLT formula
  with this tighter absolute tolerance:
  \[
    R^{\mathrm{rel,2}} = z_{0.995}^2 \cdot \frac{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}{(\varepsilon\pi)^2}
      = 2.576^2 \times \frac{2.697}{(0.01)^2 \times \pi^2}
      \approx \frac{1.79 \times 10^5}{9.870}
      \approx 1.81 \times 10^4.
  \]
  Wait --- the Chernoff-based bound ($2.03 \times 10^5$) is \emph{larger} than
  the CLT-based bound ($1.81 \times 10^4$)?  Yes: Chernoff is valid for any $R$
  but is conservative; the CLT is sharper (it accounts for the Gaussian shape
  of the distribution) but is only asymptotically valid.  For $\pi \approx 3.14$,
  both bounds are manageable.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{alertblock}{Comparison: $\pi$ vs a Rare Event with $p = 10^{-6}$}
  For $\pi$: relative error requires $\sim 2\times$ more replications than
  absolute error.  For a rare event with $p = 10^{-6}$: absolute error requires
  only a handful of replications (trivially satisfied), while relative error
  requires $\sim 1.6 \times 10^{11}$ replications.  The contrast is staggering.

  The reason for the stark difference: $\pi \approx 3.14$ is not small, so the
  relative-error condition $|\hat{I}/I - 1| \leq \varepsilon$ does not demand
  much more than the absolute-error condition $|\hat{I} - I| \leq \varepsilon$.
  But for $p = 10^{-6}$, requiring $|\hat{I}/p - 1| \leq 0.01$ means the
  estimate must be accurate to within $10^{-8}$ in absolute terms --- far harder
  than the absolute-error condition $|\hat{I} - p| \leq 0.01$ (which is trivially
  satisfied by estimating 0 every time).
\end{alertblock}

%% ---- 4.4.4 Variance and both error criteria ---------------------------------
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.4.4 How Variance Influences Both Error Criteria}

We now place the two error criteria side-by-side in a unified framework, using
the CLT approximation throughout.  For large $R$, the estimator $\hat{I} = \hat{Y}_R$
is approximately normally distributed: $\hat{I} \approx \mathcal{N}(I, \sigma_Y^2/R)$,
where $\sigma_Y^2 = \mathrm{Var}(Y)$ (sigma sub Y squared, the variance of a single
simulation output).  This approximation captures both error criteria.

\begin{block}{Required Replications: Unified Comparison}
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{lcc}
      \toprule
      Error criterion & Condition & Required $R$ \\[3pt]
      \midrule
      \textbf{Absolute} & $\mathbb{P}(|\hat{I} - I| \geq \varepsilon) \leq \delta$
        & $R = z_{1-\delta/2}^2\;\dfrac{\sigma_Y^2}{\varepsilon^2}$ \\[8pt]
      \textbf{Relative} & $\mathbb{P}(|\hat{I}/I - 1| \geq \varepsilon) \leq \delta$
        & $R = z_{1-\delta/2}^2\;\dfrac{\sigma_Y^2}{\varepsilon^2 I^2}$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  Here $\varepsilon$ (epsilon) is the error tolerance, $\delta$ (delta) is the
  failure probability, $z_{1-\delta/2}$ (z sub one-minus-delta-over-two) is the
  standard normal quantile, $\sigma_Y^2$ is the single-sample variance, and
  $I$ (capital I) is the true quantity being estimated.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Notation}
  $\sigma_Y^2$ (sigma sub Y squared) --- the variance of a single simulation
  output; this is the key ``noise level'' of the problem.  Reducing $\sigma_Y^2$
  directly reduces the required $R$.\\[4pt]
  $\varepsilon^2 I^2$ (epsilon squared times I squared) --- the denominator for
  relative error; it includes the extra factor $I^2$ (I squared), which becomes
  tiny when $I$ is small, dramatically increasing the required $R$.\\[4pt]
  The ratio of the two required sample sizes is:
  $R_{\mathrm{rel}} / R_{\mathrm{abs}} = 1/I^2$,
  which grows as $I \to 0$.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read This: The Role of Variance Reduction}
  Reducing the variance $\sigma_Y^2$ by a factor of $c$ reduces the required $R$
  by the same factor $c$ under \emph{both} error criteria.  This is why variance
  reduction techniques (importance sampling, stratified sampling, control variates,
  antithetic variates --- all developed in Chapter 5) are valuable: they change the
  estimator from $Y$ to a lower-variance $Y^*$, keeping the mean $\mathbb{E}Y^* = I$
  the same but shrinking $\sigma_{Y^*}^2$.

  For relative error with small $I$:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Naive CMC (Crude Monte Carlo) requires $R \sim \sigma_Y^2/(\varepsilon^2 I^2)$;
      since $\sigma_Y^2 \geq I(1-I) \approx I$ for Bernoulli variables, this gives
      $R \gtrsim 1/(\varepsilon^2 I)$, growing unboundedly as $I \to 0$.
    \item A good importance sampling estimator can achieve
      $\sigma_{Y^*}^2 \propto I^2$, giving $R \sim 1/\varepsilon^2$ --- independent
      of $I$.  This is called \textbf{bounded relative error}: even as $I \to 0$,
      a fixed number of replications suffices.
  \end{itemize}
  Without variance reduction, naive Monte Carlo is fundamentally unsuited for
  rare events.  With good variance reduction, the same framework applies.
\end{alertblock}

\begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: Comparing Estimators for $I = 0.001$}
  Suppose $I = 0.001$ (the probability of a rare defect in manufacturing).
  We want 1\% relative accuracy ($\varepsilon = 0.01$) with 99\% confidence
  ($\delta = 0.01$, so $z_{0.995} = 2.576$).

  \textbf{Naive Bernoulli estimator:} $Y_i \in \{0,1\}$, $\sigma_Y^2 = I(1-I) \approx 0.001$.
  \[
    R_{\mathrm{naive}} = 2.576^2 \times \frac{0.001}{(0.01)^2 \times (0.001)^2}
      \approx 6.636 \times 10^{10}.
  \]
  Over 66 billion replications --- completely impractical.

  \textbf{Perfect importance sampling:} $\sigma_{Y^*}^2 = c \cdot I^2 = c \times 10^{-6}$
  for some constant $c$ (say $c = 1$ for a good estimator).
  \[
    R_{\mathrm{IS}} = 2.576^2 \times \frac{I^2}{(0.01)^2 \times I^2}
      = 2.576^2 \times \frac{1}{(0.01)^2}
      = 2.576^2 \times 10{,}000
      \approx 66{,}355.
  \]
  Only about 66,000 replications --- a factor of $10^6$ improvement.
\end{exampleblock}

\end{frame}

%% ---- 4.4 Python code -------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[fragile]{4.4 Python: Comparing Bounds and Sample Size Calculations}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# ---- Sample size formulas -----------------------------------------------
def R_abs_clt(sigma2, epsilon, delta):
    """
    Required R for ABSOLUTE error: P(|Y_bar - I| >= epsilon) <= delta.
    Uses the CLT formula: R = z^2 * sigma^2 / epsilon^2.
    sigma2  : Var(Y), the single-sample variance
    epsilon : absolute error tolerance
    delta   : failure probability (e.g. 0.01 for 99% confidence)
    """
    z = stats.norm.ppf(1 - delta / 2)   # z_{1 - delta/2}
    return int(np.ceil(z**2 * sigma2 / epsilon**2))

def R_rel_clt(sigma2, I, epsilon, delta):
    """
    Required R for RELATIVE error: P(|Y_bar/I - 1| >= epsilon) <= delta.
    Uses CLT: R = z^2 * sigma^2 / (epsilon^2 * I^2).
    I : the true value being estimated
    """
    z = stats.norm.ppf(1 - delta / 2)
    return int(np.ceil(z**2 * sigma2 / (epsilon**2 * I**2)))

def R_rel_chernoff(p, epsilon, delta):
    """
    Required R for relative error, Chernoff bound (Bernoulli case).
    Formula: R >= 3 * ln(2/delta) / (epsilon^2 * p).
    Only valid when Y_i ~ Bernoulli(p).
    """
    return int(np.ceil(3 * np.log(2 / delta) / (epsilon**2 * p)))

# ---- Comparison for pi estimation ---------------------------------------
VarY  = 16 * (np.pi / 4) * (1 - np.pi / 4)  # Var(Y) for hit-or-miss ~ 2.697
I     = np.pi                                  # true value
eps   = 0.01; delt = 0.01

print("=== Estimating pi: eps=delta=0.01 ===")
print(f"Absolute error (CLT)        : R = {R_abs_clt(VarY, eps, delt):,}")
print(f"Relative error (Chernoff)   : R = {R_rel_chernoff(np.pi/4, eps, delt):,}")
print(f"Relative error (CLT)        : R = {R_rel_clt(VarY, I, eps, delt):,}")

# ---- Comparison for rare event p = 0.001 --------------------------------
p = 0.001
VarY_rare = p * (1 - p)   # Var(Bernoulli(p))
print(f"\n=== Rare event p=0.001: eps=delta=0.01 ===")
print(f"Absolute error (CLT)        : R = {R_abs_clt(VarY_rare, eps, delt):,}")
print(f"Relative error (Chernoff)   : R = {R_rel_chernoff(p, eps, delt):,}")
print(f"Relative error (CLT)        : R = {R_rel_clt(VarY_rare, p, eps, delt):,}")
# Observe: absolute error needs very few R (misleadingly small for rare events),
# while relative error needs enormous R -> motivates importance sampling.
\end{lstlisting}
\end{frame}

\section{4.5 Conclusions}

%% ---- 4.5.1 Fundamental relationship revisited -----------------------------
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.5 Conclusions: The Fundamental Relationship Revisited}

Chapter 4 has developed a complete toolkit for quantifying the accuracy of Monte
Carlo estimates based on independent replications.  Every result in this chapter,
regardless of what quantity is being estimated (mean, transformed mean, or
quantile), leads back to a single unifying equation.

\begin{block}{The Fundamental Relationship (Eq.\ 5.1)}
  \[
    b \;=\; \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\; D}{\sqrt{R}},
  \]
  where:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $b$ (b) is the \textbf{confidence interval half-width}: the radius of the
      symmetric confidence interval centred at the point estimate.
      Smaller $b$ means higher precision.
    \item $R$ (capital R) is the \textbf{number of independent replications}
      (simulations) we run.  The error shrinks as $1/\sqrt{R}$.
    \item $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ (z sub one-minus-alpha-over-two) is the
      \textbf{standard normal quantile} at level $1-\alpha/2$.
      For 95\% confidence: $\alpha = 0.05$, $z_{0.975} = 1.96$.
      For 99\% confidence: $\alpha = 0.01$, $z_{0.995} = 2.576$.
    \item $D$ (capital D) is the \textbf{problem's variability constant},
      measuring the inherent difficulty of the estimation problem (unrelated
      to how many replications we run).  See below for its form in each case.
  \end{itemize}
\end{block}

\begin{block}{The Variability Constant $D$ for Each Estimation Problem}
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      Quantity being estimated & Variability constant $D$ & Section \\
      \midrule
      $I = \mathbb{E}Y$ (1D mean) &
        $D = \sigma_Y = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}$ & 4.1 \\[4pt]
      $k(I)$, $I = \mathbb{E}Y$ (transformed mean) &
        $D = |k'(I)|\;\sigma_Y$ & 4.2 \\[4pt]
      $k(\mathbf{I})$, $\mathbf{I} = \mathbb{E}\mathbf{Y}$ ($d$-dim.\ transformed mean) &
        $D = \sqrt{\nabla k(\mathbf{I})\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}\;(\nabla k(\mathbf{I}))^T}$ & 4.2 \\[4pt]
      $q_p$ (the $p$-th quantile) &
        $D = \sqrt{p(1-p)}\;/\;f(q_p)$ & 4.3 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  Here $\sigma_Y = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}$ (sigma sub Y) is the standard deviation
  of a single output; $k'(I)$ (k prime of I) is the derivative of the
  transformation; $\nabla k(\mathbf{I})$ (nabla k of I) is the gradient vector;
  $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}$ (bold Sigma sub Y) is the covariance matrix;
  $f(q_p)$ (f of q sub p) is the density at the quantile; and $p(1-p)$
  (p times one-minus-p) is the binomial variance factor.
\end{block}

\framebreak

\begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Fundamental Relationship}
  Rearranging $b = z_{1-\alpha/2} D/\sqrt{R}$ gives two equivalent forms:
  \[
    R = z_{1-\alpha/2}^2 \frac{D^2}{b^2}
    \qquad \text{and} \qquad
    D = \frac{b\sqrt{R}}{z_{1-\alpha/2}}.
  \]
  The first form answers: ``How many replications do I need?''  The second
  answers: ``What variability level can I tolerate for a given budget?''

  The crucial property is that $b$ shrinks as $1/\sqrt{R}$.  This has two
  important implications:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Diminishing returns:} To halve the error $b$, you must
      \emph{quadruple} $R$.  There is no escaping this --- it is a mathematical
      law, not a limitation of any particular algorithm.
    \item \textbf{No curse of dimensionality:} The rate $b = O(R^{-1/2})$ holds
      regardless of the dimension $d$ of the problem (whether $\mathbf{I}$ is
      1-dimensional or 1000-dimensional).  Deterministic integration methods
      (such as the trapezoidal rule) achieve $O(R^{-2/d})$ --- exponentially
      slower in high dimensions.  Monte Carlo's $O(R^{-1/2})$ rate is the
      reason it dominates in high-dimensional integration.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{alertblock}

\framebreak

\textbf{Practical workflow for a Monte Carlo study.}
The fundamental relationship guides a systematic four-step approach to any
Monte Carlo estimation task.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Define the estimator.}  Express the quantity of interest as
    $I = \mathbb{E}Y$, or as $k(I)$ or $q_p$, and identify the corresponding
    variability constant $D$ (using the table above).

  \item \textbf{Pilot simulation.}  Run a small ``pilot'' batch of $m$ replications
    (typically $m = 200$ to $1{,}000$) to estimate $D$ from the data.
    For a 1D mean: estimate $\sigma_Y$ by the sample standard deviation $\hat{S}_m$.
    For a transformed mean: additionally compute $|k'(\hat{Y}_m)|$.
    For a quantile: additionally compute $\hat{Q}'_{p,m}$ via order statistics.

  \item \textbf{Compute required $R$.}  Plug the pilot estimates into
    $R = \lceil z_{1-\alpha/2}^2 \hat{D}^2/b^2 \rceil$ (ceiling function, rounded
    up to the next integer).

  \item \textbf{Main simulation and report.}  Run $R$ replications; compute the
    point estimate and its confidence interval using the formulas in the summary
    block below.  Report both.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{block}{Summary of Confidence Intervals at 95\% Confidence ($\alpha = 0.05$, $z_{0.975} = 1.96$)}
  \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      Quantity & Point Estimate & 95\% Confidence Interval \\
      \midrule
      $I = \mathbb{E}Y$
        & $\hat{Y}_R$
        & $\hat{Y}_R \;\pm\; \dfrac{1.96\;\hat{S}_R}{\sqrt{R}}$ \\
      $k(I)$, $I = \mathbb{E}Y$
        & $k(\hat{Y}_R)$
        & $k(\hat{Y}_R) \;\pm\; \dfrac{1.96\;\hat{S}_R\;|k'(\hat{Y}_R)|}{\sqrt{R}}$ \\
      $k(\mathbf{I})$, $\mathbf{I} = \mathbb{E}\mathbf{Y}$
        & $k(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R)$
        & $k(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R) \;\pm\; 1.96\;\dfrac{\hat{\varsigma}}{\sqrt{R}}$ \\
      $q_p$ (quantile)
        & $X_{(\lfloor pR \rfloor)}$
        & $\hat{q}_p \;\pm\; 1.96\;\sqrt{\dfrac{p(1-p)}{R}}\;\hat{Q}'_{p,R}$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  In the third row, $\hat{\varsigma}^2 = \sum_{i,j} k^{(i)}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R)
  \,k^{(j)}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_R)\,\hat{\mathbf{S}}_R^2(i,j)$
  is the estimated asymptotic variance from the multi-D delta method (Eq.\ 2.16).
\end{block}

\end{frame}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def full_mc_analysis(simulate_Y, R_pilot=500, b=0.05, alpha=0.05, seed=42):
    """
    Complete four-step Monte Carlo output analysis pipeline.

    Parameters
    ----------
    simulate_Y : callable, takes an np.random.Generator and returns one float Y
    R_pilot    : int, number of pilot replications for variance estimation
    b          : float, desired CI half-width
    alpha      : float, significance level (e.g. 0.05 for 95% CI)
    seed       : int, random seed for reproducibility

    Returns
    -------
    Y_bar : float -- point estimate of I = E[Y]
    ci    : tuple (lo, hi) -- confidence interval
    """
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    # --- Step 1: Pilot simulation to estimate sigma^2 = Var(Y) ---------------
    Y_pilot    = np.array([simulate_Y(rng) for _ in range(R_pilot)])
    sigma2_hat = Y_pilot.var(ddof=1)    # unbiased estimate: divide by R_pilot - 1
    print(f"Pilot sigma^2 = {sigma2_hat:.4f}  (from {R_pilot} pilot runs)")

    # --- Step 2: Required R from the fundamental relationship -----------------
    # b = z * sigma / sqrt(R)  =>  R = z^2 * sigma^2 / b^2
    z      = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)     # z_{1 - alpha/2}
    R_main = int(np.ceil(z**2 * sigma2_hat / b**2))
    print(f"Required R for b={b}, alpha={alpha}: R = {R_main:,}")

    # --- Step 3: Main simulation ---------------------------------------------
    Y_main = np.array([simulate_Y(rng) for _ in range(R_main)])
    Y_bar  = Y_main.mean()
    S      = Y_main.std(ddof=1)

    # --- Step 4: Report point estimate and CI --------------------------------
    half_width = z * S / np.sqrt(R_main)
    ci_lo = Y_bar - half_width
    ci_hi = Y_bar + half_width
    print(f"Point estimate: {Y_bar:.4f}")
    print(f"Actual half-width achieved: {half_width:.4f}  (target: {b})")
    print(f"95% CI: ({ci_lo:.4f}, {ci_hi:.4f})")
    return Y_bar, (ci_lo, ci_hi)

# ---- Application: estimate pi using hit-or-miss estimator ----------------
# E[Y] = pi, Var(Y) = 16*(pi/4)*(1-pi/4) ~ 2.697
def sim_pi(rng):
    u = rng.random(2)
    return 4.0 * float(u[0]**2 + u[1]**2 <= 1)

est, ci = full_mc_analysis(sim_pi, R_pilot=1000, b=0.05)
print(f"True pi = {np.pi:.4f}; contained in CI: {ci[0] <= np.pi <= ci[1]}")
\end{lstlisting}

%% ---- 4.5 Final key takeaways -----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{4.5 Key Takeaways of Chapter 4}

This frame collects the six central lessons of Chapter 4.  Understanding these
deeply provides the foundation for all subsequent variance reduction and advanced
simulation methodology.

\begin{alertblock}{1. The Monte Carlo Rate Is Universal and Dimension-Free}
  The confidence interval half-width satisfies $b = z_{1-\alpha/2} D/\sqrt{R}$,
  so $b$ decreases as $O(R^{-1/2})$ regardless of the dimension $d$ of the
  problem.  This is the decisive advantage of Monte Carlo over deterministic
  numerical integration, which suffers the \emph{curse of dimensionality} (the
  number of function evaluations needed for fixed accuracy grows exponentially
  with $d$ for grid-based methods).  For high-dimensional problems ($d \geq 5$),
  Monte Carlo is often the only practical approach.
\end{alertblock}

\begin{alertblock}{2. CLT Is Much Sharper Than Chebyshev}
  For 95\% confidence, the CLT-based confidence interval has half-width
  $z_{0.975}\sigma_Y/\sqrt{R} = 1.96\sigma_Y/\sqrt{R}$, while the Chebyshev bound
  gives $\sigma_Y/(\sqrt{0.05}\sqrt{R}) \approx 4.47\sigma_Y/\sqrt{R}$.
  The ratio is $1.96/4.47 \approx 0.438$, meaning the CLT-based required sample
  size is $(0.438)^2 \approx 19\%$ of the Chebyshev required sample size ---
  a $\approx 5\times$ reduction.  The price is that the CLT holds only
  asymptotically (for large $R$), whereas Chebyshev is exact for all $R$.
  In practice, the CLT approximation is excellent for $R \geq 30$ and the
  distribution of $Y$ is not extremely heavy-tailed.
\end{alertblock}

\framebreak

\begin{alertblock}{3. The Delta Method for Transformed Estimators}
  When the quantity of interest is $z = k(I)$ rather than $I$ itself, the
  natural estimator $k(\hat{Y}_R)$ is generally biased.  The bias is of order
  $O(R^{-1})$ and equals $\beta^Z = \sigma_Y^2 k''(I)/2$ (when $\hat{Y}_R$ is
  unbiased), from Jensen's inequality.  However, the standard deviation of
  $k(\hat{Y}_R)$ is of order $O(R^{-1/2})$, which dominates the bias for large
  $R$.  Therefore bias corrections are typically unnecessary, and the delta method
  CI (Eq.\ 2.3) has the correct asymptotic coverage.

  The delta method replaces the asymptotic variance $\sigma_Y^2$ by
  $k'(I)^2 \sigma_Y^2$: the transformation amplifies uncertainty where $k$ is
  steep ($|k'(I)|$ large) and compresses it where $k$ is flat ($|k'(I)|$ small).
\end{alertblock}

\begin{alertblock}{4. Quantile Estimation: Density Matters}
  Estimating the $p$-th quantile $q_p$ is harder than estimating a mean.
  The asymptotic variance is $\varsigma^2 = p(1-p)/f(q_p)^2$, which grows
  large when the density $f(q_p)$ is small (quantile is in a sparse region
  of the distribution).  The density must be estimated from the data using an
  order-statistic finite-difference formula with bandwidth $m_R \sim \sqrt{R}$
  (simple) or $m_R \sim R^{4/5}$ (MSE-optimal).  The resulting confidence
  interval converges at the usual Monte Carlo rate $O(R^{-1/2})$, but its
  asymptotic variance constant $\varsigma^2$ is typically larger than for mean
  estimation.
\end{alertblock}

\framebreak

\begin{alertblock}{5. Relative Error Is Exponentially Harder Than Absolute Error for Rare Events}
  For a Bernoulli$(p)$ estimator:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Absolute error ($|\hat{Y}_R - p| \leq \varepsilon$): requires
      $R \sim p(1-p)/\varepsilon^2$, which \emph{decreases} as $p \to 0$.
      This is misleading --- a trivial estimate of 0 satisfies the criterion.
    \item Relative error ($|\hat{Y}_R/p - 1| \leq \varepsilon$): requires
      $R \sim 1/(\varepsilon^2 p)$, which \emph{increases} as $p \to 0$.
      For $p = 10^{-6}$ and $\varepsilon = 0.01$: over $10^{11}$ replications
      needed --- completely infeasible for naive Monte Carlo.
  \end{itemize}
  Relative error is the correct criterion for rare events.  Satisfying it with
  naive Monte Carlo is impossible for very small $p$; this \emph{motivates all
  of Chapter 5} on variance reduction, particularly importance sampling.
\end{alertblock}

\begin{alertblock}{6. Multivariate Confidence Regions Have Three Shapes}
  When estimating a vector mean $\mathbf{I} = \mathbb{E}\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^d$:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ellipsoid} (Eq.\ 1.15): the smallest region achieving
      coverage $1-\alpha$ exactly; requires the full covariance matrix
      $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}$ and its eigendecomposition.
    \item \textbf{Parallelogram} (Eq.\ 1.18): slightly larger, aligned with
      the eigenvectors of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Y}}$; also achieves
      exactly $1-\alpha$.
    \item \textbf{Rectangle} (Eq.\ 1.19): largest, axis-aligned (ignores
      correlations); coverage is at least $1-\alpha$ (conservative), but
      easiest to compute and communicate.
  \end{itemize}
  As the dimension $d$ increases, all three regions grow in size because the
  chi-squared quantile $q_{1-\alpha}(\chi_d^2)$ increases with $d$, reflecting
  the increased difficulty of simultaneously estimating more components.
\end{alertblock}

\end{frame}
