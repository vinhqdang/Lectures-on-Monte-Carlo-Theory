\section{Introduction and Motivation}
% ==================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{What Is Bracketing and Why Does It Matter?}

  Optimization problems ask: ``Where does this function achieve its smallest
  value?''  A naive approach would be to evaluate the function at thousands of
  random locations, but this is wasteful.  \textbf{Bracketing} gives us a
  smarter strategy: rather than searching the entire real line, we first identify
  a \emph{region} (an interval) that is guaranteed to contain the minimum, and
  then progressively \emph{shrink} that region until the minimum is pinpointed
  to whatever precision we desire.

  \begin{block}{Definition: Bracketing}
    \textbf{Bracketing} is the process of identifying an interval $[a,\, c]$ ---
    read ``the closed interval from $a$ (the left endpoint) to $c$ (the right
    endpoint), inclusive'' --- within which a local minimum of the function $f$
    is guaranteed to lie, and then progressively narrowing that interval.
    The notation $[a, c]$ means all real numbers $x$ satisfying $a \le x \le c$.
  \end{block}

  Bracketing applies exclusively to \textbf{univariate functions} --- functions
  that take a single real-valued input $x$ and return a single real-valued
  output $f(x)$.  The letter $x$ represents the variable we are optimising over,
  and $f(x)$ represents the ``cost'' or ``objective'' we want to minimise.
  If we know the minimum is inside $[a, c]$, every evaluation of $f$ at an
  interior point tells us something about the shape of $f$ and lets us
  \emph{eliminate} part of the interval.  Repeating this procedure drives the
  interval length toward zero.

  Derivative information --- the slope $f'(x)$ of the function at a point,
  read ``f prime of x'' --- can accelerate the search when it is available, but
  several of the methods in this chapter work with \textbf{function values alone}.
  They treat $f$ as a ``black box'' that can only be queried at chosen points,
  with no access to slope information.  This is particularly valuable when $f$
  comes from a simulation, a physical experiment, or any other process where
  computing the derivative is impractical.

  The methods in this chapter form the critical foundation for
  \textbf{line search} in multi-variable optimisation (covered in later chapters):
  when we minimise a function of many variables by moving along one direction at a
  time, each move reduces to a one-dimensional problem of exactly the type studied
  here.

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight}
    We do not need to evaluate $f$ everywhere.  A handful of cleverly placed
    evaluations, combined with the right structural assumption about $f$
    (such as \emph{unimodality}, defined on the next slide), is enough to
    locate the minimum accurately.  The art of bracketing is choosing
    \emph{where} to evaluate in order to learn the most from each query.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{Unimodality}
% ==================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{3.1\ \ Unimodality: The Key Assumption}

  Before we can safely discard part of a bracketing interval, we need a
  structural guarantee that discarding it will not throw away the true minimum.
  The assumption that provides this guarantee is called \emph{unimodality}.

  \begin{block}{Definition: Unimodal Function}
    A function $f$ is called \textbf{unimodal} (from Latin: \emph{unus} = one,
    \emph{modus} = mode or peak) if it has \emph{exactly one} local minimum,
    and the function strictly decreases on one side of that minimum and strictly
    increases on the other.  More precisely, there exists a unique point $x^{*}$
    (read ``$x$ star''; the superscript $*$ is a conventional symbol meaning
    ``optimal'') such that:
    \[
      f(x_1) > f(x_2) \;\text{ for all }\; x_1 < x_2 \le x^{*}
      \qquad\text{and}\qquad
      f(x_1) < f(x_2) \;\text{ for all }\; x^{*} \le x_1 < x_2.
    \]
    In plain language: the function consistently decreases as we approach
    $x^{*}$ from the left, and consistently increases as we move away from
    $x^{*}$ to the right.  There are no other ``dips'' or ``bumps''.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for Unimodal Definition}
    $x^{*}$ (x star): the unique global minimiser --- the point where $f$
    achieves its smallest value.\\
    $x_1, x_2$ (x sub 1, x sub 2): two arbitrary points in the domain used
    to express the monotonicity condition.  The subscripts 1 and 2 label two
    distinct points, not specific values.\\
    $f(x_1) > f(x_2)$: the function value at $x_1$ is strictly greater than
    at $x_2$, meaning $x_1$ is ``higher up'' than $x_2$.\\
    The symbol $\le$ means ``less than or equal to'' and $<$ means ``strictly
    less than''.
  \end{block}

  A helpful mental image: think of a smooth valley --- like the letter ``U''
  --- with a unique lowest point at the bottom.  A function such as
  $f(x) = (x - 2)^2$ is unimodal with minimum at $x^{*} = 2$: moving away
  from $x = 2$ in either direction causes $f$ to increase.

  \begin{block}{Definition: Multimodal Function}
    A function is called \textbf{multimodal} (from Latin: \emph{multi} = many)
    if it has more than one local minimum --- multiple ``valleys'' or ``dips''.
    A classic example is $f(x) = \sin(x)$ (read ``sine of x'', a trigonometric
    function that oscillates between $-1$ and $+1$): it achieves the value $-1$
    at infinitely many points.  The bracketing refinement methods (Fibonacci,
    golden section, quadratic fit) in this chapter apply only to
    \textbf{unimodal} functions.  For multimodal functions, different strategies
    (such as the Shubert-Piyavskii method, also in this chapter) are needed.
  \end{block}

  Unimodality gives us a powerful \emph{elimination rule}: if we find three
  points $a$, $b$, $c$ arranged in increasing order (so $a < b < c$, meaning
  $a$ is to the left of $b$, which is to the left of $c$) such that the
  \emph{middle} value is the lowest, then the minimum must lie strictly between
  the outer two points.

  \begin{block}{Definition: Bracketing Triple}
    Given a unimodal function $f$, an ordered triple of points $(a, b, c)$
    with $a < b < c$ is called a \textbf{bracketing triple} if:
    \[
      f(a) > f(b) \qquad \text{and} \qquad f(b) < f(c).
    \]
    The two conditions together say that the middle point $b$ produces a
    \emph{lower} function value than both outer points $a$ and $c$.  The
    notation $f(a)$ means ``the function $f$ evaluated at the point $a$''.
    When both inequalities hold, the global minimum of $f$ is guaranteed to
    lie inside the open interval $(a, c)$ --- that is, somewhere strictly
    between $a$ and $c$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for Bracketing Triple}
    $a$ (left endpoint): the leftmost of the three points, a real number.\\
    $b$ (middle point): the interior point that achieves the lowest function
    value; $a < b < c$.\\
    $c$ (right endpoint): the rightmost point; $c > b > a$.\\
    $f(a), f(b), f(c)$: the function values at the three points.  These are
    real numbers (positive, negative, or zero).\\
    $(a, c)$ with round brackets (as opposed to $[a, c]$ with square brackets):
    the \emph{open} interval, meaning all $x$ with $a < x < c$ (endpoints
    excluded).
  \end{block}

  Why does the bracketing triple guarantee the minimum is in $(a, c)$?
  Because $f$ is unimodal, the minimum $x^{*}$ must be to the right of $a$
  (since $f(a) > f(b)$ means we are still descending as we move right from
  $a$) and to the left of $c$ (since $f(c) > f(b)$ means we are still
  descending as we move left from $c$).  Both conditions together trap the
  minimum in $(a, c)$.

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Why Unimodality Makes Bracketing Safe}
    The unimodality assumption is what makes every evaluation informative:
    once we have a valid triple $(a, b, c)$ with $f(a) > f(b) < f(c)$,
    every subsequent evaluation either tightens the bracket from the left or
    from the right, but never risks losing the minimum.  Without unimodality,
    the minimum could escape through a ``back door'' --- a second valley
    hidden outside our current bracket.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{Finding an Initial Bracket}
% ==================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{3.2\ \ Finding an Initial Bracket}

  Before we can apply any of the refinement methods (Fibonacci, golden section,
  quadratic fit), we need a valid starting bracketing triple $(a, b, c)$.
  This section describes a simple but effective algorithm that \emph{finds}
  an initial bracket by probing the function, without any prior knowledge
  of where the minimum is.

  The strategy is intuitive: start at some initial point $x_0$ (read ``x sub
  zero''; the subscript 0 indicates the starting, or zeroth, iteration) and
  take a small step in the positive direction.  If the function value goes
  \emph{up} after that step, the minimum is to the left of $x_0$, so we
  reverse direction.  We then march forward, doubling the step size at each
  iteration, until we find a point where the function starts going back up.
  At that moment we have a bracket: the current point is higher than the
  previous point, which is higher than the one before that, forming a valid
  triple.

  The step doubling is the crucial design choice: it ensures the algorithm
  reaches the minimum in a logarithmic number of steps even if the minimum is
  far away, rather than crawling at constant speed.

  \begin{block}{Algorithm 3.1 --- \texttt{bracket\_minimum}$(f,\; x_0;\; s,\; k)$}
    \textbf{Inputs:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $f$ --- the objective function to minimise (accepts a real number,
            returns a real number)
      \item $x_0$ (x sub zero) --- the starting point; default value $0$
      \item $s$ --- the initial step size (a small positive real number);
            default $10^{-2}$ (one hundredth)
      \item $k$ (kappa, pronounced ``kap-ah'') --- the step expansion factor,
            a multiplier greater than 1; default value $2$
    \end{itemize}
    \textbf{Output:} an interval $[a, b]$ that brackets a local minimum.\\[4pt]
    \textbf{Procedure:}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Set $a \leftarrow x_0$ and compute $y_a \leftarrow f(a)$.  Then
            set $b \leftarrow a + s$ and compute $y_b \leftarrow f(b)$.
            (The arrow $\leftarrow$ means ``assign the right-hand value to
            the left-hand variable''.)
      \item If $y_b > y_a$ (the step went uphill): swap $a$ and $b$ (so now
            $a$ is the right point and $b$ is the left), swap $y_a$ and $y_b$,
            and negate $s$ (set $s \leftarrow -s$) so we now march leftward.
      \item \textbf{Repeat} until a bracket is found:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item Compute $c \leftarrow b + s$ and $y_c \leftarrow f(c)$.
          \item If $y_c > y_b$ (the function went back uphill): the minimum
                is between the last three points.  \textbf{Return} the sorted
                interval $[\min(a,c),\, \max(a,c)]$.
          \item Otherwise, advance: set $a \leftarrow b$, $y_a \leftarrow y_b$,
                $b \leftarrow c$, $y_b \leftarrow y_c$.
          \item Expand step: set $s \leftarrow k \cdot s$ (multiply step
                by $k$; with $k = 2$ this doubles the step each iteration).
        \end{itemize}
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation}
    $x_0$ (x sub zero): the user-supplied starting guess for the location
    of the minimum; it is a single real number.\\
    $s$: the current step size at any given iteration, measured in the same
    units as $x$.  It starts small and grows exponentially.\\
    $k$ (kappa): the expansion factor --- each step is $k$ times longer than
    the previous one.  Setting $k = 2$ means the step doubles each time.\\
    $y_a, y_b, y_c$: shorthand for the function values $f(a)$, $f(b)$, $f(c)$
    stored in memory to avoid re-evaluating $f$, which may be expensive.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read This Algorithm: The Hiking Analogy}
    Imagine a hiker who cannot see the whole landscape and is trying to find
    the bottom of a valley.  She takes a single step forward.  If it goes
    uphill, she turns around.  She then starts walking with increasingly
    long strides --- one step of length $s$, then $ks$, then $k^2 s$, and
    so on.  As soon as she takes a step and finds herself going back uphill,
    she knows the valley bottom must be somewhere behind her last two
    positions.  The exponential stride growth ensures she reaches the valley
    in at most $O(\log(\text{distance}/s))$ steps rather than crawling.
  \end{alertblock}

  \textbf{Important Caveat:} If the function has \emph{no} local minimum on
  the real line --- for example $f(x) = e^x$ (e to the power $x$, a function
  that decreases without bound as $x \to -\infty$, read ``$x$ goes to minus
  infinity'') --- this algorithm will run forever.  Always confirm that the
  function has a minimum before calling it.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Python: \texttt{bracket\_minimum}}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode, caption={Python implementation of Algorithm 3.1 (bracket\_minimum)}]
import numpy as np

def bracket_minimum(f, x=0.0, s=1e-2, k=2.0):
    """Find an interval [a, b] that brackets a local minimum of f.

    Parameters
    ----------
    f : callable  -- univariate objective function
    x : float     -- starting point (default 0)
    s : float     -- initial step size (default 1e-2)
    k : float     -- step expansion factor, k > 1 (default 2)

    Returns
    -------
    (a, b) : tuple of floats with a < b
    """
    a, ya = x, f(x)
    b, yb = a + s, f(a + s)

    if yb > ya:                     # first step went uphill -- reverse
        a, b = b, a
        ya, yb = yb, ya
        s = -s

    while True:
        c, yc = b + s, f(b + s)
        if yc > yb:                 # found the bracket -- return it
            return (a, c) if a < c else (c, a)
        a, ya, b, yb = b, yb, c, yc
        s *= k                      # expand the step by factor k
\end{lstlisting}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Bracket Minimum: Seeing the Algorithm in Action}

  The figure below shows four successive stages of the
  \texttt{bracket\_minimum} algorithm applied to a sample function.
  Each panel corresponds to one decision made by the algorithm.

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{fig_bracket_minimum}
  \end{center}

  Reading the panels from left to right:

  \textbf{Panel 1 (leftmost):} The algorithm takes an initial step to the
  right from the starting point $x_0$.  It finds that the function value
  went \emph{up} (the step was uphill), so it reverses direction by setting
  $s \leftarrow -s$ and begins marching to the left.

  \textbf{Panels 2 and 3:} Each subsequent step covers $k = 2$ times the
  distance of the previous one.  The function values are still decreasing
  as we march left, so no bracket has been found yet.  Notice the growing
  arrow lengths: the algorithm is accelerating geometrically.

  \textbf{Panel 4 (rightmost):} The algorithm takes a step and the function
  value \emph{increases} for the first time.  This signals that the minimum
  lies somewhere between the second-to-last and last positions.  Together
  with the position before that, a valid bracketing triple is formed and
  the algorithm returns the bounding interval.

  The key observation: the step size doubling (with $k = 2$) means that
  even if the minimum is very far from $x_0$, the algorithm needs only
  a logarithmic number of steps.  Specifically, if the minimum is at
  distance $D$ from $x_0$ and the initial step size is $s$, the algorithm
  terminates in approximately $\log_2(D/s)$ (log base 2 of $D$ over $s$)
  iterations.

\end{frame}

\section{Fibonacci Search}
% ==================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{3.3\ \ Fibonacci Search --- The Core Idea}

  Once we have a valid bracketing interval $[a, b]$, the next question is:
  how do we shrink it as efficiently as possible?  \textbf{Fibonacci search}
  gives the provably optimal answer for a \emph{fixed budget} of $n$ function
  evaluations on a unimodal function.

  The central observation is that at each step, we place two interior query
  points inside the current interval.  After comparing the two function values,
  we discard one side of the interval.  Crucially, \emph{one} of the two points
  is reused in the next step --- we already know its function value --- so we
  only need \emph{one new evaluation} per step after the first.  The question
  is: where exactly should we place the two points to maximise the guaranteed
  interval shrinkage after all $n$ evaluations?

  \begin{block}{The Two-Query Optimal Placement}
    Suppose we have a unimodal $f$ on $[a, b]$ and are allowed exactly
    \emph{two} evaluations.  Placing them at the $\tfrac{1}{3}$ and
    $\tfrac{2}{3}$ positions --- that is, at $a + \tfrac{1}{3}(b-a)$ and
    $a + \tfrac{2}{3}(b-a)$ --- guarantees that regardless of which value
    is lower, we can discard exactly $\tfrac{1}{3}$ of the interval.
    No two-query strategy can guarantee discarding more than $\tfrac{1}{3}$.
    However, if we are allowed to place the two points infinitesimally close
    together at the center, we can discard nearly \emph{half} the interval
    --- but this only works when we have effectively infinite evaluations
    remaining to resolve which side the minimum is on.
  \end{block}

  Fibonacci search generalises this trade-off: the optimal placement ratio
  for $n$ total evaluations is determined by the ratio of two consecutive
  Fibonacci numbers.

  \begin{columns}
    \column{0.55\textwidth}
    As the total budget $n$ increases, the placement ratio for each step
    approaches $\varphi^{-1} \approx 0.618$ (phi to the minus one, read
    ``one over phi'', where $\varphi$ (phi, pronounced ``fee'' or ``fye'')
    is the golden ratio).  This observation leads directly to golden section
    search, discussed in the next section.

    \medskip
    The key saving: one of the two interior evaluation points from the current
    step becomes an interior point of the \emph{next} step.  This means we
    only pay for one \emph{new} evaluation per step, not two.

    \column{0.42\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_fibonacci_2query}
  \end{columns}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Fibonacci Search Is Provably Optimal}
    Given a fixed budget of $n$ function evaluations on a unimodal function,
    no deterministic algorithm can guarantee a smaller final interval than
    Fibonacci search.  The final interval has length $I_1 / F_{n+1}$, where
    $I_1 = b - a$ is the initial interval length and $F_{n+1}$ (F sub $n$ plus
    one) is the $(n+1)$-th Fibonacci number.  Because Fibonacci numbers grow
    exponentially, more evaluations always help --- but with diminishing
    returns.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Fibonacci Numbers and Why They Control Shrinkage}

  To understand \emph{why} Fibonacci numbers determine the optimal shrinkage,
  we first review the sequence itself and its key properties.

  \begin{columns}
    \column{0.55\textwidth}

    \begin{block}{The Fibonacci Sequence (Recurrence)}
      The Fibonacci sequence is defined by the following rule (a
      \emph{recurrence}, meaning each term is defined in terms of previous
      terms):
      \[
        F_n =
        \begin{cases}
          1 & \text{if } n \le 2 \\[4pt]
          F_{n-1} + F_{n-2} & \text{if } n > 2
        \end{cases}
      \]
      Reading the formula: $F_n$ (F sub $n$) is the $n$-th term in the
      sequence, where $n$ is a positive integer index starting at 1.  Each
      term is the sum of the two immediately preceding terms.
      The first several values are: $F_1 = 1$, $F_2 = 1$, $F_3 = 2$,
      $F_4 = 3$, $F_5 = 5$, $F_6 = 8$, $F_7 = 13$, $F_8 = 21$,
      $F_9 = 34$, $F_{10} = 55, \ldots$
    \end{block}

    \medskip
    After $n$ evaluations of Fibonacci search, the initial interval shrinks
    by the factor $F_{n+1}$.  Concretely: with $n = 5$ evaluations, the
    shrinkage factor is $F_6 = 8$, so an interval of length $8$ becomes
    an interval of length $8/8 = 1$.

    \column{0.43\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_fibonacci_intervals}\\[2pt]
    {\footnotesize Each row shows the remaining bracket after one more
    evaluation.  The key relation is $I_1 = F_{n+1} \cdot I_n$, where
    $I_k$ is the interval length at step $k$ (counting from the end),
    and $I_1$ is the final interval length after all $n$ evaluations.}
  \end{columns}

  \medskip

  \begin{block}{Binet's Closed-Form Formula}
    The Fibonacci numbers can be computed directly --- without applying the
    recurrence $n$ times --- using \textbf{Binet's formula}:
    \[
      F_n = \frac{\varphi^n - (1 - \varphi)^n}{\sqrt{5}},
      \qquad
      \varphi = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.618
    \]
    Here $\varphi$ (phi, the golden ratio) and $\sqrt{5}$ (the square root
    of five, approximately $2.236$) are the two key constants.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for Binet's Formula}
    $F_n$ (F sub $n$): the $n$-th Fibonacci number, a positive integer.\\
    $\varphi$ (phi, pronounced ``fee'' or ``fye''): the golden ratio,
    approximately $1.618$.  It satisfies the remarkable algebraic property
    $\varphi^2 = \varphi + 1$, which is the source of many of its special
    properties in geometry, art, and mathematics.\\
    $\varphi^n$ (phi to the power $n$): the golden ratio raised to the
    $n$-th power.  This term grows without bound as $n$ increases.\\
    $(1 - \varphi)^n$: the second term, approximately $(-0.618)^n$.
    Since $|1 - \varphi| \approx 0.618 < 1$, repeated multiplication drives
    this term toward zero, so for large $n$, $F_n \approx \varphi^n / \sqrt{5}$.\\
    $\sqrt{5}$ (square root of five): the normalising constant.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read Binet's Formula}
    The formula tells us that Fibonacci numbers grow \emph{exponentially},
    at the rate of $\varphi \approx 1.618$ per step.  This means that every
    additional evaluation of Fibonacci search shrinks the interval by a factor
    of approximately $1.618$ --- equivalent to about 70\% of the previous
    length.  Ten additional evaluations shrink the interval by $\varphi^{10}
    \approx 123$ times.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{block}{Ratio of Successive Fibonacci Numbers}
    The exact fractional placement used at step $n$ of Fibonacci search is:
    \[
      \rho_n = \frac{F_n}{F_{n+1}} = \varphi^{-1}\,
               \frac{1 - s^{n+1}}{1 - s^{n}},
      \qquad s = \frac{1 - \sqrt{5}}{1 + \sqrt{5}} \approx -0.382
    \]
    where $\rho_n$ (rho sub $n$, pronounced ``row'') is the fraction of the
    interval from the left endpoint at which the interior point is placed, and
    $s$ (a small constant, approximately $-0.382$) is a correction factor that
    accounts for the difference between the exact Fibonacci ratio and the
    limiting golden ratio.  As $n \to \infty$ (as $n$ grows without bound),
    $\rho_n \to \varphi^{-1} \approx 0.618$, confirming that golden section
    search uses the limiting ratio.
  \end{block}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Algorithm 3.2: Fibonacci Search in Python}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode, caption={Python: Fibonacci search for unimodal f on [a,b] with n evaluations}]
import numpy as np

def fibonacci_search(f, a, b, n, eps=0.01):
    """Fibonacci search: find minimum of unimodal f on [a, b].

    n   : total number of function evaluations (n > 1)
    eps : small tolerance for the final disambiguation step
    Returns the bracketing interval (a, b) after n evaluations.
    """
    phi = (1 + np.sqrt(5)) / 2            # golden ratio, ~1.618
    s   = (1 - np.sqrt(5)) / (1 + np.sqrt(5))  # correction, ~-0.382

    # Placement ratio for the first (right) interior point
    rho = 1.0 / (phi * (1 - s**(n+1)) / (1 - s**n))
    r   = (1 - rho) * a + rho * b         # right interior point
    yr, n_left = f(r), n - 1              # evaluate f; one eval used

    while n_left > 0:
        if n_left > 1:
            lam = rho * a + (1 - rho) * b     # left interior point
        else:
            lam = eps * a + (1 - eps) * r     # last step: near center

        rho    = 1.0 / (phi * (1 - s**(n_left+1)) / (1 - s**n_left))
        yl     = f(lam)                        # one new evaluation
        n_left -= 1

        if yl < yr:                            # left is lower
            r, b, yr = lam, r, yr             # keep left half [a, r]
        else:                                  # right is lower (or equal)
            a, b = b, lam                     # keep right half [lam, b]

    return (a, b) if a < b else (b, a)
\end{lstlisting}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Fibonacci Search Code Walkthrough}

  Let us trace through the key parts of the Python implementation to build
  intuition for what each line accomplishes.

  \textbf{Lines 1--2 (Setup constants):} We compute $\varphi$ (phi) as
  $(1 + \sqrt{5})/2 \approx 1.618$ and the correction factor $s \approx -0.382$.
  These two constants, combined with the number of remaining evaluations, give
  the exact Fibonacci-optimal placement ratio $\rho$ (rho, pronounced ``row'')
  at each step.

  \textbf{Placement ratio $\rho$ (rho):} The variable \texttt{rho} holds the
  fractional distance from the left endpoint $a$ to the right interior query
  point.  For example, $\rho = 0.6$ means the point is placed 60\% of the way
  from $a$ to $b$.  This ratio is re-computed at each step based on how many
  evaluations remain.

  \textbf{Interior points $r$ and $\lambda$ (lambda, pronounced ``lam-da''):}
  At each iteration the algorithm maintains two interior points: \texttt{r}
  (the ``right'' interior point) and \texttt{lam} (short for $\lambda$, the
  ``left'' interior point).  The point \texttt{r} was evaluated in the previous
  step and its value \texttt{yr} is already known.  Only \texttt{lam} requires
  a new call to $f$ --- this is the one-new-evaluation-per-step property.

  \textbf{Bracket update rule:}
  After comparing $f(\lambda)$ (stored as \texttt{yl}) and $f(r)$ (stored as
  \texttt{yr}):
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If $f(\lambda) < f(r)$ (the left interior point is lower): the minimum
          must be in $[a, r]$, so we discard everything to the right of $r$
          by updating $b \leftarrow r$.
    \item Otherwise: the minimum is in $[\lambda, b]$, so we discard everything
          to the left of $\lambda$ by updating $a \leftarrow \lambda$.
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Last step ($\varepsilon$ (epsilon, pronounced ``ep-si-lon''), a very
  small positive number):} The final iteration places a point very close to
  $r$ (at fraction $\varepsilon$ from $a$ to $r$) to resolve which side of the
  center contains the minimum.  The default \texttt{eps} $= 0.01$ is small
  enough for most purposes.

\end{frame}

\section{Golden Section Search}
% ==================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{3.4\ \ Golden Section Search}

  Fibonacci search is provably optimal but requires one practical decision
  up front: we must commit to the total number of evaluations $n$
  \emph{before} the first evaluation.  If we later decide we need more
  precision, we cannot simply continue the same sequence.

  \textbf{Golden section search} removes this limitation.  It uses a
  \emph{constant} placement ratio at every step --- the ratio $\varphi^{-1}
  \approx 0.618$ (one over phi) --- which is the \emph{limit} of the
  Fibonacci placement ratio as $n \to \infty$.  Because the ratio never
  changes, we can add evaluations one at a time and stop whenever the
  bracket is small enough.

  \begin{block}{Key Formula: The Limiting Ratio (derivation of golden section)}
    As the total budget $n$ grows without bound (written $n \to \infty$,
    read ``$n$ goes to infinity''), the Fibonacci placement ratio converges
    to the reciprocal of the golden ratio:
    \[
      \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{F_n}{F_{n+1}} = \frac{1}{\varphi}
      = \varphi - 1 = \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2} \approx 0.618
    \]
    \textbf{Golden section search} uses this constant ratio $\rho = \varphi^{-1}
    \approx 0.618$ at every iteration, regardless of how many evaluations
    remain.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation}
    $\lim_{n \to \infty}$ (read ``the limit as $n$ goes to infinity''):
    the value the expression approaches as $n$ increases without bound.\\
    $F_n / F_{n+1}$ (F sub $n$ divided by F sub $n$ plus one): the ratio
    of two consecutive Fibonacci numbers; this ratio converges to $1/\varphi$.\\
    $\varphi$ (phi, the golden ratio): $\approx 1.618$, satisfying
    $\varphi^2 = \varphi + 1$.\\
    $\varphi^{-1}$ (phi to the minus one, read ``one over phi''): the
    reciprocal of the golden ratio, $\approx 0.618$.  Remarkably,
    $\varphi^{-1} = \varphi - 1$.\\
    $\sqrt{5}$ (square root of five): $\approx 2.236$.
  \end{block}

  Using the constant ratio $\rho = \varphi^{-1} \approx 0.618$ at every step:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The interval is always split in the same golden proportion, so the
          algorithm has a regular, predictable structure.
    \item After $n$ evaluations, the bracket length has shrunk by the factor
          $\varphi^{n-1}$: an initial interval of length $I_1$ becomes an
          interval of length $I_1 \cdot \varphi^{-(n-1)}$.
    \item To guarantee the minimum is located within a desired tolerance
          $\varepsilon$ (epsilon, the target precision --- the minimum
          acceptable interval length):
          \[
            n = \left\lceil
              \frac{\ln\bigl((b-a)/\varepsilon\bigr)}{\ln \varphi}
            \right\rceil
            \quad \text{evaluations are sufficient.}
          \]
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{block}{Notation for the Evaluation-Count Formula}
    $\ln(\cdot)$ (the natural logarithm, read ``ell-en'' or ``log''): the
    inverse of the exponential function $e^{(\cdot)}$.  It converts the
    exponential shrinkage factor into a linear count of iterations.\\
    $(b - a)$: the initial interval length.\\
    $\varepsilon$ (epsilon): the desired final interval length (precision).\\
    $(b-a)/\varepsilon$: the ratio of initial to final interval length ---
    how much total shrinkage is needed.\\
    $\lceil \cdot \rceil$ (the ``ceiling'' function, read ``ceiling of''): round up
    to the nearest integer, since we need a whole number of evaluations.\\
    $\ln \varphi \approx 0.481$: the natural log of the golden ratio, which
    appears because each evaluation shrinks the interval by $\varphi$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: How Many Evaluations Are Needed?}
    Suppose $[a, b] = [0, 100]$ (initial interval length $= 100$) and we
    want precision $\varepsilon = 0.001$ (one thousandth).  Substituting:
    \begin{align*}
      n &= \left\lceil \frac{\ln(100 / 0.001)}{\ln(1.618)} \right\rceil
        = \left\lceil \frac{\ln(100{,}000)}{0.481} \right\rceil
        = \left\lceil \frac{11.51}{0.481} \right\rceil
        = \lceil 23.93 \rceil = 24.
    \end{align*}
    So 24 evaluations are sufficient to narrow a search region of length $100$
    down to $0.001$ --- a reduction by a factor of $100{,}000$.  This illustrates
    the remarkable efficiency of golden section search.
  \end{exampleblock}

  \begin{alertblock}{Practical Advantage: The Anytime Property}
    Because the placement ratio is always the same constant $\varphi^{-1}$,
    we can \textbf{stop at any time} and the current bracket is always valid.
    We do not need to commit to a budget in advance.  After each evaluation
    we check whether the bracket is small enough; if yes, we stop; if no, we
    continue.  This ``anytime'' property makes golden section search the
    method of choice when the required precision is not known in advance.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

    constant ratio --- so Fibonacci is a tiny bit better for very small
    budgets.  In practice this difference is negligible.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Algorithm 3.3: Golden Section Search in Python}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode, caption={Python: Golden section search on [a,b] with n total evaluations}]
import numpy as np

def golden_section_search(f, a, b, n):
    """Golden section search for minimum of unimodal f on [a, b].

    n  : total number of function evaluations (n > 1)
    Returns the bracketing interval (a, b) after n evaluations.
    """
    phi = (1 + np.sqrt(5)) / 2   # golden ratio, ~1.618
    rho = phi - 1                 # = 1/phi ~ 0.618 (placement ratio)

    # Place the first interior point (called d) at fraction rho from left
    d  = rho * b + (1 - rho) * a
    yd = f(d)                    # one evaluation used

    for _ in range(n - 1):
        # Place the symmetric interior point c
        c  = rho * a + (1 - rho) * b
        yc = f(c)                # one new evaluation per step

        if yc < yd:              # left interior point is lower
            b, d, yd = d, c, yc # discard [d, b]; new bracket [a, d]
        else:                    # right interior point is lower (or equal)
            a, b = b, c         # discard [a, c]; new bracket [c, b]
            # Note: d and yd are retained as the new right interior point

    return (a, b) if a < b else (b, a)
\end{lstlisting}

  \medskip
  Two key variables to understand:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\rho$ (rho, approximately $0.618$): the golden ratio reciprocal,
          giving the fractional position of each interior query within the
          current bracket.  It is computed once and used unchanged at every
          iteration.
    \item $d$ (an interior point, retained from the previous iteration):
          its value $yd = f(d)$ is already known, so no re-evaluation of
          $f$ is needed.  Only the \emph{new} symmetric point $c$ requires
          a function call.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Golden Section vs.\ Fibonacci: When to Use Each}

  Both methods shrink the bracket at essentially the same rate.  The choice
  between them comes down to practical considerations.

  \begin{columns}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \textbf{Fibonacci Search}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Provably optimal} for a fixed, predetermined budget of
            $n$ evaluations: no other method can guarantee a smaller final
            interval.
      \item The placement ratio $\rho_n$ changes at every step --- it depends
            on how many evaluations remain.
      \item Requires knowing $n$ before the first evaluation.
      \item After $n$ evaluations, the interval has shrunk by the exact factor
            $F_{n+1}$ (the $(n+1)$-th Fibonacci number).
    \end{itemize}

    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \textbf{Golden Section Search}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Near-optimal} for large $n$; for small $n$ the
            approximation error is tiny and practically irrelevant.
      \item The placement ratio is the constant $\rho = \varphi^{-1}
            \approx 0.618$ at every step --- no bookkeeping required.
      \item Can be run as an \emph{anytime} algorithm: stop whenever the
            bracket is small enough.
      \item After $n$ evaluations, the interval has shrunk by the factor
            $\varphi^{n-1}$ relative to the original length.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}

\section{Quadratic Fit Search}
% ==================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{3.5\ \ Quadratic Fit Search --- Motivation}

  Both golden section and Fibonacci search treat the function $f$ as a
  complete black box.  They make no assumption about the \emph{shape} of $f$,
  and they converge at a geometric rate --- the error decreases by a constant
  factor $\varphi^{-1} \approx 0.618$ per evaluation.

  Can we do better by exploiting shape information?  Yes --- if we assume
  the function is \textbf{smooth} (meaning it has continuous derivatives near
  the minimum), then by Taylor's theorem, any smooth function looks like a
  \emph{quadratic} (a parabola of the form $ax^2 + bx + c$) near its minimum.
  This is because the Taylor expansion around $x^{*}$ gives:
  \[
    f(x) \approx f(x^{*}) + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(x^{*})(x - x^{*})^2 + \ldots
  \]
  (read ``f of x approximately equals f of x-star plus one-half times f
  double-prime of x-star times the square of $x$ minus $x$-star'').
  Here $f''(x^{*})$ (f double-prime, the second derivative of $f$ at $x^{*}$)
  is a positive constant for a minimum, so the dominant behaviour is indeed
  quadratic.

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: Fit a Parabola, Jump to Its Vertex}
    Given three bracketing points, we can fit a unique parabola through all
    three (there is exactly one quadratic that passes through three given
    points).  The vertex (lowest point) of this parabola can be computed
    \emph{exactly} from a simple algebraic formula.  Jumping to the vertex
    rather than taking a cautious golden-section step produces much faster
    convergence near the minimum.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{fig_quadratic_fit}
  \end{center}
  {\footnotesize \textbf{Reading the figure:} Black dots mark the three
  current bracketing points.  The blue curve is the fitted parabola passing
  through all three.  The blue dot is the vertex of the parabola --- the
  predicted next query point.  Notice how closely the parabola tracks the
  true function (grey curve) near the minimum.}

  The convergence is typically \textbf{super-linear}: the number of correct
  decimal digits grows faster than linearly with each iteration.  For a
  perfectly quadratic function, the method finds the minimum \emph{exactly}
  in a single step.

  One important caveat: safeguards are needed when the predicted point falls
  outside the bracket, or when the three points are nearly collinear (nearly
  on a straight line), which would make the fitted parabola nearly flat and
  the vertex prediction unstable.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Quadratic Fit: Deriving the Minimiser Formula}

  Given three bracketing points $a < b < c$ with known function values
  $y_a = f(a)$, $y_b = f(b)$, $y_c = f(c)$, we derive the unique quadratic
  that passes through them and find its minimum analytically.

  \begin{block}{Step 1: The Unique Quadratic via Lagrange Interpolation}
    The unique quadratic $q(x)$ (q of x, our parabola model) passing through
    the three points $(a,\, y_a)$, $(b,\, y_b)$, $(c,\, y_c)$ is:
    \[
      q(x)
      = y_a \cdot \frac{(x-b)(x-c)}{(a-b)(a-c)}
      + y_b \cdot \frac{(x-a)(x-c)}{(b-a)(b-c)}
      + y_c \cdot \frac{(x-a)(x-b)}{(c-a)(c-b)}
    \]
    Each of the three terms is a \emph{Lagrange basis polynomial}: a quadratic
    that equals 1 at one of the three nodes and 0 at the other two.  Multiplied
    by the corresponding function value and summed, they interpolate $f$ exactly
    at all three points.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for the Lagrange Formula}
    $q(x)$: the fitted quadratic function, our model of $f$ near the minimum.\\
    $a, b, c$: the three query $x$-coordinates, with $a < b < c$.\\
    $y_a, y_b, y_c$: the function values $f(a)$, $f(b)$, $f(c)$ at those points.\\
    $(x - b)(x - c)$: a product of two linear expressions.  This equals zero
    when $x = b$ and when $x = c$, ensuring those points are handled by the
    other two terms.\\
    $(a - b)(a - c)$: the denominator for the first basis polynomial, a
    normalising constant ensuring the term equals 1 at $x = a$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Step 2: Finding the Minimiser --- Setting $q'(x^*) = 0$}
    The derivative $q'(x)$ of a quadratic is a linear function, so setting
    it to zero gives a unique solution.  After algebra, the minimiser of
    $q(x)$ is:
    \[
      x^* = \frac{1}{2}
            \cdot
            \frac{y_a(b^2 - c^2) + y_b(c^2 - a^2) + y_c(a^2 - b^2)}
                 {y_a(b - c)   + y_b(c - a)   + y_c(a - b)}
    \]
    This gives the $x$-coordinate of the parabola's vertex --- the point
    where the fitted quadratic $q(x)$ is smallest.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for the Minimiser Formula}
    $x^{*}$ (x star): the predicted minimiser, the next query point.\\
    The \textbf{numerator} $y_a(b^2 - c^2) + y_b(c^2 - a^2) + y_c(a^2 - b^2)$:
    a weighted combination of the squared positions of the three points,
    where the weights are the function values.  It has the same units as
    $y_a \cdot x^2$.\\
    The \textbf{denominator} $y_a(b - c) + y_b(c - a) + y_c(a - b)$:
    a weighted combination of the gaps between points, with units of
    $y_a \cdot x$.  If the denominator is zero, the three points are collinear
    (lie on a straight line) and no unique quadratic minimum exists.  In practice,
    this is handled by a guard check.\\
    The factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$ scales the numerator-over-denominator ratio to
    give the correct vertex position.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Minimiser Formula}
    Think of $x^*$ as a weighted average of the three points $a$, $b$, $c$,
    where the weights depend on the function values.  If $y_b$ is much smaller
    than $y_a$ and $y_c$ (the middle point is much lower than the ends), the
    formula pulls $x^*$ close to $b$.  If the true $f$ is exactly a parabola,
    this formula gives the \emph{exact} answer in one step.  For functions that
    are only approximately quadratic near the minimum, several iterations bring
    the bracket to high precision --- but each iteration reduces the error
    faster than any fixed geometric ratio, which is what we call
    ``super-linear convergence''.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Algorithm 3.4: Quadratic Fit Search in Python}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode, caption={Python: Quadratic fit search with n evaluations starting from triple (a,b,c)}]
import numpy as np

def quadratic_fit_search(f, a, b, c, n):
    """Quadratic fit search for minimum of smooth f.

    a < b < c  : initial bracketing triple (a, b, c) with f(b) < f(a), f(c)
    n          : total evaluations including the initial 3 (so n >= 3)
    Returns (a, b, c) as the final bracketing triple.
    """
    ya, yb, yc = f(a), f(b), f(c)

    for _ in range(n - 3):          # n-3 additional evaluations
        # Compute the vertex of the fitted quadratic
        denom = ya * (b - c) + yb * (c - a) + yc * (a - b)
        x = 0.5 * (ya * (b**2 - c**2) +
                   yb * (c**2 - a**2) +
                   yc * (a**2 - b**2)) / denom
        yx = f(x)                   # evaluate at the predicted minimiser

        if x > b:                   # x lies between b and c
            if yx > yb:             # f went up from b to x
                c, yc = x, yx       # tighten from right
            else:                   # f went down from b to x
                a, ya, b, yb = b, yb, x, yx
        elif x < b:                 # x lies between a and b
            if yx > yb:             # f went up from b back to x
                a, ya = x, yx       # tighten from left
            else:                   # f went down from b to x
                c, yc, b, yb = b, yb, x, yx

    return a, b, c
\end{lstlisting}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Quadratic Fit: A Worked Numerical Example}

  We now trace through the formula with explicit numbers to build confidence
  that the algebra is correct and to see how the method converges.

  \begin{block}{Problem Setup}
    Minimise $f(x) = (x - 1)^2 + 1$ (read ``the quantity $x$ minus 1, squared,
    plus 1'') starting with the bracketing triple $(a, b, c) = (0,\; 1,\; 3)$.
    The true minimum is at $x^{*} = 1$ with $f(1) = 0 + 1 = 1$.
  \end{block}

  \textbf{Step 1: Compute function values.}
  \begin{align*}
    y_a &= f(0) = (0 - 1)^2 + 1 = (-1)^2 + 1 = 1 + 1 = 2 \\
    y_b &= f(1) = (1 - 1)^2 + 1 = 0 + 1 = 1 \\
    y_c &= f(3) = (3 - 1)^2 + 1 = 4 + 1 = 5
  \end{align*}

  \textbf{Step 2: Compute the denominator $D$.}
  \[
    D = y_a(b - c) + y_b(c - a) + y_c(a - b)
      = 2(1 - 3) + 1(3 - 0) + 5(0 - 1)
      = 2(-2) + 1(3) + 5(-1)
      = -4 + 3 - 5 = -6
  \]

  \textbf{Step 3: Compute the numerator $N$.}
  \[
    N = y_a(b^2 - c^2) + y_b(c^2 - a^2) + y_c(a^2 - b^2)
      = 2(1 - 9) + 1(9 - 0) + 5(0 - 1)
      = 2(-8) + 9 + 5(-1)
      = -16 + 9 - 5 = -12
  \]

  \textbf{Step 4: Apply the formula.}
  \[
    x^* = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{N}{D}
        = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{-12}{-6}
        = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2
        = 1.0
  \]

  \begin{exampleblock}{Result and Interpretation}
    The quadratic fit formula returns $x^* = 1.0$, which is the \emph{exact}
    minimiser, found in a single step.  This is not a coincidence: the true
    function $f(x) = (x-1)^2 + 1$ is itself a perfect quadratic, so the
    fitted parabola \emph{is} the true function, and its vertex is found
    exactly.  In practice, for functions that are only approximately quadratic
    near the minimum (smooth but not exactly parabolic), several iterations
    are needed --- but each one converges super-linearly.
  \end{exampleblock}

  \begin{alertblock}{When the Method Can Struggle}
    If the denominator $D$ is very close to zero --- indicating the three points
    are nearly collinear --- the formula becomes numerically unstable (dividing
    by a very small number amplifies rounding errors).  Robust implementations
    add a guard: if $|D| < \delta$ (delta, a small threshold), fall back to
    a golden section step instead.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{Shubert-Piyavskii Method}
% ==================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{3.6\ \ Shubert-Piyavskii Method --- Motivation}

  All the methods so far --- Fibonacci, golden section, quadratic fit ---
  assume the function is \textbf{unimodal}: exactly one valley.  In many
  real-world problems the objective has multiple local minima (it is
  \emph{multimodal}), and we need the \emph{global} minimum, not just a
  local one that happens to be nearby.

  The \textbf{Shubert-Piyavskii method} (named after its inventors, Boris
  Shubert and Shubert Piyavskii) addresses this harder problem.  Its key idea
  is to maintain a \textbf{global lower bound} on $f$ across the entire
  interval $[a, b]$, and always sample the point where this lower bound is
  \emph{most optimistic} (lowest) --- since that is where the global minimum
  might be hiding.

  The mathematical tool that makes this possible is
  \textbf{Lipschitz continuity}, which bounds how fast the function can
  change from one point to another.

  \begin{block}{Definition: Lipschitz Continuity}
    A function $f$ is said to be \textbf{Lipschitz continuous} on the interval
    $[a, b]$ with \textbf{Lipschitz constant} $\ell$ (lowercase letter L,
    pronounced like the letter ``ell'') if the following inequality holds for
    every pair of points $x$ and $y$ in $[a, b]$:
    \[
      |f(x) - f(y)| \;\le\; \ell \,|x - y|
      \qquad \forall\; x,\, y \in [a, b].
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for Lipschitz Continuity}
    $|\cdot|$ (absolute value, the vertical bars): the distance from zero,
    making the quantity always non-negative.  So $|f(x) - f(y)|$ is the
    (non-negative) difference in function values, and $|x - y|$ is the
    (non-negative) distance between the two input points.\\
    $\ell$ (ell): the Lipschitz constant.  It is an upper bound on the
    magnitude of the slope $|f'(x)|$ at every point in $[a, b]$.
    Intuitively, $\ell$ is the maximum speed (in function-value units per
    $x$-unit) at which $f$ can rise or fall.\\
    $\forall$ (upside-down A, read ``for all''): the inequality must hold
    for every possible pair $(x, y)$, not just one particular pair.\\
    $x, y \in [a, b]$ (read ``$x$ and $y$ are in the interval $[a, b]$''):
    both points lie within the interval we are optimising over.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Lipschitz Condition: The Ruler Analogy}
    Imagine drawing the function on paper.  Place a ruler angled at slope
    $+\ell$ or $-\ell$ at any sampled point.  The Lipschitz condition
    guarantees the true function \emph{never rises or falls faster than
    the ruler line}.  From any sampled point $(x_0,\, f(x_0))$, the
    function must lie \emph{above} the ``V-shaped tent'' formed by two
    lines of slope $+\ell$ and $-\ell$ radiating outward from that point:
    \[
      f(x) \ge f(x_0) - \ell\,|x - x_0| \quad \text{for all } x \in [a, b].
    \]
    This V-shaped lower bound is the building block of the Shubert-Piyavskii
    method.  Combining the V-shapes from all sampled points gives a
    \emph{sawtooth} lower bound on the entire function.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{The Lipschitz Lower Bound: From a Single Point}

  \begin{columns}
    \column{0.58\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_lipschitz_bound}
    \column{0.40\textwidth}
    \textbf{Reading the figure:} At the sampled point $x_0$ we draw two
    straight lines emanating outward:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item A line of slope $+\ell$ going to the right: the function can
            increase at most this fast as we move right from $x_0$.
      \item A line of slope $-\ell$ going to the left: the function can
            increase at most this fast as we move left from $x_0$.
    \end{itemize}
    The region \emph{below} both lines (the shaded V beneath the two
    dashed lines) is where the function is guaranteed \emph{never to be}.
    The function must lie \emph{above} the V at every point.
  \end{columns}

  \medskip
  The bottom of the V at any point $x$ provides a lower bound on $f(x)$.
  As we accumulate more sampled points, each gives its own V-shape.  The
  \emph{tightest} lower bound at any given $x$ is the \emph{maximum} of
  all the individual V-shapes from all sampled points --- since the maximum
  of a set of lower bounds is itself a lower bound, and a tighter one.
  This maximum-of-V-shapes is the characteristic \emph{sawtooth} pattern
  of the Shubert-Piyavskii method.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Shubert-Piyavskii: The Sawtooth Lower Bound}

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig_shubert_piyavskii}
  \end{center}

  This figure shows the sawtooth lower bound building up across successive
  iterations of the algorithm.  Reading the panels:

  \textbf{After Iteration 1:} The algorithm samples the midpoint of $[a, b]$.
  A single V-shape radiates from this point, giving a crude lower bound
  that covers the entire interval.

  \textbf{After Iteration 2:} The next sample is placed at the \emph{global
  minimum of the current sawtooth} --- the lowest point of the V-shape, where
  the lower bound is most optimistic.  Two V-shapes now combine to form a
  two-tooth sawtooth; the bound is sharper.

  \textbf{After Iteration 3:} The sawtooth sharpens further.  More peaks
  approach the true function, and more valleys are elevated upward.

  \textbf{Termination:} The algorithm stops when the \emph{optimality gap}
  $\delta$ (delta, pronounced ``del-ta'', meaning the gap between the best
  function value found and the current sawtooth minimum) satisfies:
  \[
    \delta \;=\; f_{\text{best}} - y_{\text{saw,min}} \;\le\; \varepsilon
  \]
  where $f_{\text{best}}$ is the smallest $f$ value seen so far,
  $y_{\text{saw,min}}$ is the minimum of the sawtooth lower bound, and
  $\varepsilon$ (epsilon) is the desired tolerance.  When this gap is small,
  we know the global minimum cannot be much better than $f_{\text{best}}$.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Uncertainty Regions After Each Evaluation}

  After each set of evaluations, the Shubert-Piyavskii method can quantify
  exactly which sub-regions of $[a, b]$ could still contain the global
  minimum.

  \begin{block}{Uncertainty Region Formula}
    For the $i$-th sawtooth vertex at position $x^{(i)}$ (x superscript $i$,
    the $i$-th lower vertex of the sawtooth) with sawtooth value $y^{(i)}$
    (the lower bound at that vertex), and for the best function value found so
    far $y_{\min}$ (the smallest $f$ evaluated at any sampled point), the
    \textbf{uncertainty region} around the $i$-th vertex is:
    \[
      \left[
        x^{(i)} - \frac{y_{\min} - y^{(i)}}{\ell},
        \;\;
        x^{(i)} + \frac{y_{\min} - y^{(i)}}{\ell}
      \right]
    \]
    This region is non-empty only when $y^{(i)} < y_{\min}$, meaning the
    sawtooth lower bound dips below the best sampled value --- so there is
    still hope for improvement in that region.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for the Uncertainty Region}
    $x^{(i)}$ (x superscript i, in parentheses to distinguish from
    exponentiation): the horizontal position of the $i$-th sawtooth vertex.\\
    $y^{(i)}$ (y superscript i): the sawtooth lower bound value at $x^{(i)}$.\\
    $y_{\min}$: the smallest function value evaluated at any sampled point so far.
    This is the ``best solution we have found''.\\
    $y_{\min} - y^{(i)}$ (y-min minus y superscript i): how far the sawtooth
    dips below the best known value.  A larger dip means the global minimum
    might be significantly better than what we have found, so we cannot rule
    out that region.\\
    $\ell$ (ell): the Lipschitz constant.  A larger $\ell$ (the function can
    change faster) means a narrower uncertainty region: the slope constraint
    restricts how far from $x^{(i)}$ the improvement could be.\\
    Dividing by $\ell$ converts a ``depth'' (in $y$-units) into a
    ``width'' (in $x$-units) using the slope constraint $|f(x) - f(x_0)|
    \le \ell |x - x_0|$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Uncertainty Region Formula}
    The formula says: given that the sawtooth at $x^{(i)}$ is $y^{(i)}$
    (our lower bound on $f$ near $x^{(i)}$) and the best value we have
    found so far is $y_{\min}$, the true global minimum could be in the
    range $[y^{(i)},\, y_{\min}]$.  The horizontal width of the region
    where this improvement could be hiding is $(y_{\min} - y^{(i)})/\ell$.
    As we sample more, the sawtooth tightens, the dips get smaller, and
    the uncertainty regions shrink to zero.
  \end{alertblock}

  \textbf{Limitations of the Shubert-Piyavskii Method:}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Requires knowing $\ell$.}  In practice the Lipschitz constant
          is often unknown.  If $\ell$ is too small, the lower bound is invalid
          (the true function may lie below the sawtooth).  If $\ell$ is too
          large, the lower bound is very loose and convergence is extremely slow.
          Estimating a good $\ell$ is itself a non-trivial problem.
    \item \textbf{One dimension only.}  The method works on an interval $[a, b]$
          in one dimension.  Extending to multiple dimensions requires
          specialised algorithms such as DIRECT (Dividing Rectangles) or LIPO,
          and the number of evaluations needed grows exponentially with the
          number of dimensions (the ``curse of dimensionality'').
  \end{enumerate}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Algorithm 3.5: Shubert-Piyavskii in Python}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode, basicstyle=\ttfamily\tiny,
  caption={Python: Shubert-Piyavskii method (requires Lipschitz constant ell)}]
import numpy as np

def shubert_piyavskii(f, a, b, ell, eps=1e-5, max_iter=500):
    """Global minimum of f on [a, b] via sawtooth lower bound.

    ell      : Lipschitz constant (upper bound on |f'(x)| everywhere)
    eps      : optimality-gap tolerance: stop when gap <= eps
    max_iter : safety limit on iterations
    Returns (x_best, f_best) -- best point found.
    """
    x1 = (a + b) / 2.0           # start at midpoint
    pts = [(x1, f(x1))]          # list of (x, y) sample pairs
    delta = np.inf                # current optimality gap

    for _ in range(max_iter):
        if delta <= eps:
            break

        best = {'i': 0, 'x': 0.0, 'y': np.inf}

        # Check left boundary intersection with first V-shape
        y_left = pts[0][1] - ell * (pts[0][0] - a)
        if y_left < best['y']:
            best = {'i': 0, 'x': a, 'y': y_left}

        # Check right boundary intersection with last V-shape
        y_right = pts[-1][1] - ell * (b - pts[-1][0])
        if y_right < best['y']:
            best = {'i': len(pts), 'x': b, 'y': y_right}

        # Check all interior sawtooth valleys between adjacent samples
        for i in range(1, len(pts)):
            A, B = pts[i-1], pts[i]
            t = ((A[1] - B[1]) - ell * (A[0] - B[0])) / (2 * ell)
            P = (A[0] + t, A[1] - t * ell)    # valley (x, y)
            if P[1] < best['y']:
                best = {'i': i, 'x': P[0], 'y': P[1]}

        # Sample at the lowest sawtooth valley
        x_new = best['x']
        y_new = f(x_new)
        pts.insert(best['i'], (x_new, y_new))
        pts.sort(key=lambda p: p[0])
        delta = y_new - best['y']   # how much better than lower bound?

    return min(pts, key=lambda p: p[1])
\end{lstlisting}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Shubert-Piyavskii: Code Walkthrough}

  Let us trace through the key steps of the implementation to understand
  what each part does and why.

  \textbf{Initialisation:} We sample the midpoint $x_1 = (a+b)/2$ and store
  it as the first entry in \texttt{pts} (a list of $(x, y)$ pairs, sorted by
  $x$).  The variable \texttt{delta} ($\delta$, delta, meaning the optimality
  gap) starts at infinity because we have no gap estimate yet.

  \textbf{Finding the sawtooth valley:} The main loop scans all vertices of
  the sawtooth to find the lowest one.  There are three types of vertices to
  check:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item The left boundary $a$: where the V-shape from the first sampled
          point intersects the left wall.
    \item The right boundary $b$: where the V-shape from the last sampled
          point intersects the right wall.
    \item Interior valleys: for each pair of adjacent samples $A$ and $B$,
          the valley between their V-shapes occurs at the horizontal position
          where the right-going arm of $A$'s V equals the left-going arm of
          $B$'s V.  Solving for this intersection:
          \[
            t = \frac{(A_y - B_y) - \ell(A_x - B_x)}{2\ell},
            \quad P_x = A_x + t, \quad P_y = A_y - \ell\cdot t
          \]
          where $A_x, A_y$ are the $x$ and $y$ coordinates of sample $A$.
  \end{enumerate}

  \textbf{Sampling:} We evaluate $f$ at the lowest sawtooth vertex (the most
  optimistic location for the global minimum) and insert it into \texttt{pts},
  keeping the list sorted by $x$.

  \textbf{Optimality gap update:} After evaluating $f$ at the best sawtooth
  vertex with value \texttt{best['y']}, the gap is $\delta = f(x_{\text{new}})
  - \texttt{best['y']}$.  When $\delta \le \varepsilon$, the true global
  minimum cannot be more than $\varepsilon$ below the best value found.

\end{frame}

\section{Bisection Method}
% ==================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{3.7\ \ Bisection: Using Derivatives to Find Minima}

  All the methods so far use only \emph{function values} $f(x)$.  If we
  also have access to the \textbf{derivative} $f'(x)$ (read ``f prime of x'',
  the instantaneous slope or rate of change of $f$ at the point $x$), we
  can use a qualitatively different and often faster approach.

  The key observation is that at any local minimum $x^*$, the derivative
  must equal zero: $f'(x^*) = 0$.  This is because the function is neither
  increasing nor decreasing at its minimum.  So finding the minimum of $f$ is
  equivalent to finding a \textbf{root} (a zero) of $f'$.

  The \textbf{bisection method} solves this root-finding problem using a
  simple strategy: if the derivative is negative at one end of an interval
  and positive at the other, then by the \emph{Intermediate Value Theorem}
  (a fundamental theorem of calculus stating that a continuous function
  must pass through every intermediate value), there must be a zero
  of $f'$ somewhere inside the interval.  We then halve the interval and
  repeat.

  \begin{block}{The Intermediate Value Theorem Applied to $f'$}
    If $f'$ is \textbf{continuous} (no sudden jumps or breaks) on $[a, b]$,
    and if $f'(a)$ and $f'(b)$ have \textbf{opposite signs} --- one is
    strictly positive and one is strictly negative --- then there exists at
    least one point $x^* \in (a, b)$ (strictly inside the interval) where
    $f'(x^*) = 0$.\\[4pt]
    In plain English: if the slope of $f$ is downward (negative $f'$) at
    the left end and upward (positive $f'$) at the right end, the slope
    must pass through zero somewhere in the middle.  That zero-crossing
    is exactly the location of a minimum.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{The Bisection Algorithm Applied to $f'$}
    We apply bisection to the function $f'$ (the derivative), treating
    zero-crossing of $f'$ as the root to be found.
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Check validity:} Verify $f'(a) \cdot f'(b) \le 0$
            (the product of two values of opposite sign is negative).
      \item \textbf{Evaluate midpoint:} Compute $m = (a + b)/2$ (m, the
            midpoint, halfway between $a$ and $b$) and $y_m = f'(m)$.
      \item \textbf{If $y_m = 0$:} We found the root exactly at $m$; stop.
      \item \textbf{If $\text{sign}(y_m) = \text{sign}(f'(a))$:}
            (they have the same sign, meaning the zero is in the right half):
            set $a \leftarrow m$ (discard the left half).
      \item \textbf{Otherwise:}
            set $b \leftarrow m$ (discard the right half).
      \item \textbf{Repeat} until $b - a < \varepsilon$ (epsilon, the
            interval width tolerance).
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Convergence Rate of Bisection}
    Each iteration \emph{halves} the interval, regardless of the function
    shape.  Starting from an interval of length $|b - a|$, after $n$
    iterations the interval has length $|b - a| \,/\, 2^n$ (the original
    length divided by $2$ to the power $n$).  To achieve precision
    $\varepsilon$ (epsilon, the target interval length):
    \[
      n = \left\lceil \log_2\!\left(\frac{|b - a|}{\varepsilon}\right) \right\rceil
      \quad \text{iterations are needed.}
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for the Convergence Formula}
    $2^n$ (two to the power $n$): the total shrinkage factor after $n$
    halvings.  After 10 halvings, the interval is $2^{10} = 1024$ times
    shorter.\\
    $|b - a|$ (absolute value of $b$ minus $a$): the initial interval length,
    always a positive number.\\
    $\log_2(\cdot)$ (logarithm base two, read ``log base two of''): counts
    how many times we need to halve to reach the target precision.  Specifically,
    $\log_2(N)$ is the number $n$ such that $2^n = N$.\\
    $\lceil \cdot \rceil$ (the ceiling function): rounds up to the nearest
    integer, since we need a whole number of iterations.\\
    $\varepsilon$ (epsilon): the desired final interval width.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Convergence Formula: One Bit Per Step}
    Each iteration of bisection produces exactly one additional correct
    \emph{bit} of the answer: it halves the uncertainty interval.  Ten
    iterations give $2^{10} = 1024$ times better precision.  Thirty
    iterations give $2^{30} \approx 10^9$ times better precision --- more
    than a billion-fold improvement.  This linear convergence rate is
    moderate (quadratic fit search is faster near the minimum), but it is
    \emph{completely reliable}: it works regardless of the function shape
    as long as $f'$ is continuous and brackets the zero.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Algorithm 3.6: Bisection Method in Python}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode, caption={Python: Bisection method applied to f\_prime to find a root on [a,b]}]
import numpy as np

def bisection(f_prime, a, b, eps=1e-6):
    """Find a root of f_prime on [a, b] using bisection.

    f_prime : callable -- the derivative of the objective function
    a, b    : bracket endpoints; must satisfy f_prime(a)*f_prime(b) <= 0
    eps     : interval width tolerance (default 1e-6)
    Returns (a, b) enclosing the root, with b - a <= eps.
    """
    if a > b:
        a, b = b, a           # ensure a < b regardless of user input

    ya, yb = f_prime(a), f_prime(b)

    if ya == 0:               # root is exactly at left endpoint
        return a, a
    if yb == 0:               # root is exactly at right endpoint
        return b, b

    sign_ya = np.sign(ya)     # record sign of left endpoint (+1 or -1)

    while b - a > eps:
        m  = (a + b) / 2.0   # midpoint
        ym = f_prime(m)       # evaluate derivative at midpoint
        if ym == 0:
            return m, m       # exact root found
        elif np.sign(ym) == sign_ya:
            a = m             # root is in right half [m, b]
        else:
            b = m             # root is in left half [a, m]

    return a, b
\end{lstlisting}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Algorithm 3.7: Finding a Sign-Change Bracket}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode,
  caption={Python: Expand [a, b] symmetrically until f\_prime has a sign change}]
import numpy as np

def bracket_sign_change(f_prime, a, b, k=2.0):
    """Expand [a, b] until f_prime(a) and f_prime(b) have opposite signs.

    k : expansion factor (each step, half-width grows by factor k)
    Returns (a, b) with f_prime(a) * f_prime(b) <= 0, suitable for bisection.
    """
    if a > b:
        a, b = b, a             # ensure a < b

    center     = (a + b) / 2.0
    half_width = (b - a) / 2.0

    while f_prime(a) * f_prime(b) > 0:
        half_width *= k         # expand the half-width by factor k
        a = center - half_width
        b = center + half_width

    return a, b
\end{lstlisting}

  \medskip
  This utility function prepares a starting bracket for bisection.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{center}: the midpoint of the initial $[a, b]$, kept fixed.
    \item \texttt{half\_width}: half the interval length; grows by factor $k$
          each iteration, so the interval expands symmetrically around the center.
    \item The loop exits as soon as $f'(a) \cdot f'(b) \le 0$, indicating
          opposite signs (or a zero at an endpoint).
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{alertblock}{Caveat: When the Sign-Change Search Can Fail}
    If two roots of $f'$ lie close together within the expanding interval,
    the signs may cancel: $f'(a)$ and $f'(b)$ could both be positive (or
    both negative) even though two roots of $f'$ are hiding inside the
    interval.  The expanding search would skip over both roots and grow
    the interval forever.  This is a known limitation when multiple critical
    points of $f$ are clustered close together.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Bisection: A Worked Numerical Example}

  Let us apply bisection to a concrete function and trace every iteration
  in detail.

  \begin{block}{Problem}
    Minimise $f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}x^2 - x$ (one-half $x$ squared minus $x$).
    We apply bisection to the derivative $f'(x) = x - 1$.  Initial bracket:
    $[a, b] = [0,\, 1000]$.
  \end{block}

  \textbf{Verifying the bracket is valid:}
  \[
    f'(0) = 0 - 1 = -1 < 0
    \quad \text{and} \quad
    f'(1000) = 1000 - 1 = 999 > 0.
  \]
  Since $f'(a) = -1$ is negative and $f'(b) = 999$ is positive, they have
  opposite signs.  The bracket is valid and the bisection algorithm guarantees
  it will find the root.

  \textbf{Iteration trace:}

  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}clllll@{}}
      \toprule
      Step & $a$ & $b$ & Midpoint $m$ & $f'(m) = m - 1$ & Action \\
      \midrule
      0 & 0 & 1000 & 500 & $499 > 0$ & Set $b \leftarrow 500$ \\
      1 & 0 & 500  & 250 & $249 > 0$ & Set $b \leftarrow 250$ \\
      2 & 0 & 250  & 125 & $124 > 0$ & Set $b \leftarrow 125$ \\
      3 & 0 & 125  & 62.5 & $61.5 > 0$ & Set $b \leftarrow 62.5$ \\
      4 & 0 & 62.5 & 31.25 & $30.25 > 0$ & Set $b \leftarrow 31.25$ \\
      5 & 0 & 31.25 & 15.625 & $14.625 > 0$ & Set $b \leftarrow 15.625$ \\
      $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ \\
      $\approx 30$ & $\approx 1$ & $\approx 1$ & $\approx 1$ & $\approx 0$ & Converged \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  The true minimum is at $x^* = 1$ (since $f'(1) = 1 - 1 = 0$).
  The number of iterations to reach tolerance $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$:
  \[
    n = \left\lceil \log_2\!\left(\frac{1000}{10^{-6}}\right) \right\rceil
      = \left\lceil \log_2(10^9) \right\rceil
      = \left\lceil 29.9 \right\rceil
      = 30.
  \]

  \begin{exampleblock}{Interpretation}
    Exactly 30 iterations are needed, regardless of the shape of $f$ or
    the location of the minimum.  Every iteration halves the interval;
    after 30 halvings, the original length of 1000 has been reduced to
    $1000 / 2^{30} \approx 9.3 \times 10^{-7} < 10^{-6}$.  This guaranteed
    convergence in a predictable number of steps is the hallmark of bisection.
  \end{exampleblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Brent-Dekker: Bisection Plus Super-Charged Speed}

  Pure bisection converges at a fixed linear rate (one correct bit per
  iteration).  For many smooth functions we can do much better near the root
  by fitting a low-degree polynomial through recent points and jumping to
  its zero.  However, these faster methods can occasionally produce points
  \emph{outside} the current bracket, destroying the convergence guarantee.

  The \textbf{Brent-Dekker method} (named after Richard Brent and Theodorus
  Dekker, who developed it independently in the early 1970s) resolves this
  tension by combining three methods:

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Bisection:} Always converges at linear rate $O(2^{-n})$
          (order $2$ to the $-n$, meaning the error halves each step).
          Used as a ``safety net'' whenever the faster methods are unsafe.
    \item \textbf{Secant method:} Fits a \emph{line} (a polynomial of degree
          one) through the two most recent points and finds where that line
          crosses zero.  Converges super-linearly at rate $\approx \varphi
          \approx 1.618$ (digits per digit) but can diverge or jump outside
          the bracket.
    \item \textbf{Inverse quadratic interpolation (IQI):} Fits a
          \emph{parabola} (a polynomial of degree two) through three recent
          points, but in the \emph{inverse} direction (treating $x$ as the
          function and $y = f'(x)$ as the variable, then evaluating at $y = 0$).
          Even faster than the secant method but can also diverge.
  \end{enumerate}

  At each step, Brent-Dekker uses IQI or the secant method if the predicted
  point falls inside the bracket and is sufficiently far from both endpoints.
  Otherwise it reverts to bisection.  The result is an algorithm that
  combines the \emph{reliability} of bisection with the \emph{speed} of
  quadratic methods.

  \begin{block}{Using Brent-Dekker in Python (via SciPy)}
    The standard Python scientific library \texttt{scipy} (Scientific Python)
    provides Brent-Dekker in two forms:

    \textbf{Root-finding form} (for when you want to find a zero of $f'$):
    \begin{center}
      \texttt{scipy.optimize.brentq(f\_prime, a, b)}
    \end{center}
    This finds a root of \texttt{f\_prime} on the interval
    \texttt{[a, b]}, requiring a sign change in \texttt{f\_prime} across
    the interval.

    \textbf{Minimisation form} (for when you want to minimise $f$ directly):
    \begin{center}
      \texttt{scipy.optimize.minimize\_scalar(f, bounds=(a, b), method='bounded')}
    \end{center}
    This minimises the scalar function $f$ on $[a, b]$ using Brent's hybrid
    of golden section and parabolic interpolation.  The result object's
    attribute \texttt{res.x} holds the minimiser and \texttt{res.fun} holds
    the minimum function value.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight: The Practical Standard}
    For any one-dimensional bracketing problem in practice, prefer
    \texttt{scipy.optimize.minimize\_scalar} with \texttt{method='bounded'}.
    It provides the guaranteed convergence of bisection (it \emph{always}
    converges), the speed of quadratic interpolation (typically super-linear
    near the minimum), and requires no tuning of parameters.  It is the
    method used inside most professional scientific computing software.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{3.8\ \ Summary of Chapter 3}

  This chapter introduced a hierarchy of methods for finding a minimum of a
  univariate (one-dimensional) function.  Each method makes progressively
  stronger assumptions about $f$ to achieve faster or more powerful convergence.
  The methods form a toolkit: we choose the appropriate tool based on what we
  know about the function and what resources (evaluations, derivative access,
  Lipschitz constant knowledge) we have.

  \begin{block}{All Key Methods at a Glance}
    \textbf{\texttt{bracket\_minimum}:} Finds an initial bracketing triple
    $(a, b, c)$ by stepping from $x_0$ and doubling the step size each time.
    Requires only the existence of a local minimum.  This is always the
    \emph{first} step before applying any refinement method.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Fibonacci search:} Given a fixed budget of $n$ evaluations on a
    unimodal function, this is the provably optimal strategy --- no other
    algorithm can guarantee a smaller final interval.  The bracket shrinks
    by the exact Fibonacci factor $F_{n+1}$.  Must know $n$ in advance.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Golden section search:} Uses the constant placement ratio
    $\rho = \varphi^{-1} \approx 0.618$ at every step.  Slightly suboptimal
    relative to Fibonacci for small $n$, but effectively identical for
    $n > 10$.  Can be run as an anytime algorithm.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Quadratic fit search:} Fits a parabola to three bracketing points
    and jumps to its vertex.  Achieves super-linear convergence near a smooth
    minimum.  Requires smoothness; safeguards needed for degenerate cases.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Shubert-Piyavskii:} A global optimisation method that builds a
    sawtooth lower bound using the Lipschitz constant $\ell$ (ell).
    Guaranteed to find the global minimum on $[a, b]$ even for multimodal
    functions, provided $\ell$ is known.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Bisection:} Root-finding applied to the derivative $f'$.
    Requires a bracket with opposite-sign derivatives.  Converges linearly
    (halves the interval each step).  Completely robust and predictable.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Brent-Dekker:} A hybrid of bisection, secant, and inverse
    quadratic interpolation.  As robust as bisection, as fast as quadratic
    methods in practice.  The standard choice for production scientific code.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{The Central Takeaway}
    Bracketing transforms the search for a minimum into a sequence of
    interval-narrowing decisions.  Every method in this chapter answers the
    same question --- ``where should we sample next to learn the most about
    where the minimum is?'' --- but with different assumptions and different
    guarantees.  Understanding the assumptions (unimodality, smoothness,
    Lipschitz bound, derivative availability) is the key to choosing the
    right method for any given problem.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Algorithm Comparison Table}

  The table below summarises the key properties of all methods in this chapter.
  Each row corresponds to one method; the columns capture the most important
  practical attributes for choosing between them.

  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lllll@{}}
      \toprule
      Algorithm & Needs $f'$? & Global? & Convergence & Budget \\
      \midrule
      \texttt{bracket\_minimum} & No & No & --- (setup only) & Varies \\
      Fibonacci search          & No & No & $O(F_{n+1}^{-1})$ & Fixed $n$ \\
      Golden section            & No & No & $O(\varphi^{-(n-1)})$ & Any \\
      Quadratic fit             & No & No & Super-linear & Any \\
      Shubert-Piyavskii         & No & \textbf{Yes} & Depends on $\ell$ & Adaptive \\
      Bisection                 & \textbf{Yes} & No & $O(2^{-n})$ & Fixed $\varepsilon$ \\
      Brent-Dekker              & \textbf{Yes} & No & Super-linear & Adaptive \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  \smallskip
  \textbf{Column glossary:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Needs $f'$?} --- Does the method require evaluating the
          first derivative (slope) of $f$?  Methods that do not are usable
          for black-box functions where derivatives are unavailable.
    \item \emph{Global?} --- Does the method guarantee finding the global
          minimum of a potentially multimodal function?
    \item \emph{Convergence} --- The rate at which the error decreases.
          $O(2^{-n})$ (order $2$ to the $-n$) means the error halves each
          step (linear convergence); super-linear means faster than any
          fixed ratio per step; $O(F_{n+1}^{-1})$ means the interval
          shrinks by the $(n+1)$-th Fibonacci number.
    \item \emph{Budget} --- Whether the evaluation count must be fixed in
          advance (Fixed $n$), set as a precision target (Fixed $\varepsilon$),
          or can be determined adaptively (Any / Adaptive).
  \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Selected Exercises with Full Solutions}

  These exercises test and deepen your understanding of the methods in this
  chapter.  Work through each exercise on your own first, then check the
  solution.

  \begin{block}{Exercise 3.1: When to Prefer Fibonacci Over Bisection}
    \textbf{Question:} Give a concrete example where Fibonacci search is
    the correct choice over bisection.\\[6pt]
    \textbf{Solution:} Use Fibonacci search whenever the \textbf{derivative
    $f'$ is not available}.  For example, suppose $f$ is the output of a
    physical experiment (measuring the fuel efficiency of an engine at different
    throttle settings), a neural network forward pass (no analytical gradient),
    or a legacy simulation tool.  In these cases, computing $f'(x)$ is
    impractical.  Bisection requires a sign change in $f'$ across the bracket,
    so it \emph{cannot} be used.  Fibonacci search requires only evaluations
    of $f$ itself.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Exercise 3.2: Main Drawback of Shubert-Piyavskii}
    \textbf{Question:} What is the main practical limitation of the
    Shubert-Piyavskii method?\\[6pt]
    \textbf{Solution:} The method requires knowing a valid \textbf{Lipschitz
    constant} $\ell$ (ell) in advance.  In practice, $\ell$ is often
    unknown.  If $\ell$ is too small, the sawtooth lower bound may lie
    \emph{above} the true function at some points, violating the guarantee
    that the sawtooth is a valid lower bound.  If $\ell$ is too large, the
    V-shapes are very wide, the sawtooth is very low (a very loose bound),
    and convergence is extremely slow.  There is no automatic way to choose
    $\ell$ optimally without additional knowledge of $f$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Exercise 3.5: Validity of a Lipschitz Constant}
    \textbf{Question:} Is $\ell = 2$ a valid Lipschitz constant for
    $f(x) = (x + 2)^2$ on $[0,\, 1]$?\\[6pt]
    \textbf{Solution:} No.  The derivative is $f'(x) = 2(x + 2)$.  On $[0, 1]$
    this ranges from $f'(0) = 2(0 + 2) = 4$ at the left end to
    $f'(1) = 2(1 + 2) = 6$ at the right end.  A valid Lipschitz constant
    must be an upper bound on $|f'(x)|$ at every point in $[0, 1]$.  The
    maximum is $|f'(1)| = 6$.  Since $\ell = 2 < 6$, it is \emph{not}
    a valid Lipschitz constant for this function on this interval.  Choosing
    $\ell = 2$ would produce a sawtooth lower bound that violates the
    true function at some points.  A safe choice is any $\ell \ge 6$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Exercise 3.6: Fibonacci Budget for a Given Precision}
    \textbf{Question:} With 3 evaluations on $[1,\, 32]$, can we guarantee
    narrowing the optimum to an interval of length $\le 10$?\\[6pt]
    \textbf{Solution:} No.  With $n = 3$ evaluations, Fibonacci search
    shrinks the interval by $F_{n+1} = F_4 = 3$.  The final interval
    has length:
    \[
      \frac{b - a}{F_4} = \frac{32 - 1}{3} = \frac{31}{3} \approx 10.33 > 10.
    \]
    We cannot guarantee an interval of length $\le 10$ with only 3 evaluations.
    With $n = 4$ evaluations: $F_5 = 5$, giving a guaranteed length of
    $31/5 = 6.2 < 10$.  So 4 evaluations suffice.
  \end{block}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Looking Ahead: From One Dimension to Many}

  The one-dimensional bracketing methods in this chapter are not just
  standalone tools --- they are the fundamental building blocks that
  multi-dimensional optimisation algorithms call \emph{repeatedly}.

  \textbf{Chapter 4: Local Descent Methods.}
  When minimising a function of many variables $f(\mathbf{x})$ (bold
  $\mathbf{x}$ denotes a \emph{vector} of inputs, such as
  $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_d)$), a common strategy is to move
  in one direction at a time.  The amount to move in a chosen direction
  $\mathbf{d}$ (d bold, a direction vector) is the \textbf{step size}
  $\alpha$ (alpha, pronounced ``al-fah'', a non-negative real number).
  Choosing $\alpha$ to minimise $f(\mathbf{x} + \alpha \mathbf{d})$
  as a function of the scalar $\alpha$ alone is a one-dimensional
  minimisation problem --- called a \textbf{line search} --- and golden
  section or Brent's method is used to solve it.

  \textbf{Line search in gradient descent, conjugate gradient, and
  quasi-Newton methods.}
  All of these widely-used multi-dimensional methods call a 1D bracketing
  subroutine at each outer iteration.  The efficiency of the line search
  directly determines the speed of the outer loop.

  \textbf{Shubert-Piyavskii and higher-dimensional global optimisation.}
  The Lipschitz lower bound idea extends to multiple dimensions.  The
  DIRECT algorithm (Dividing Rectangles) and the LIPO method generalise
  the sawtooth lower bound to boxes in $d$-dimensional space.  However,
  the number of evaluations needed grows exponentially with the dimension
  $d$ (the ``curse of dimensionality''), so these methods are typically
  limited to problems with $d \le 20$ or so.

  \begin{alertblock}{The Big Picture}
    Every multi-dimensional optimiser studied in later chapters has a
    one-dimensional sub-problem at its heart.  The bracketing methods
    here --- the bracket-finding heuristic, golden section search, and
    Brent-Dekker --- will appear again and again as key subroutines.
    A solid understanding of this chapter is therefore the foundation
    on which everything else in the course is built.
  \end{alertblock}
