\section{History of Optimization}
%% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{A Brief History of Optimization}

Optimization is one of the oldest intellectual pursuits in mathematics.
Long before computers existed, thinkers were asking: ``What is the \emph{best}
arrangement, shape, or strategy?''  Understanding this history helps us see
why the mathematical tools we use today look the way they do, and why they
are so effective across so many different fields.

  \begin{block}{Ancient Roots (300--250 BC)}
    The Greeks posed geometric extremum problems --- questions such as ``which
    shape encloses the largest area for a fixed perimeter?''  \textbf{Euclid}
    ($\sim$300 BC) established rigorous geometric reasoning, and showed that among
    all rectangles with a given perimeter, the square has the largest area.
    \textbf{Archimedes} ($\sim$250 BC) studied the problem of maximising the area
    of a polygon inscribed in a circle.  As the number of polygon sides grows
    without bound, the polygon area approaches the circle area --- a conceptual
    forerunner of the \emph{calculus of variations}, which optimises over
    \emph{shapes} rather than just numbers.  Much later, \textbf{Fibonacci}
    (1170--1250) popularised algebra and the idea that mathematics could be
    applied to solve practical problems of commerce and engineering, planting
    seeds that would eventually grow into modern optimization theory.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{The Calculus Era: 17th and 18th Centuries}
    The invention of calculus made it possible to study \emph{rates of change}
    precisely.  This turns out to be exactly what is needed to find maxima and
    minima --- because at a maximum or minimum, the rate of change of the function
    must be zero.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Fermat (1643)} observed that the derivative of a smooth function
    must equal zero at an interior maximum or minimum.  This single insight ---
    ``set the derivative to zero and solve'' --- remains the cornerstone of
    optimization theory to this day.  Every gradient-based algorithm in this book
    is, at its heart, searching for a point where the derivative vanishes.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Newton and Leibniz (1660s--1680s)} independently developed
    differential calculus, providing the machinery to compute derivatives
    systematically and to identify \emph{stationary points} of arbitrary
    functions.  Newton also gave us an iterative root-finding procedure
    (Newton's method) that, when applied to the derivative, directly finds
    stationary points; this is the basis of Newton's optimization method
    in Chapter 6.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Euler and Lagrange (1740--1770s)} extended these ideas to
    \emph{functions of functions}, founding the \emph{calculus of variations}.
    Lagrange (pronounced ``la-GRON-zh'') also introduced his celebrated
    \emph{multiplier method} for handling equality constraints.
    In Lagrange's method, a new variable $\lambda$ (lambda) is introduced for
    each constraint, and the constrained problem is converted into an
    unconstrained one involving a function called the \emph{Lagrangian}.
    This technique is still used daily in engineering and economics; we cover
    it fully in Chapter 10.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{The Modern Era: Numerical and Computational Optimization}
    The 19th and 20th centuries brought two revolutions: the formalisation of
    numerical algorithms, and the arrival of computers to run them at scale.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Cauchy (1847)} proposed the \emph{steepest-descent method} ---
    the first algorithm that iteratively improves a solution by moving in the
    direction of the negative gradient $-\nabla f$ (negative nabla f, meaning
    the direction in which the function decreases most steeply).  This is the
    direct ancestor of every modern gradient-based optimizer, including the Adam
    algorithm that trains GPT-style language models today.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Kantorovich (1939)} formulated \emph{linear programming} to solve
    production-planning problems efficiently --- specifically, how to allocate
    resources such as labour and materials to maximise output subject to capacity
    constraints.  His work was so influential that he was awarded the Nobel Prize
    in Economics in 1975.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Dantzig (1947)} invented the \emph{Simplex algorithm}, which made
    linear programming computationally tractable and is still widely used today.
    The Simplex algorithm moves along the edges of the feasible polytope (the
    geometric shape defined by the linear constraints) from vertex to vertex,
    always improving the objective, until it reaches the optimal vertex.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Kuhn and Tucker (1951)} derived the \emph{KKT conditions} ---
    the necessary conditions for constrained optimality that generalise Fermat's
    derivative condition to problems with both inequality and equality constraints.
    KKT stands for Karush--Kuhn--Tucker (Karush independently derived them
    earlier in 1939, but his master's thesis went unnoticed for decades).

    \medskip
    \textbf{The deep-learning era (2012--present)}: modern language models and
    vision systems (GPT, Llama, ResNet) are trained by minimising a loss
    function over \emph{billions} of parameters using variants of stochastic
    gradient descent.  Optimization is literally the engine that makes AI work.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Takeaway}
    Every major advance in optimization was driven by a practical need: geometry,
    mechanics, economics, manufacturing, and now machine learning.  The algorithms
    in this book are the latest chapter in a 2000-year story --- and the story
    is still being written.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{The Optimization Process}
%% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{The Optimization Process}

Optimization is not just a mathematical operation --- it is a \emph{process}
that begins with a real-world problem and ends with an implementable design.
Understanding this process prevents the common mistake of jumping straight to
an algorithm without first carefully formulating the problem.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{The Five-Step Engineering Cycle}
        \begin{enumerate}
          \item \textbf{Specify the design problem.}  What physical or business
            system are you trying to improve?  What can you actually change
            (the \emph{design variables})?  What outcome do you care about
            (the \emph{objective})?  Being precise here prevents wasted effort later.

          \item \textbf{Choose a mathematical formulation.}  Translate the
            problem into a precise mathematical form: define the objective
            function $f(\vx)$ (f of x), the design vector $\vx$ (bold x), and any constraints.

          \item \textbf{Select an optimization algorithm.}  Different algorithms
            suit different problem structures.  The choice depends on the number
            of variables, whether gradients are available, and the computational
            budget.  This entire book is devoted to helping you make this choice well.

          \item \textbf{Evaluate the design.}  Run simulations, physical
            experiments, or analytical calculations to compute $f(\vx)$ for
            candidate designs.  This step is often the computational bottleneck.

          \item \textbf{Iterate until convergence.}  Repeat steps 3--4,
            updating $\vx$ according to the algorithm's rule, until improvement
            stalls or a stopping criterion is met.  Then check whether the
            solution is physically meaningful.
        \end{enumerate}
      \end{block}

    \column{0.50\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_opt_process}
      \vspace{0.3em}
      \begin{exampleblock}{Representative Design Problems}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Aerospace}: choose airfoil chord (front-to-back length),
            thickness, and sweep angle to minimise aerodynamic drag subject to
            minimum-lift and structural-integrity constraints.
          \item \textbf{Structural engineering}: choose bicycle frame tube
            diameters to minimise mass subject to maximum stress limits.
          \item \textbf{Control}: tune a vehicle controller's gain parameters to
            minimise tracking error subject to actuator limits.
          \item \textbf{Machine learning}: adjust neural network weights to
            minimise prediction error on training data.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{exampleblock}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{How to Choose an Algorithm}
    Before selecting an algorithm, you must characterise your problem along
    several dimensions.  There is no universally best choice --- the right
    algorithm depends on problem structure.  Here are the key questions to ask.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Dimensionality} ($n$, the number of design variables): low-dimensional
    problems ($n < 10$) allow exhaustive or derivative-free search, while
    high-dimensional problems ($n > 1000$) typically require gradient-based methods
    because they scale much better with $n$.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Gradient availability}: some problems supply exact gradients
    analytically or via automatic differentiation (Chapters 2--3); others only
    allow function evaluations, requiring derivative-free methods (Chapter 4 onward).

    \medskip
    \textbf{Constraint structure}: unconstrained problems are simpler to handle;
    bound constraints (Chapter 4), equality constraints, and general inequality
    constraints (Chapter 10) each require specialised treatment.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Evaluation budget}: if each evaluation of $f(\vx)$ requires an
    hour-long Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulation, you can afford far
    fewer iterations than if $f(\vx)$ evaluates in microseconds.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Noise and stochasticity}: if $f(\vx)$ is corrupted by random noise
    (e.g., measured experimentally), stochastic optimizers (Chapters 8--9) are
    more appropriate than deterministic gradient descent.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Multi-modality}: if the objective landscape has many local minima,
    global search strategies such as genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, or
    Bayesian optimization may be necessary to avoid being trapped.
  \end{block}

  \begin{exampleblock}{The No-Free-Lunch Theorem (informal)}
    No single algorithm outperforms all others \emph{averaged over all possible
    problems}.  This result, proved by Wolpert and Macready (1997), is not a
    counsel of despair --- it means that by \emph{knowing your problem structure}
    you can choose an algorithm that works well for your specific situation.
    An algorithm that performs poorly on random problems can be excellent on
    structured problems like those arising in engineering.
  \end{exampleblock}

  \begin{block}{A Survey of Algorithm Families}
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Family} & \textbf{Information used} & \textbf{Representative method} \\
      \midrule
      First-order  & Gradient $\grad f$ (nabla f) & Gradient descent (Ch.\ 5) \\
      Second-order & Gradient + Hessian $\hess f$ (nabla-squared f) & Newton's method (Ch.\ 6) \\
      Stochastic   & Noisy gradient samples & SGD, Adam (Ch.\ 8) \\
      Population   & Many function values & Genetic algorithms (Ch.\ 14) \\
      Surrogate    & Fitted model of $f$ & Bayesian optimization (Ch.\ 17) \\
      Direct search & Function values only & Nelder--Mead (Ch.\ 4) \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Takeaway}
    The optimization process is a cycle, not a one-shot calculation.
    Formulate carefully, choose the algorithm that fits your problem's structure,
    and iterate --- checking at every step that the mathematics matches
    the real-world intent.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\section{Mathematical Formulation}
%% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Mathematical Formulation of an Optimization Problem}

Before an algorithm can be applied, the real-world problem must be translated
into a precise mathematical statement.  This section introduces the standard
form used throughout the book.  Every optimization problem in this text can
be written in this form, so it is worth spending time to understand it thoroughly.

  \begin{block}{Standard Form of an Optimization Problem}
    An optimization problem is written as:
    \[
      \min_{\vx \in \mathcal{X}} \; f(\vx)
      \quad \text{subject to} \quad
      g_i(\vx) \leq 0, \; i = 1,\ldots,m,
      \quad
      h_j(\vx) = 0, \; j = 1,\ldots,p
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation: Every Symbol Explained in Plain Language}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\vx = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)^\top \in \R^n$ is the
        \textbf{design vector} (also called the \emph{decision variable} or
        \emph{optimisation variable}).  It is a column vector containing $n$
        real numbers that the algorithm is free to choose.  The superscript
        $\top$ (top) means ``transpose'' --- turning a row of numbers into
        a column.  Each component $x_i$ (x sub $i$) is one design parameter:
        for example, in wing design, $x_1$ might be the chord length, $x_2$ the
        thickness-to-chord ratio, and so on.  The notation $\R^n$ (blackboard R
        to the power n) denotes the set of all $n$-tuples of real numbers.

      \item $f : \R^n \to \R$ is the \textbf{objective function}.  This notation
        means $f$ takes an $n$-dimensional input vector $\vx$ and returns a
        single real number, measuring how ``good'' that design is.  We write
        \emph{min} to indicate we want the smallest possible value; if you want
        to maximise, simply minimise $-f$.

      \item $g_i(\vx) \leq 0$ for $i = 1, \ldots, m$ are the
        \textbf{inequality constraints}.  There are $m$ (m) of them.
        The subscript $i$ indexes each constraint.
        Writing $g_i \leq 0$ is a universal convention: any constraint can be
        rewritten in this form.  For example, ``thrust $\geq 500$ N'' becomes
        $-\text{thrust}(\vx) + 500 \leq 0$.

      \item $h_j(\vx) = 0$ for $j = 1, \ldots, p$ are the
        \textbf{equality constraints}.  There are $p$ (p) of them.
        The subscript $j$ indexes each one.
        For example, ``total portfolio weight equals 1'' becomes
        $\sum_{i=1}^n x_i - 1 = 0$, where $\sum$ (Sigma, meaning ``sum'')
        adds up all $n$ weights.

      \item $\mathcal{X}$ (calligraphic X, read ``script X'') is the
        \textbf{feasible set}: the collection of all vectors $\vx$ that
        simultaneously satisfy every constraint.  Only points inside
        $\mathcal{X}$ are valid candidate solutions.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read This Formula}
    The expression $\min_{\vx \in \mathcal{X}} f(\vx)$ is read aloud as:
    ``find the value of $\vx$ that lies inside the feasible set $\mathcal{X}$
    and produces the smallest possible output of $f$.''

    Two failure modes exist.
    If the feasible set is empty (the constraints are contradictory --- for
    example, requiring $x \geq 5$ and $x \leq 3$ simultaneously), the problem
    has \emph{no solution} because there are no valid candidate points.
    If $f$ can decrease without bound inside $\mathcal{X}$ (for instance,
    minimising $-e^{x}$ over all real $x$), the problem is \emph{unbounded}
    and also has no finite solution.  A well-posed problem has a non-empty
    feasible set and a finite minimum.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{The Feasible Set in Detail}
        The feasible set collects every design point that respects all
        constraints simultaneously:
        \[
          \mathcal{X} = \{\,\vx \;\mid\; g_i(\vx)\leq 0 \;\forall\,i,\;
          h_j(\vx)=0 \;\forall\,j\,\}
        \]
        Here the curly braces $\{\ldots\}$ denote ``the set of all $\vx$ such
        that $\ldots$'', and the vertical bar $\mid$ is read ``such that.''
        The symbol $\forall$ (for all) means ``for every.''
        A point $\vx$ is called \textbf{feasible} when it belongs to
        $\mathcal{X}$, and \textbf{infeasible} otherwise.

        \medskip
        The feasible set can be a complicated geometric region --- a polygon,
        a curved surface, or even a disconnected collection of points.  The
        figure on the right illustrates a two-dimensional example where the
        feasible region is shaded in grey; the optimizer must find the lowest
        point of $f$ within that shaded area.
      \end{block}

      \vspace{0.4em}

      \begin{block}{Three Types of Constraint}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Inequality}: $g(\vx) \leq 0$ --- defines a half-space
            or a curved region.  The boundary $g(\vx) = 0$ is called an
            \emph{active constraint} when the optimal solution lies on it.
          \item \textbf{Equality}: $h(\vx) = 0$ --- restricts $\vx$ to a
            lower-dimensional surface (e.g., a plane through the origin in 3-D
            space becomes a line, reducing three degrees of freedom to two).
          \item \textbf{Box / bound}: $l_i \leq x_i \leq u_i$ --- each variable
            is confined to a closed interval $[l_i, u_i]$; these can be written
            as two separate inequality constraints.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}

    \column{0.50\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_feasible_set}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Feasible Set --- Worked Numerical Example}

To make the abstract standard form concrete, let us work through a small
two-dimensional example completely by hand.  This illustrates how constraints
shape the set of allowable solutions and why the constrained optimum may differ
from the unconstrained one.

  \begin{exampleblock}{Example: A 2-D Constrained Problem}
    We wish to minimise the squared distance from the point $(2, 1)$:
    \[
      \min_{x_1,\, x_2} \; f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - 2)^2 + (x_2 - 1)^2
    \]
    subject to three constraints:
    \[
      g_1:\; x_1 + x_2 \leq 2, \qquad
      g_2:\; x_1 \geq 0, \qquad
      g_3:\; x_2 \geq 0.
    \]
    Here $x_1$ (x sub 1) and $x_2$ (x sub 2) are the two decision variables.
    The objective $f$ measures the squared Euclidean distance between the
    current point $(x_1, x_2)$ and the fixed target point $(2, 1)$.
    The constraints $g_2$ and $g_3$ restrict us to the first quadrant (both
    coordinates non-negative), while $g_1$ cuts off the upper-right corner.
  \end{exampleblock}

  \begin{block}{Step 1: Find the Unconstrained Minimum}
    Without any constraints, we minimise $f$ by finding where its gradient
    is zero.  Since $f = (x_1-2)^2 + (x_2-1)^2$ is a sum of squares, it achieves
    its minimum value of $0$ exactly at the target point $(x_1, x_2) = (2, 1)$.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Check feasibility}: does $(2, 1)$ satisfy the constraint $g_1$?
    \[
      x_1 + x_2 = 2 + 1 = 3 \not\leq 2. \quad \text{\textcolor{red}{Infeasible!}}
    \]
    The unconstrained optimum violates the first constraint, so we cannot
    simply ignore the constraints here.  We must look for the best feasible point.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Step 2: Examine the Active Boundary}
    Because the unconstrained minimum is outside $\mathcal{X}$, the constrained
    minimum must lie \emph{on the boundary} of the feasible set.  The boundary
    closest to $(2,1)$ is the line $x_1 + x_2 = 2$ (where $g_1$ is active,
    meaning satisfied with equality).  We restrict to this boundary and minimise.

    \medskip
    Substituting $x_2 = 2 - x_1$ into $f$:
    \[
      f(x_1, 2-x_1) = (x_1-2)^2 + (2-x_1-1)^2 = (x_1-2)^2 + (1-x_1)^2.
    \]
    Expanding: $= x_1^2 - 4x_1 + 4 + x_1^2 - 2x_1 + 1 = 2x_1^2 - 6x_1 + 5$.
    Setting the derivative to zero: $4x_1 - 6 = 0 \Rightarrow x_1^* = 1.5$.
    Then $x_2^* = 2 - 1.5 = 0.5$.
    \[
      f(1.5,\, 0.5) = (1.5-2)^2 + (0.5-1)^2 = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5.
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}
      \begin{exampleblock}{Classification of Sample Points}
        \centering
        \begin{tabular}{llll}
          \toprule
          \textbf{Point} & \textbf{Feasible?} & $f$ \textbf{value} & \textbf{Note} \\
          \midrule
          $(0, 0)$   & Yes & $4 + 1 = 5$   & Corner of $\mathcal{X}$ \\
          $(1, 1)$   & Yes & $1 + 0 = 1$   & Boundary ($g_1$ active) \\
          $(1.5, 0.5)$ & Yes & $0.5$ & \textbf{Constrained optimum} \\
          $(2, 1)$   & \textcolor{red}{No}  & $0$           & Unconstrained min \\
          \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
      \end{exampleblock}
      \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight}
        When the unconstrained minimum is infeasible, the constrained
        minimum is typically found on the \emph{boundary} of the feasible set ---
        specifically, at the feasible point closest to the unconstrained optimum.
        In this example, the constraint $g_1$ is active at the solution: the
        optimal point is literally being ``pushed against'' the boundary.
      \end{alertblock}

    \column{0.42\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Checking Feasibility Systematically}
        For any candidate point $(x_1, x_2)$, feasibility requires:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item Constraint $g_1$: compute $g_1 = x_1 + x_2 - 2$.
            Feasible if and only if $g_1 \leq 0$, i.e.\ $x_1 + x_2 \leq 2$.
          \item Constraint $g_2$: compute $g_2 = -x_1$.
            Feasible iff $g_2 \leq 0$, i.e.\ $x_1 \geq 0$.
          \item Constraint $g_3$: compute $g_3 = -x_2$.
            Feasible iff $g_3 \leq 0$, i.e.\ $x_2 \geq 0$.
        \end{itemize}
        All three conditions must hold simultaneously for the point to lie
        in $\mathcal{X}$.  This is straightforward to check in code by
        evaluating each constraint function and testing its sign.
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Constrained Optimisation in Python --- SciPy Example}

Python's \texttt{scipy.optimize.minimize} function can solve this problem
automatically.  The code below sets up the objective, constraints, and bounds,
then calls the SLSQP solver (Sequential Least Squares Programming, pronounced
``S-L-S-Q-P''), which is designed specifically for smooth constrained problems.
SLSQP works by solving a sequence of small quadratic subproblems, each a
second-order approximation to the original problem near the current iterate.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
      \begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode,caption={Constrained optimization with SciPy}]
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

# Objective: f(x) = (x1-2)^2 + (x2-1)^2
def f(x):
    return (x[0]-2)**2 + (x[1]-1)**2

# Constraints: x1+x2 <= 2, x1>=0, x2>=0
# SciPy 'ineq' means fun(x) >= 0, so we write
# 2 - x1 - x2 >= 0  <=>  x1+x2 <= 2
constraints = [
    {'type': 'ineq', 'fun': lambda x: 2 - x[0] - x[1]},
]
# Bounds encode x1 >= 0 and x2 >= 0
bounds = [(0, None), (0, None)]

x0 = np.array([0.5, 0.5])   # initial guess
result = minimize(f, x0,
                  method='SLSQP',
                  bounds=bounds,
                  constraints=constraints)

print("Optimal x:", result.x)
print("f(x*)   :", result.fun)
# Optimal x: [1.5  0.5]
# f(x*)   : 0.5
      \end{lstlisting}

    \column{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Reading the SciPy API}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \texttt{method='SLSQP'}: the solver algorithm.  SLSQP handles
            both inequality and equality constraints by solving a sequence of
            quadratic subproblems.
          \item \texttt{bounds}: a list of \texttt{(lower, upper)} pairs ---
            one per variable.  \texttt{None} means no bound on that side,
            i.e.\ the variable is unconstrained in that direction.
          \item \texttt{constraints}: a list of dictionaries.  \texttt{'type':
            'ineq'} means $\text{fun}(\vx) \geq 0$.  This is the
            \emph{opposite} sign from our $g_i \leq 0$ convention, so you
            may need to negate your constraint function.
          \item \texttt{result.x}: the optimal design vector $\vx^*$.
          \item \texttt{result.fun}: the optimal objective value $f(\vx^*)$.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
      \begin{exampleblock}{Verifying the Output}
        The solver returns $\vx^* = (1.5,\, 0.5)$ with $f(\vx^*) = 0.5$,
        exactly matching our hand calculation.  Checking constraint $g_1$:
        $1.5 + 0.5 = 2 \leq 2$ --- the constraint is \emph{active}
        (satisfied with equality), confirming the solution lies on the boundary
        and agrees with our geometric argument.
      \end{exampleblock}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\section{Applications}
%% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Applications of Optimization}

Optimization pervades nearly every quantitative field.  The five examples
below show how the same mathematical framework --- choose $\vx$ (bold x, the
design vector) to minimise $f(\vx)$ subject to constraints --- applies across
very different domains, from aerodynamics to medicine.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}

      \begin{block}{Aircraft Design (Aeronautics)}
        An aircraft designer wants to minimise aerodynamic drag $D(\vx)$,
        which directly determines fuel consumption and operating cost.
        The design vector $\vx$ collects geometric parameters: chord length
        (the front-to-back distance of the wing cross-section), thickness-to-chord
        ratio (how thick the wing is relative to its length), and sweep angle
        (how far back the wing is angled).

        \medskip
        The constraints enforce that the wing must generate at least a minimum
        amount of lift to keep the aircraft airborne, and that structural stresses
        remain below the material's yield limit.  Each evaluation of $D(\vx)$
        requires a Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulation taking hours of
        computing time, so the optimizer must extract maximum information from a
        limited evaluation budget (typically tens to hundreds of evaluations).
      \end{block}

      \vspace{0.4em}

      \begin{block}{Deep Learning (Machine Learning)}
        Training a neural network means finding a weight vector $\vx \in \R^n$
        (where $n$ can be billions) that minimises the \emph{cross-entropy loss}
        $\mathcal{L}(\vx)$ (calligraphic L, meaning the loss function), which
        measures the discrepancy between the network's predictions and the true
        labels.  Because computing the loss over the entire dataset at each step
        is too slow for large datasets, we use \emph{Stochastic Gradient Descent}
        (SGD) or Adam, which approximate the gradient using a random mini-batch
        (a small random subset of training data) at each iteration.
      \end{block}

    \column{0.50\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_dl_loss}
      \vspace{0.3em}
      \begin{block}{}
        \scriptsize The figure shows typical training and validation loss curves
        during neural-network optimization.  The horizontal axis is the iteration
        (training step) number; the vertical axis is the loss value $\mathcal{L}$.
        The training loss (solid line) decreases monotonically as the optimizer
        adjusts the weights.  The validation loss (dashed line) initially tracks
        the training loss, then may rise if the model starts to overfit
        (memorise the training data rather than learning general patterns).
        A practitioner stops training when the validation loss begins to rise.
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Financial Portfolio Optimization (Markowitz, 1952)}
        Harry Markowitz showed that portfolio selection can be cast as a
        quadratic program.  Let $\vx \in \R^n$ be the vector of portfolio
        weights, where $x_i$ (x sub $i$) is the fraction of total wealth
        invested in asset $i$.

        \medskip
        \textbf{Objective}: minimise portfolio variance (risk):
        \[
          \vx^\top \Sigma\, \vx
        \]
        where $\Sigma$ (Sigma, pronounced ``SIG-ma'') is the $n\times n$
        covariance matrix encoding how assets move together.
        The notation $\vx^\top \Sigma \vx$ (x-transpose Sigma x) is called
        a \emph{quadratic form}: for a two-asset portfolio with
        $\vx = (x_1, x_2)$, it equals
        $\sigma_{11} x_1^2 + 2\sigma_{12} x_1 x_2 + \sigma_{22} x_2^2$,
        where $\sigma_{ij}$ (sigma sub ij) is the covariance between assets
        $i$ and $j$.  A larger value means more risk.

        \medskip
        Simultaneously, we require the expected return
        $\boldsymbol{\mu}^\top \vx \geq r_{\min}$
        (where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, bold mu, is the vector of expected returns
        and $r_{\min}$ is the minimum acceptable return), plus
        $\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = 1$ (invest all wealth) and $x_i \geq 0$ (no
        short-selling).

        \medskip
        Solving for many values of $r_{\min}$ and plotting the resulting
        (risk, return) pairs traces the \textbf{efficient frontier}.
      \end{block}

    \column{0.50\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_portfolio}
      \vspace{0.3em}
      \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Markowitz Formula}
        The portfolio variance $\vx^\top \Sigma \vx$ increases when:
        (1) individual weights $x_i$ are large (concentrated bets), or
        (2) assets are positively correlated (they all move up or down
        together, giving no diversification benefit).
        Diversification works by choosing assets with \emph{low or negative}
        correlations, so their fluctuations partially cancel out.
      \end{alertblock}
      \vspace{0.3em}
      \begin{exampleblock}{Python Note}
        Use \texttt{scipy.optimize.minimize} with \texttt{method='SLSQP'},
        the equality constraint $\mathbf{1}^\top \vx = 1$ (sum of weights
        equals one), and bounds $x_i \in [0, 1]$.  Solve for a range of
        target returns $r_{\min}$ to trace the efficient frontier.
      \end{exampleblock}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Medical Radiation Therapy Planning}
        In cancer radiotherapy, the goal is to deliver a prescribed radiation
        dose to the tumour while minimising collateral damage to surrounding
        healthy tissue.  The optimizer chooses the \emph{beam intensities}
        (how strong each radiation beam is) and \emph{beam angles} (the directions
        from which beams enter the body), all collected in the design vector $\vx$.

        \medskip
        The objective is a risk measure over the spatial dose distribution ---
        for example, the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR, pronounced ``C-VAR'')
        of the dose received by critical organs such as the spinal cord.
        Constraints require that the tumour receives at least the prescribed
        minimum dose everywhere within the tumour volume.

        \medskip
        This is typically a large-scale linear or quadratic program, solved by
        interior-point methods (Chapter 11).  The stakes are high: a poor
        solution can under-dose the tumour (missing cancer cells) or over-dose
        healthy tissue (causing long-term side effects).
      \end{block}

      \vspace{0.4em}

      \begin{block}{Job Scheduling (Combinatorial Optimization)}
        Given $n$ jobs and $m$ machines, we want to assign jobs to machines
        to minimise the \emph{makespan} (the time until all jobs are finished).
        The number of possible orderings grows as $n!$ (n factorial, meaning
        $n \times (n-1) \times \cdots \times 2 \times 1$): for $n = 20$, that
        is over $2 \times 10^{18}$ possibilities.  Exhaustive search is
        completely impractical for large $n$, so heuristics such as simulated
        annealing and genetic algorithms are used instead.
      \end{block}

    \column{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{Cross-Domain Summary}
        Despite their apparent differences, all five applications share
        the same mathematical skeleton: choose a vector of decision variables
        to minimise a scalar objective subject to constraints.
        \medskip
        \centering
        \begin{tabular}{lll}
          \toprule
          \textbf{Domain} & \textbf{Minimise} & \textbf{Variables} \\
          \midrule
          Aeronautics   & Drag $D(\vx)$              & Wing geometry \\
          Deep learning & Loss $\mathcal{L}(\vx)$    & Network weights \\
          Finance       & Portfolio variance           & Asset fractions \\
          Medicine      & Organ dose risk              & Beam intensities \\
          Manufacturing & Makespan                    & Job--machine assign.\ \\
          \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
        \medskip
        Mastering the standard form unlocks all these applications simultaneously.
      \end{alertblock}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\section{Minima: Local vs.\ Global}
%% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Types of Minima}

Not all ``low points'' of a function are equally desirable.  An algorithm
might converge to a point that is locally optimal but globally suboptimal ---
like resting in a small valley while a much deeper valley exists elsewhere in
the landscape.  This section defines precisely what we mean by local and global
minima, so we can reason clearly about what an algorithm has and has not achieved.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}

      \begin{block}{Global Minimum}
        A point $\vx^*$ (x-star, the ``starred'' or optimal point) is a
        \textbf{global minimum} of $f$ over $\mathcal{X}$ if
        \[
          f(\vx^*) \leq f(\vx) \quad \text{for all } \vx \in \mathcal{X}.
        \]
        In plain words: no other feasible point produces a smaller objective
        value.  If the inequality is strict --- $f(\vx^*) < f(\vx)$ for all
        $\vx \neq \vx^*$ --- then $\vx^*$ is the \emph{unique} global minimum.

        \medskip
        Finding the global minimum of a general non-convex function is
        NP-hard in the worst case --- no polynomial-time algorithm can guarantee
        finding it without essentially examining every point.  For convex
        functions (Chapters 5 and 6), every local minimum is also a global
        minimum, which is one reason convexity is so prized.
      \end{block}

      \vspace{0.4em}

      \begin{block}{Local Minimum}
        A point $\vx^*$ is a \textbf{local minimum} if there exists a radius
        $\delta > 0$ (delta, pronounced ``DEL-tah'', representing a small
        positive real number) such that:
        \[
          f(\vx^*) \leq f(\vx)
          \quad \text{for all } \vx \in \mathcal{X} \text{ with }
          \|\vx - \vx^*\| < \delta.
        \]
        Here $\|\vx - \vx^*\|$ (double-bar norm, read ``the norm of x minus
        x-star'') denotes the Euclidean distance between $\vx$ and $\vx^*$.
        The condition says: within a ball of radius $\delta$ (delta) centred at
        $\vx^*$, no other feasible point achieves a smaller $f$ value.
        Outside this ball, there may well be points with smaller $f$ values.

        \medskip
        A \textbf{strong local minimum} satisfies the strict inequality
        $f(\vx^*) < f(\vx)$ for all $\vx \neq \vx^*$ within the ball.
        Every global minimum is also a local minimum, but the converse is false.
      \end{block}

    \column{0.50\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_local_global}
      \vspace{0.3em}
      \begin{exampleblock}{Concrete Numerical Example}
        Consider $f(x) = \tfrac{x^3}{3} - x$ (``x-cubed over three minus x'').

        \medskip
        Computing the derivative: $f'(x) = x^2 - 1$.
        Setting $f'(x) = 0$: $x^2 = 1$, so $x = \pm 1$.

        \medskip
        At $x = 1$: $f''(1) = 2 > 0$ --- this is a local minimum,
        with value $f(1) = \tfrac{1}{3} - 1 = -\tfrac{2}{3} \approx -0.667$.

        \medskip
        At $x = -1$: $f''(-1) = -2 < 0$ --- this is a local maximum,
        with value $f(-1) = -\tfrac{1}{3} + 1 = \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$.

        \medskip
        However, as $x \to -\infty$, $f(x) \to -\infty$, so the
        function is unbounded below and the local minimum at $x=1$
        is \textbf{not} a global minimum.  The function has no global
        minimum on $\R$.
      \end{exampleblock}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Classification of Stationary Points}
    A \textbf{stationary point} is any point where the gradient is zero (or, in
    one dimension, where $f'(x) = 0$).  Not all stationary points are minima:
    \centering
    \vspace{0.4em}
    \begin{tabular}{llll}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Condition on $f$} & \textbf{Uniqueness} & \textbf{Remark} \\
      \midrule
      Strong local min & $f(\vx^*) < f(\vx)$ for nearby $\vx \neq \vx^*$ & Locally unique & Most algorithms find these \\
      Weak local min   & $f(\vx^*) \leq f(\vx)$ for nearby $\vx$         & Plateau possible & Flat regions complicate search \\
      Global min       & $f(\vx^*) \leq f(\vx)$ for all $\vx \in \mathcal{X}$ & May be non-unique & Hard to certify in general \\
      Saddle point     & Gradient zero but not a min or max              & N/A & Gradient descent can stall here \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{block}

  \vspace{0.3em}

  \begin{alertblock}{Practical Implication: Most Algorithms Find Local Minima Only}
    The vast majority of practical optimization algorithms --- gradient descent,
    Newton's method, quasi-Newton, conjugate gradients --- are designed to find
    \emph{local} minima.  If the objective is non-convex, they may converge to a
    local minimum that is far from the global optimum.

    \medskip
    Common strategies to search for a global minimum:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Multi-start}: run the algorithm from many random initial
        points and keep the best solution found.  Simple but requires many evaluations.
      \item \textbf{Simulated annealing}: occasionally accept a worse solution
        with a probability that decreases over time, allowing the search to
        ``climb out'' of local basins.  The name comes from metallurgy (cooling
        metal slowly lets atoms find low-energy crystalline arrangements).
      \item \textbf{Population methods} (genetic algorithms, particle swarm):
        maintain a diverse set of solutions to explore multiple regions simultaneously.
      \item \textbf{Convex relaxation}: reformulate the problem to remove
        non-convexities, obtaining a global lower bound and often a good solution.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{exampleblock}{Exercise 1.1 (Solved)}
        \textbf{Question}: Give a function that has a local minimum that is
        not a global minimum.

        \medskip
        \textbf{Answer}: $f(x) = x^3/3 - x$.
        The local minimum at $x=1$ has value $f(1) = -2/3$, but
        $f(x) \to -\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$, so no global minimum exists.
      \end{exampleblock}

    \column{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{exampleblock}{Exercise 1.2 (Solved)}
        \textbf{Question}: What is the minimum of $f(x) = x^3 - x$?

        \medskip
        \textbf{Answer}: No minimum exists.  As $x \to -\infty$,
        $x^3$ dominates and $f(x) \to -\infty$, so the function is unbounded
        below and therefore has no global minimiser anywhere on $\R$.
      \end{exampleblock}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\section{Optimality Conditions}
%% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Optimality Conditions --- One Variable}

A key question in optimization is: how do we \emph{recognise} a minimum without
exhaustively testing every possible point?  The answer lies in
\emph{optimality conditions} --- mathematical properties that every minimum must
satisfy.  If a point fails these conditions, it cannot be a minimum.
We begin with the one-dimensional case for clarity, then generalise to
multiple variables in the next section.

  \begin{block}{Setting: Unconstrained Univariate Minimisation}
    We consider the problem of minimising a smooth (infinitely differentiable)
    function $f : \R \to \R$ over the entire real line, with no constraints.
    Our goal is to find conditions on a candidate point $x^*$ that are
    \emph{necessary} for $x^*$ to be a local minimum.
  \end{block}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.50\textwidth}

      \begin{alertblock}{First-Order Necessary Condition (FONC)}
        If $x^*$ is a local minimum of $f$, then
        \[
          f'(x^*) = 0.
        \]
        Here $f'(x^*)$ (f prime at x-star) is the first derivative of $f$
        evaluated at the point $x^*$.  A point where the derivative is zero
        is called a \textbf{stationary point} (the function is momentarily
        ``flat'' there).

        \medskip
        \textbf{Why must this hold?}  If $f'(x^*) > 0$, the function is
        increasing at $x^*$, so moving slightly to the left (decreasing $x$)
        would decrease $f$ --- contradicting the assumption that $x^*$ is a
        minimum.  Similarly, if $f'(x^*) < 0$, moving to the right would
        decrease $f$.  Only when $f'(x^*) = 0$ is there no direction to
        immediately improve.

        \medskip
        \textbf{Caution}: the FONC is \emph{necessary but not sufficient}.
        A stationary point could be a local maximum, a saddle (inflection) point,
        or a local minimum.  We need additional conditions to distinguish them.
      \end{alertblock}

      \vspace{0.4em}

      \begin{alertblock}{Second-Order Necessary Condition (SONC)}
        If $x^*$ is a local minimum of $f$, then
        \[
          f''(x^*) \geq 0.
        \]
        Here $f''(x^*)$ (f double-prime at x-star) is the second derivative,
        which measures the \emph{curvature} of the function at $x^*$.
        A non-negative second derivative means the function is concave-up
        (bowl-shaped, opening upward) at $x^*$, consistent with being at
        the bottom of a valley.  If $f''(x^*) < 0$, the function is
        concave-down (hill-shaped) --- a maximum, not a minimum.
      \end{alertblock}

    \column{0.48\textwidth}

      \begin{block}{Sufficient Condition for a Strong Local Minimum}
        If both of the following hold simultaneously:
        \[
          f'(x^*) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad f''(x^*) > 0,
        \]
        then $x^*$ is a \textbf{strong local minimum}.  The strict inequality
        $f''(x^*) > 0$ ensures that the function curves strictly upward,
        guaranteeing that all nearby points have strictly larger $f$ values.
        This is a sufficient condition --- if both hold, we can confidently
        declare $x^*$ a strong local minimum without any further analysis.
      \end{block}

      \vspace{0.4em}

      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_optimality_1d}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Derivation of the FONC via Taylor Expansion}
    To understand \emph{why} the FONC must hold, we use the Taylor expansion
    of $f$ around $x^*$.  For a small step size $h > 0$ (h, a small positive
    real number representing how far we move from $x^*$), we have:
    \begin{align*}
      f(x^*+h) &= f(x^*) + h\,f'(x^*) + O(h^2), \\
      f(x^*-h) &= f(x^*) - h\,f'(x^*) + O(h^2).
    \end{align*}
    Here $O(h^2)$ (``big-O of h-squared'', read ``order h-squared'') denotes
    terms that shrink at least as fast as $h^2$ as $h \to 0$ (i.e., they become
    negligible for small $h$).

    \medskip
    If $x^*$ is a local minimum, then $f(x^*+h) \geq f(x^*)$ and
    $f(x^*-h) \geq f(x^*)$ for all sufficiently small $h > 0$.  Substituting:
    \begin{align*}
      f(x^*+h) \geq f(x^*) &\implies h\,f'(x^*) + O(h^2) \geq 0. \\
      f(x^*-h) \geq f(x^*) &\implies -h\,f'(x^*) + O(h^2) \geq 0.
    \end{align*}
    Dividing both inequalities by $h > 0$ and taking $h \to 0$ (so that
    the $O(h)$ remainder vanishes):
    \[
      f'(x^*) \geq 0 \quad \text{and} \quad -f'(x^*) \geq 0.
    \]
    The only value satisfying both simultaneously is $f'(x^*) = 0$.
    This completes the derivation.
  \end{block}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Derivation of the SONC via Taylor Expansion}
        Using the FONC ($f'(x^*) = 0$ is already established), the second-order
        Taylor expansion gives:
        \[
          f(x^*+h) = f(x^*) + \underbrace{h\,f'(x^*)}_{=\;0}
                      + \frac{h^2}{2}\,f''(x^*) + O(h^3).
        \]
        If $x^*$ is a local minimum, then $f(x^*+h) \geq f(x^*)$ for all
        small $h$, which means:
        \[
          \frac{h^2}{2}\,f''(x^*) + O(h^3) \geq 0.
        \]
        Dividing by $h^2/2 > 0$ (which is always positive) and taking $h\to 0$:
        \[
          f''(x^*) \geq 0.
        \]
        This is the SONC for a local minimum.
      \end{block}

    \column{0.48\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_taylor}
      \begin{block}{}
        \scriptsize The Taylor polynomial approximates $f$ near $x^*$.  The
        zeroth-order approximation is just the constant $f(x^*)$.  Adding the
        first-order term $h f'(x^*)$ gives the tangent line.  Adding the second-
        order term $\tfrac{h^2}{2}f''(x^*)$ gives the best quadratic approximation.
        The figure shows how each successive order better matches the true function
        curve near $x^*$.
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: Applying the Conditions to $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^2$}
    Let us verify the optimality conditions at all stationary points of
    $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^2$ (x to the fourth power minus four x-squared).
    This function is a ``double-well'' potential with two symmetric minima.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Step 1 --- Find stationary points by applying the FONC:}
    \[
      f'(x) = 4x^3 - 8x = 4x(x^2 - 2) = 0.
    \]
    Factoring: $4x = 0$ gives $x = 0$, and $x^2 - 2 = 0$ gives
    $x = \pm\sqrt{2} \approx \pm 1.414$.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Step 2 --- Apply the SONC at each stationary point using $f''(x) = 12x^2 - 8$:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item At $x = 0$: $f''(0) = 0 - 8 = -8 < 0$ --- concave-down, so this
        is a \textbf{local maximum} with $f(0) = 0$.
      \item At $x = \sqrt{2}$: $f''(\sqrt{2}) = 12 \times 2 - 8 = 16 > 0$ ---
        concave-up, so this is a \textbf{strong local minimum},
        $f(\sqrt{2}) = 4 - 8 = -4$.
      \item At $x = -\sqrt{2}$: $f''(-\sqrt{2}) = 16 > 0$ (same by symmetry) ---
        another \textbf{strong local minimum}, $f(-\sqrt{2}) = -4$.
    \end{itemize}

    \medskip
    Since $f(x) \to +\infty$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, both points
    $x = \pm\sqrt{2}$ are also \textbf{global minima} with equal minimum value
    $f^* = -4$.  This function has two equally good global minima, illustrating
    that global optima need not be unique.
  \end{exampleblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Optimality Conditions --- Multiple Variables}

The one-dimensional conditions extend naturally to functions of $n$ variables,
but the derivative is replaced by the \emph{gradient} (a vector of partial
derivatives) and the second derivative is replaced by the \emph{Hessian matrix}
(a square matrix of second-order partial derivatives).  Both objects are central
to every topic in the remainder of this book.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}

      \begin{alertblock}{First-Order Necessary Condition (Multivariate FONC)}
        If $\vx^*$ is a local minimum of $f : \R^n \to \R$, then
        \[
          \grad f(\vx^*) = \mathbf{0}.
        \]
        Here $\grad$ (nabla, written $\nabla$, pronounced ``NAB-lah''; also
        called ``del'' or ``grad'') denotes the \textbf{gradient operator}.
        The gradient is the vector of all partial derivatives:
        \[
          \grad f(\vx) = \left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\;
          \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2},\; \ldots,\;
          \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right]^\top.
        \]
        The notation $\partial f / \partial x_i$ (partial derivative of $f$
        with respect to $x_i$, read ``d-f d-x-i'') measures how $f$ changes
        when only $x_i$ changes and all other variables are held fixed.

        \medskip
        The condition $\grad f(\vx^*) = \mathbf{0}$ (gradient equals the
        zero vector $\mathbf{0}$, bold zero) means that \emph{all} partial
        derivatives are zero simultaneously --- the function has no direction
        in which it can be immediately decreased.
      \end{alertblock}

    \column{0.46\textwidth}

      \begin{alertblock}{Second-Order Necessary Condition (Multivariate SONC)}
        If $\vx^*$ is a local minimum, then the \textbf{Hessian matrix}
        $\hess f(\vx^*)$ (nabla-squared f, pronounced ``hessian'') must be
        \emph{positive semidefinite}:
        \[
          \hess f(\vx^*) \succeq 0.
        \]
        The symbol $\succeq 0$ (``succeeds or equals zero'', read ``is positive
        semidefinite'') means every eigenvalue of the matrix is $\geq 0$.
        The Hessian is the $n \times n$ matrix of second-order partial
        derivatives:
        \[
          [\hess f(\vx)]_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \,\partial x_j}.
        \]
        Intuitively, the Hessian plays the role of curvature in multiple
        dimensions: it tells us how the function bends in every direction from
        $\vx^*$.  The $(i,j)$ entry measures the rate at which the
        $i$-th partial derivative changes as $x_j$ varies.
      \end{alertblock}

  \end{columns}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Multivariate FONC and SONC}
    Just as in one dimension, these conditions are \emph{necessary but not
    sufficient}.  Finding a point where $\grad f = \mathbf{0}$ and
    $\hess f \succeq 0$ tells us the point \emph{could} be a local minimum ---
    but it could also be a saddle point (where some eigenvalues of the Hessian
    are zero).  The sufficient condition for a \emph{strong} local minimum is
    $\grad f = \mathbf{0}$ \emph{and} $\hess f \succ 0$ (all eigenvalues
    strictly positive, read ``positive definite'').
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Three Cases at a Stationary Point ($\grad f = \mathbf{0}$)}
    Once we find a stationary point $\vx^*$ (satisfying the FONC), the Hessian
    matrix tells us what kind of stationary point it is.  The key is the
    \emph{signs of the eigenvalues} $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n$
    (lambda sub 1 through lambda sub n) of the Hessian:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Local minimum (bowl shape)}: $\hess f(\vx^*) \succ 0$
        (positive definite --- all eigenvalues $\lambda_i > 0$).  The function
        curves upward in every direction, so moving away from $\vx^*$ in any
        direction increases $f$.
      \item \textbf{Local maximum (hill shape)}: $\hess f(\vx^*) \prec 0$
        (negative definite --- all eigenvalues $\lambda_i < 0$).  The function
        curves downward in every direction.
      \item \textbf{Saddle point}: $\hess f(\vx^*)$ is indefinite (some
        eigenvalues positive, some negative).  The function curves upward in
        some directions and downward in others --- like the surface of a horse
        saddle or a mountain pass.  This is \emph{not} a local minimum.
        Gradient descent can stall near saddle points in high dimensions,
        which is a significant challenge in deep learning.
    \end{enumerate}
    \vspace{0.3em}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.52\linewidth]{fig_multivariate_regions}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: The Rosenbrock Function}
    The \textbf{Rosenbrock function} (named after Howard Rosenbrock, 1960)
    is a famous benchmark for testing optimization algorithms because its
    minimum sits at the bottom of a long, narrow, curved ``banana-shaped''
    valley that is very hard to navigate:
    \[
      f(\vx) = (1 - x_1)^2 + 5(x_2 - x_1^2)^2.
    \]
    Here $x_1$ and $x_2$ (x sub one and x sub two) are the two variables.
    The first term $(1-x_1)^2$ penalises deviation of $x_1$ from $1$.
    The second term $5(x_2 - x_1^2)^2$ penalises deviation of $x_2$ from
    the parabola $x_2 = x_1^2$.  The coefficient $5$ controls the relative
    weighting; larger values create a narrower valley and make the problem harder.
    We verify that $\vx^* = (1, 1)$ satisfies both FONC and SONC.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Step 1 --- Compute the gradient $\grad f$:}
    \[
      \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} = 2(10x_1^3 - 10x_1 x_2 + x_1 - 1), \qquad
      \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} = 10(x_2 - x_1^2).
    \]
    At $(1, 1)$: the first partial $= 2(10 - 10 + 1 - 1) = 0$,
    and the second partial $= 10(1 - 1) = 0$.
    So $\grad f(1,1) = [0, 0]^\top = \mathbf{0}$. FONC satisfied. \checkmark

    \medskip
    \textbf{Step 2 --- Compute the Hessian $\hess f$ and evaluate at $(1,1)$:}
    \[
      \hess f(1,1) = \begin{bmatrix} 42 & -20 \\ -20 & 10 \end{bmatrix}.
    \]

    \medskip
    \textbf{Step 3 --- Check positive definiteness of the Hessian:}
    For a $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrix, it is positive definite if and only
    if both the leading entry and the determinant are positive:
    \[
      \text{leading entry} = 42 > 0, \qquad
      \det(\hess f) = 42 \times 10 - (-20)^2 = 420 - 400 = 20 > 0.
    \]
    Both conditions are met, so $\hess f(1,1)$ is positive definite.
    Therefore $(1,1)$ is a \textbf{strong local minimum}.
    Since $f(1,1) = 0$ is the smallest possible value of $f$ (which is always
    $\geq 0$ as a sum of squares), $(1,1)$ is also the unique \textbf{global minimum}.
  \end{exampleblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.53\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_rosenbrock}
    \column{0.45\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Reading the Rosenbrock Contour Plot}
        A \textbf{contour plot} shows lines along which the function value is
        constant --- like elevation contours on a topographic map.  Each contour
        line connects points where $f(\vx) = c$ for some fixed constant $c$.

        \medskip
        In the Rosenbrock contour plot, the contours form an elongated,
        banana-shaped valley.  The minimum $(1,1)$ sits at the centre of the
        tightest (innermost) contours.  The valley is extremely narrow in one
        direction and almost flat along its length --- this is why simple gradient
        descent struggles: a step large enough to make progress along the valley
        is too large to stay within it, causing the algorithm to ``zigzag''
        back and forth inefficiently.  This property makes the Rosenbrock
        function a stern test of an algorithm's ability to exploit curvature
        information.
      \end{block}
      \begin{exampleblock}{Saddle Point Example}
        For $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 - x_2^2$ (x-one-squared minus x-two-squared):
        \begin{itemize}
          \item $\grad f = [2x_1,\; -2x_2]^\top = \mathbf{0}$ at the origin.
          \item $\hess f = \text{diag}(2, -2)$ (a diagonal matrix with entries
            $+2$ and $-2$) --- eigenvalues $+2$ and $-2$, so it is indefinite.
          \item Conclusion: the origin is a \textbf{saddle point} --- a minimum
            along the $x_1$-axis but a maximum along the $x_2$-axis.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{exampleblock}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Optimality Conditions --- Python Verification}

The following Python code computes the gradient and Hessian of the Rosenbrock
function numerically and verifies that $(1, 1)$ satisfies both the FONC and the
SONC.  This pattern --- compute gradient, compute Hessian, check eigenvalues ---
is a standard verification workflow that you can apply to any smooth function.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
      \begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode,caption={Checking optimality conditions for the Rosenbrock function}]
import numpy as np

def rosenbrock(x):
    """f(x) = (1-x1)^2 + 5*(x2-x1^2)^2"""
    return (1 - x[0])**2 + 5*(x[1] - x[0]**2)**2

def grad_rosenbrock(x):
    """Gradient vector [df/dx1, df/dx2]"""
    x1, x2 = x
    df_dx1 = 2*(10*x1**3 - 10*x1*x2 + x1 - 1)
    df_dx2 = 10*(x2 - x1**2)
    return np.array([df_dx1, df_dx2])

def hess_rosenbrock(x):
    """2x2 Hessian matrix of second derivatives"""
    x1, x2 = x
    h11 = -20*(x2 - x1**2) + 40*x1**2 + 2
    h12 = -20*x1
    h22 = 10
    return np.array([[h11, h12], [h12, h22]])

xstar = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
g = grad_rosenbrock(xstar)
H = hess_rosenbrock(xstar)
# eigvalsh assumes H is symmetric (it is, by Clairaut's theorem)
eigs = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)

print("Gradient:", g)         # should be [0. 0.]
print("Hessian:\n", H)        # [[42 -20][-20 10]]
print("Eigenvalues:", eigs)   # should be > 0 (positive definite)
print("FONC satisfied:", np.allclose(g, 0))
print("SONC satisfied:", np.all(eigs >= 0))
      \end{lstlisting}

    \column{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Expected Output}
        \texttt{Gradient: [0. 0.]}\\[2pt]
        \texttt{Hessian:}\\
        \texttt{\ [[42. -20.]}\\
        \texttt{\ \ [-20.  10.]]}\\[2pt]
        \texttt{Eigenvalues: [2.0, 50.0]}\\
        \texttt{(both positive)}\\[4pt]
        \texttt{FONC satisfied: True}\\
        \texttt{SONC satisfied: True}
      \end{block}
      \begin{alertblock}{Conclusion}
        Both conditions are satisfied at $\vx^* = (1, 1)$, confirming
        it is a strong local minimum.  Since it is also the unique global
        minimum of the Rosenbrock function, $f(\vx^*) = 0$.

        \medskip
        Note: \texttt{np.linalg.eigvalsh} is used rather than \texttt{eigvals}
        because the Hessian $H$ is always symmetric for smooth functions
        (by Clairaut's theorem: mixed partial derivatives are equal, so
        $\partial^2 f/\partial x_i \partial x_j = \partial^2 f/\partial x_j \partial x_i$).
        The function \texttt{eigvalsh} exploits this symmetry for better
        numerical stability.
      \end{alertblock}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

      \begin{block}{The Progression from Simple to Complex}
        Chapters 5--9 all assume the objective is smooth and unconstrained
        (or only box-constrained).  They differ in \emph{what information
        about $f$ they use}, with higher-order information enabling faster
        convergence but at higher computational cost per iteration.

        \medskip
        \textbf{Chapter 5} uses only the gradient $\grad f$ (nabla f,
        first-order information).  The gradient tells us which way is
        ``downhill'' but gives no information about curvature --- how steeply
        downhill.  Convergence can be slow near the minimum where the gradient
        is small.

        \medskip
        \textbf{Chapter 6} adds the Hessian $\hess f$ (nabla-squared f,
        second-order information), allowing Newton's method to take steps
        that account for curvature and converge much faster.  The cost is
        computing and factorising the $n \times n$ Hessian matrix, which
        requires $O(n^3)$ (order n-cubed) work and is prohibitive for very
        large $n$.

        \medskip
        \textbf{Chapter 7} uses conjugate directions, which accumulate
        information from previous gradient evaluations to avoid revisiting
        the same search directions.  Especially effective on quadratic objectives
        (convergence in exactly $n$ steps).

        \medskip
        \textbf{Chapters 8--9} handle the stochastic setting where $\grad f$
        is not available exactly but can be estimated from random samples ---
        the regime of modern deep learning with mini-batch training.
      \end{block}

      \end{block}
  \end{columns}

  \begin{block}{When Gradients Are Unavailable: Surrogate and Bayesian Methods}
    Many real engineering problems do not provide gradients.  Either the
    objective is computed by a legacy simulator that outputs only a single
    scalar number, or the function is stochastic and the gradients are too
    noisy to be useful.  In these cases, we must rely on \emph{function values alone}.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Surrogate models} (Chapter 14): we observe the function at a set
    of evaluation points, then fit a computationally cheap model (such as a
    polynomial, a radial basis function network, or a Gaussian process
    --- a type of probabilistic model that quantifies our uncertainty about
    the function everywhere) to the observed values.  We then optimise the
    cheap surrogate model rather than the expensive true function.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Bayesian optimisation} (Chapter 17): we maintain a probabilistic
    belief (a posterior distribution, using Bayes' theorem to update our belief
    as we collect data) over the unknown function $f$.  At each step, we choose
    the next evaluation point by maximising an \emph{acquisition function}
    $\alpha(\vx)$ (alpha of x) that balances:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Exploitation}: evaluating near the current best estimate
        of the minimum (low predicted value).
      \item \textbf{Exploration}: reducing uncertainty in regions we have not
        yet sampled (high posterior variance).
    \end{itemize}
    Bayesian optimisation is especially valuable when $n$ (the number of
    variables) is small but evaluations are extremely expensive.
  \end{block}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Summary of Chapter 1}

Chapter 1 introduced the fundamental concepts that underpin every topic in
this book.  Before proceeding to the algorithms, make sure you can state and
apply each of the following ideas.  If any concept is still unclear, return
to the relevant section and work through the examples again.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Core Concepts}
        \begin{enumerate}
          \item \textbf{What is optimization?}  The process of choosing a
            design vector $\vx$ (bold x) from a feasible set $\mathcal{X}$
            (script X) to minimise (or maximise) an objective function $f(\vx)$.

          \item \textbf{Standard form}: every problem can be written as
            \[
              \min_{\vx \in \mathcal{X}} f(\vx)
              \quad \text{subject to constraints.}
            \]

          \item \textbf{Local minimum}: a point $\vx^*$ where $f(\vx^*) \leq f(\vx)$
            for all nearby $\vx$.  A \textbf{global minimum} is one where this
            holds for \emph{all} feasible $\vx$.  Most algorithms find local
            minima; finding global minima is generally much harder.

          \item \textbf{First-Order Necessary Condition (FONC)}:
            $\grad f(\vx^*) = \mathbf{0}$ (nabla f at x-star equals zero
            vector).  Zero gradient is necessary but not sufficient for a
            minimum --- a zero gradient could also indicate a maximum or saddle.

          \item \textbf{Second-Order Necessary Condition (SONC)}:
            $\hess f(\vx^*) \succeq 0$ (the Hessian is positive semidefinite ---
            all eigenvalues non-negative).  This rules out local maxima.

          \item \textbf{Sufficient condition} for a strong local minimum:
            $\grad f = \mathbf{0}$ \emph{and} $\hess f \succ 0$ (positive
            definite --- all eigenvalues strictly positive).

          \item \textbf{No Free Lunch}: no single algorithm is best for all
            problems.  Algorithm selection depends on knowing your problem's
            structure (dimensionality, gradient availability, budget, noise).
        \end{enumerate}
      \end{block}

    \column{0.46\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{Core Formulas to Memorise}
        \[
          \text{FONC (1-D):}\quad f'(x^*)=0
        \]
        \[
          \text{FONC (n-D):}\quad \grad f(\vx^*)=\mathbf{0}
        \]
        \[
          \text{SONC (1-D):}\quad f''(x^*)\geq 0
        \]
        \[
          \text{SONC (n-D):}\quad \hess f(\vx^*)\succeq 0
        \]
        \[
          \text{Sufficient:}\quad f'(x^*)=0,\; f''(x^*)>0
        \]
        In each formula, $f'$ (f prime) denotes the first derivative in 1-D,
        $f''$ (f double-prime) the second derivative, $\grad$ (nabla) the
        gradient vector in $n$-D, and $\hess$ (nabla-squared) the Hessian
        matrix.  The symbols $\succeq 0$ and $\succ 0$ mean positive
        semidefinite and positive definite respectively.
      \end{alertblock}

      \vspace{0.4em}

      \begin{block}{Applications Span Many Fields}
        The same mathematical framework applies to:
        aerospace engineering (minimise aerodynamic drag),
        machine learning (minimise prediction loss over neural network weights),
        quantitative finance (minimise portfolio variance subject to return
        and budget constraints),
        medicine (minimise radiation dose to healthy tissue),
        and manufacturing scheduling (minimise makespan).
        The unifying language is: choose $\vx$ to minimise $f(\vx)$.
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Exercises (with Full Solutions)}

The following exercises are drawn directly from the textbook.  Work through
each one yourself before reading the solution --- the process of struggling
with a problem and discovering the answer is far more educational than
simply reading the answer.  Each exercise illustrates a subtle but important
point about the nature of optima.

  \begin{block}{Exercise 1.1}
    \textbf{Question}: Give an example of a function that has a local minimum
    that is not a global minimum.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Solution}: Consider $f(x) = \tfrac{x^3}{3} - x$
    (one-third x-cubed minus x).
    \begin{itemize}
      \item The derivative is $f'(x) = x^2 - 1$.  Setting $f'(x) = 0$ gives
        $x^2 = 1$, so stationary points are at $x = +1$ and $x = -1$.
      \item Second derivative: $f''(x) = 2x$.  At $x = 1$: $f''(1) = 2 > 0$,
        so $x = 1$ is a local minimum with $f(1) = \tfrac{1}{3} - 1 = -\tfrac{2}{3}$.
      \item As $x \to -\infty$, $f(x) = \tfrac{x^3}{3} - x \to -\infty$,
        so the function is unbounded below.  The local minimum at $x = 1$
        cannot be a global minimum because there are points with much smaller values.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Exercise 1.2}
    \textbf{Question}: What is the minimum of $f(x) = x^3 - x$?

    \medskip
    \textbf{Solution}: No minimum exists.  As $x \to -\infty$,
    the cubic term $x^3$ dominates and $f(x) \to -\infty$, so $f$ has no
    lower bound and therefore no global minimiser on the real line $\R$.
    Note that this function has a local minimum at $x = 1$ (where $f'(1) = 0$
    and $f''(1) = 6 > 0$), but that local minimum is not global.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Exercise 1.3}
    \textbf{Question}: Give a function that is bounded below but has no
    global optimum on $\mathcal{X} = [0, 1]$ (the closed interval from 0 to 1).

    \medskip
    \textbf{Solution}: Define
    \[
      f(x) = \begin{cases} x & \text{if } x \neq 0 \\ 1 & \text{if } x = 0 \end{cases}.
    \]
    This function satisfies $f(x) > 0$ for all $x \in [0,1]$ (bounded below by $0$).
    The infimum (greatest lower bound) is $\inf_{x \in [0,1]} f(x) = 0$, but this
    infimum is never attained: at $x = 0$ the function jumps to $1$, and for
    any $x > 0$ we have $f(x) = x > 0$.  So there is no point in $[0,1]$
    where $f$ achieves its infimum of $0$.  This illustrates that the infimum
    and minimum are different concepts: the infimum always exists for a
    bounded-below function, but a minimum (the infimum actually achieved by
    some feasible point) may not.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Exercise 1.4}
    \textbf{Question}: What are the minima and minimizers of $f(x) = \sin(x)$?

    \medskip
    \textbf{Solution}: The function $\sin(x)$ is periodic with period $2\pi$
    (``2 pi'', approximately 6.28 radians, one full revolution).
    Its global minimum value is $-1$, since $\sin(x) \geq -1$ for all $x$
    and $\sin(x) = -1$ whenever $x = \tfrac{3}{2}\pi + 2\pi k$ for any
    integer $k$ (i.e., at $x \approx 4.71,\; 10.99,\; 17.28,\; \ldots$ and
    $x \approx -1.57,\; -7.85,\; \ldots$).
    There are infinitely many global minimizers, all equally optimal.
    This illustrates that a function can have multiple (even infinitely many)
    points at which the global minimum is attained.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Exercise 1.5}
    \textbf{Question}: Does the FONC $f'(x) = 0$ necessarily hold at the
    optimal solution of a \emph{constrained} problem?

    \medskip
    \textbf{Solution}: \textbf{No.}  Consider the simple problem
    $\min_{x} f(x) = x$ subject to $x \geq 1$.
    The optimal solution is $x^* = 1$ (the smallest feasible value), but
    $f'(1) = 1 \neq 0$.

    \medskip
    The reason is that at a constrained boundary, the optimizer is
    ``pushing against'' the constraint: moving away from $x^* = 1$ in the
    feasible direction ($x > 1$) would increase $f$, even though the
    gradient $f'(1) = 1 > 0$ is non-zero.  The correct first-order conditions
    for constrained problems are the \textbf{KKT (Karush--Kuhn--Tucker)
    conditions} (Chapter 10), which generalise the FONC by accounting for
    the forces exerted by active constraints.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Exercise 1.6}
    \textbf{Question}: How many minima does $f(x, y) = x^2 + y$ have,
    subject to the constraints $x > y \geq 1$?

    \medskip
    \textbf{Solution}: \textbf{None.}  The constraint $x > y$ (strict
    inequality) excludes the boundary $x = y$ from the feasible set.
    As we let $x \to y^+$ (x approaches y from above) and $y \to 1^+$
    (y approaches 1 from above), $f(x, y) \to 1^2 + 1 = 2$, but we can
    never actually attain $f = 2$ because the boundary is excluded.
    Therefore the infimum of $f$ over the feasible set is $2$, but no
    minimiser exists.  The problem's solution requires a closed feasible
    set (containing its boundary); open strict inequalities can prevent a
    minimum from existing even when the infimum is finite.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Exercise 1.7}
    \textbf{Question}: How many inflection points does $f(x) = x^3 - 10$ have?

    \medskip
    \textbf{Solution}: An \textbf{inflection point} is a point where the
    second derivative changes sign (the function switches from concave-up to
    concave-down, or vice versa).  Computing:
    \[
      f'(x) = 3x^2, \qquad f''(x) = 6x.
    \]
    Setting $f''(x) = 6x = 0$ gives $x = 0$.
    For $x < 0$, $f''(x) = 6x < 0$ (concave-down, curving like an arch).
    For $x > 0$, $f''(x) = 6x > 0$ (concave-up, curving like a bowl).
    Since the sign of $f''$ changes at $x = 0$, there is exactly
    \textbf{one inflection point}, located at $x = 0$.

    \medskip
    The $-10$ is merely a vertical shift of the entire graph and does not
    affect the shape, the curvature, or the location of the inflection point.
    The inflection point is at $(0,\; f(0)) = (0, -10)$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{exampleblock}{Study Tips for Chapter 1}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Always check \emph{both} the FONC \emph{and} the SONC when
        identifying a minimum.  The FONC alone is not sufficient --- a
        stationary point could be a maximum or saddle.  For the sufficient
        condition, also verify that the Hessian is strictly positive definite.

      \item For constrained problems, the unconstrained FONC ($\grad f = \mathbf{0}$)
        does not apply at boundary solutions.  The correct framework is the
        KKT conditions, introduced in Chapter 10.  This distinction is crucial
        for applied work --- never apply the unconstrained FONC to a constrained
        problem without checking feasibility.

      \item In Python, \texttt{scipy.optimize.minimize} is your go-to solver.
        Use \texttt{method='L-BFGS-B'} (L-BFGS-B) for unconstrained or
        box-constrained problems, and \texttt{method='SLSQP'} when you have
        general inequality or equality constraints.

      \item Draw the function \emph{before} running an algorithm.  For one-
        and two-dimensional problems, plotting the objective and feasible set
        often reveals whether local minima exist, where the global minimum
        is likely to be, and which algorithm family is most appropriate.
        Visualisation is not optional --- it is a diagnostic tool.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{exampleblock}
