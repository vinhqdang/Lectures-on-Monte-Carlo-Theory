\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Motivation: Why Second-Order Information?}

  When we minimise a function $f(\xbf)$, we need to decide \emph{two} things at
  every iteration: in which \emph{direction} to move, and by \emph{how far} to
  move.  First-order methods answer only the first question by consulting the
  gradient $\nabla f(\xbf)$ (nabla $f$, i.e.\ the vector of partial derivatives).
  They tell us the direction of steepest ascent, so we step in the opposite
  direction.  However, the gradient gives no information about the curvature of
  $f$ -- it cannot say whether the function is a gentle slope or a sharp valley.
  As a result, first-order methods often take many small, cautious steps in highly
  curved regions.

  \begin{block}{First-Order Methods: Direction Without Curvature}
    A first-order method uses $\nabla f(\xbf)$ (nabla $f$, the gradient vector) to
    determine the search direction.  The update takes the form
    \[
      \xbf^{(k+1)} = \xbf^{(k)} - \alpha^{(k)} \nabla f\!\left(\xbf^{(k)}\right),
    \]
    where $\xbf^{(k)}$ (x superscript $k$) is the current point at iteration $k$,
    $\alpha^{(k)}$ (alpha superscript $k$) is the step size chosen at iteration $k$,
    and $\nabla f(\xbf^{(k)})$ is the gradient evaluated there.  The gradient tells
    us the direction of steepest ascent, so taking a step opposite to it decreases
    $f$.  However, the gradient alone cannot tell us the \emph{optimal} step size:
    a flat function and a sharply curved function have the same gradient at an
    intermediate point, yet require very different step sizes.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Second-Order Methods: Building a Local Quadratic Model}
    Second-order methods augment the gradient with the \textbf{Hessian}
    $\mathbf{H}(\xbf) = \nabla^2 f(\xbf)$ (nabla-squared $f$), which is the matrix
    of second partial derivatives.  The entry $H_{ij}$ (H sub $ij$) is
    $\partial^2 f / \partial x_i \partial x_j$, encoding how the $i$-th component
    of the gradient changes as we move along the $j$-th coordinate.  With both
    gradient and Hessian in hand we can fit a \emph{quadratic} (bowl-shaped or
    saddle-shaped) local model to $f$, and a bowl has a unique lowest point that
    we can find analytically -- no trial-and-error step size needed.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight}
    A quadratic function has exactly one stationary point (where derivative is
    zero), and we can jump there in a single step.  If $f$ is well approximated
    by a quadratic near the current iterate, second-order methods converge much
    faster than gradient-only methods.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_first_vs_second_order.pdf}
      \\[4pt]
      {\scriptsize Top: a linear (first-order) approximation misses the minimum --
       the tangent line at the current point points away from where the function
       actually bottoms out.  Bottom: a quadratic (second-order) approximation
       curves down to enclose the minimum, allowing us to step directly there.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{What the Hessian $\Hbf$ Tells Us}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item If $\Hbf$ is \emph{positive definite} (all eigenvalues positive),
            the quadratic model is bowl-shaped and has a unique minimum.  The
            Newton step jumps directly to it.
          \item If $\Hbf$ is \emph{indefinite} (mixed-sign eigenvalues), the model
            is saddle-shaped and the Newton step may go uphill.  Damping (as in
            Levenberg-Marquardt) repairs this.
          \item The diagonal entries $H_{ii}$ measure curvature along each
            coordinate axis: a large $H_{ii}$ means the function is sharply curved
            along $x_i$, so a small step is warranted in that direction.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Quadratic Approximation via Taylor Expansion}

  The mathematical foundation of all second-order methods is the
  \textbf{Taylor expansion}: any smooth function can be approximated near a
  given point by a polynomial.  Stopping at the second-order term gives a
  quadratic approximation $q$ that is much more accurate than the linear
  (gradient-only) approximation, yet still has a simple analytical minimum.

  \begin{block}{Univariate: Second-Order Taylor Expansion}
    For a function $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ of a single variable, the
    second-order Taylor expansion around the current iterate $x^{(k)}$ (x at
    iteration $k$) is:
    \[
      q(x) \;=\; f\!\left(x^{(k)}\right)
              + \bigl(x - x^{(k)}\bigr)\,f'\!\left(x^{(k)}\right)
              + \frac{\bigl(x - x^{(k)}\bigr)^2}{2}\,f''\!\left(x^{(k)}\right)
    \]
    \textbf{Symbol guide:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $x^{(k)}$ is the expansion point -- the current best guess.
      \item $f'(x^{(k)})$ (f prime at $x^{(k)}$) is the first derivative -- the
        slope of $f$ at the expansion point.
      \item $f''(x^{(k)})$ (f double-prime at $x^{(k)}$) is the second derivative
        -- the curvature, i.e.\ how fast the slope itself is changing.
      \item $(x - x^{(k)})$ is the displacement from the expansion point to the
        query point $x$.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read This Formula}
    Think of $q(x)$ as a parabola centred at $x^{(k)}$.  The first-derivative
    term tilts the parabola left or right (the linear trend); the
    second-derivative term determines whether the parabola opens upward
    (positive curvature, $f'' > 0$) or downward (negative curvature, $f'' < 0$).
    If $f'' > 0$ the parabola has a unique minimum we can reach by setting
    $q'(x) = 0$, which gives the Newton step.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Multivariate: Second-Order Taylor Expansion}
    For $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ (a function of $n$ (n) variables), the
    expansion generalises to:
    \[
      q(\xbf) \;=\; f\!\left(\xbf^{(k)}\right)
               + \bigl(\xbf - \xbf^{(k)}\bigr)^\top \gbf^{(k)}
               + \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\xbf - \xbf^{(k)}\bigr)^\top
                 \Hbf^{(k)}\bigl(\xbf - \xbf^{(k)}\bigr)
    \]
    \textbf{Symbol guide:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\xbf \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (bold x) is the $n$-dimensional input vector;
        $\xbf^{(k)}$ is its value at iteration $k$.
      \item $\gbf^{(k)} = \nabla f(\xbf^{(k)}) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (bold g, the
        gradient vector) contains the $n$ partial derivatives of $f$ at
        $\xbf^{(k)}$.
      \item $\Hbf^{(k)} = \nabla^2 f(\xbf^{(k)}) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$
        (bold H, the Hessian matrix) is the $n \times n$ matrix of second partial
        derivatives; entry $(i,j)$ is $\partial^2 f / \partial x_i \partial x_j$.
      \item The superscript $\top$ denotes the transpose, turning a column vector
        into a row vector so the products are dimensionally consistent.
      \item The term $(\xbf - \xbf^{(k)})^\top \Hbf^{(k)} (\xbf - \xbf^{(k)})$
        is a \emph{quadratic form} -- a scalar that encodes the curvature of $f$
        in the direction of displacement $(\xbf - \xbf^{(k)})$.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  Setting the gradient of $q$ with respect to $\xbf$ to zero (i.e.\ $\nabla q(\xbf)
  = \mathbf{0}$) and solving for $\xbf$ gives the \textbf{Newton step} directly.
  This is the central computation shared by all second-order methods in this chapter.

\end{frame}

\section{Newton's Method}
% =====================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{6.1 Newton's Method: The Core Idea}

  Newton's method asks: \emph{if the function looks like a quadratic bowl right
  now, where is the bottom of that bowl?}  It then steps directly there.  Because
  real functions are not perfectly quadratic, we repeat: rebuild the quadratic
  approximation at the new point, find its bottom, step there, and so on.  Near a
  true minimum the approximation becomes increasingly accurate, which is why
  convergence accelerates dramatically as we approach the solution.

  \begin{block}{Univariate Newton Step}
    Setting $q'(x) = 0$ in the univariate Taylor expansion and solving for the
    minimiser $x^{(k+1)}$ yields the update rule:
    \[
      x^{(k+1)} \;\leftarrow\; x^{(k)} - \frac{f'\!\left(x^{(k)}\right)}{f''\!\left(x^{(k)}\right)}
    \]
    Reading this formula: we subtract from the current point $x^{(k)}$ the ratio
    of the slope $f'$ to the curvature $f''$.  If the slope is large and the
    curvature is also large (sharp bowl), the step is moderate.  If the curvature
    is small (shallow, nearly flat), the denominator is small and the step is
    large -- reflecting the fact that a shallow bowl's minimum is far away.
    The condition $f''(x^{(k)}) > 0$ (positive curvature) is required for this
    to be a \emph{descent} step; if $f''(x^{(k)}) < 0$ the parabola opens
    downward and the step goes uphill.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Multivariate Newton Step}
    In $n$ dimensions, setting $\nabla q(\xbf) = \mathbf{0}$ and solving for
    $\xbf^{(k+1)}$ gives:
    \[
      \xbf^{(k+1)} \;\leftarrow\; \xbf^{(k)} - \left(\Hbf^{(k)}\right)^{-1} \gbf^{(k)}
    \]
    Here $(\Hbf^{(k)})^{-1}$ (H inverse) is the inverse of the Hessian matrix,
    and $\gbf^{(k)}$ (bold g) is the gradient vector at $\xbf^{(k)}$.  The product
    $(\Hbf^{(k)})^{-1} \gbf^{(k)}$ is the \emph{Newton direction}: it is the
    gradient pre-multiplied by the inverse curvature, which rescales each gradient
    component by the appropriate curvature in that direction.

    In practice we never explicitly invert $\Hbf^{(k)}$ (matrix inversion is
    numerically unstable and slower than necessary).  Instead we solve the linear
    system
    \[
      \Hbf^{(k)}\,\mathbf{d} = -\gbf^{(k)}
    \]
    for the Newton direction $\mathbf{d}$ (bold d), then update:
    $\xbf^{(k+1)} = \xbf^{(k)} + \mathbf{d}$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{When Is the Newton Step a Descent Direction?}
    The Newton direction $\mathbf{d} = -(\Hbf^{(k)})^{-1}\gbf^{(k)}$ points
    downhill (i.e.\ it is a descent direction) if and only if $\Hbf^{(k)}$ is
    \emph{positive definite} -- meaning every eigenvalue (the principal
    curvatures) is strictly positive.  When this holds, $f$ is bowl-shaped locally
    and the Newton step guarantees a decrease.  When $\Hbf^{(k)}$ is indefinite or
    negative definite, the Newton step may increase $f$, and we need to augment the
    method (e.g.\ with a line search or damping).
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Algorithm: Newton's Method (Step by Step)}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Initialise.}  Choose a starting point $\xbf^{(1)}$ and a
        convergence tolerance $\epsilon$ (epsilon, a small positive number such as
        $10^{-8}$) representing the maximum acceptable gradient magnitude.
      \item \textbf{Compute gradient.}  Evaluate $\gbf^{(k)} = \nabla f(\xbf^{(k)})$.
      \item \textbf{Check convergence.}  If $\|\gbf^{(k)}\| \leq \epsilon$ (the
        Euclidean norm of the gradient is small enough), stop and return $\xbf^{(k)}$.
      \item \textbf{Compute Hessian.}  Evaluate $\Hbf^{(k)} = \nabla^2 f(\xbf^{(k)})$.
      \item \textbf{Solve for Newton direction.}  Find $\mathbf{d}$ by solving
        $\Hbf^{(k)}\mathbf{d} = -\gbf^{(k)}$ (a linear system, not a matrix
        inversion).
      \item \textbf{Update.}  Set $\xbf^{(k+1)} = \xbf^{(k)} + \mathbf{d}$ and go
        to step 2.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Convergence Properties}
    Newton's method exhibits \textbf{quadratic convergence} in a neighbourhood of
    a local minimum: if the error at iteration $k$ is $e_k = \|\xbf^{(k)} -
    \xbf^*\|$, then $e_{k+1} \leq C \cdot e_k^2$ for some constant $C > 0$.
    Concretely, if you are within $0.1$ of the solution, the next step brings you
    within roughly $0.01$, the step after that within $0.0001$, and so on -- the
    number of accurate digits roughly doubles each iteration.  On a strictly
    quadratic function (where the second-order Taylor model is exact) Newton's
    method converges in \textbf{exactly one step} regardless of the starting
    point.  The computational cost per iteration is $O(n^3)$ (big-O n-cubed) for
    solving the $n \times n$ linear system, which can be expensive for large $n$
    but is often acceptable when $n$ is small to moderate.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{Potential Problems and Failure Modes}
        Newton's method can fail or behave badly in three ways:
        \begin{enumerate}
          \item \textbf{Zero or vanishing second derivative.}  If $f''(x^{(k)})
            \approx 0$ (the function is nearly flat), the denominator of the
            Newton step is near zero, and the step size becomes enormous.  The
            iterate may jump far outside the region where the quadratic
            approximation is valid.
          \item \textbf{Negative curvature.}  If $f''(x^{(k)}) < 0$ (or $\Hbf^{(k)}$
            is indefinite), the parabola opens downward.  The Newton step moves
            toward a \emph{maximum} of the quadratic model, not a minimum.  The
            iterate may oscillate or diverge.
          \item \textbf{Starting far from the minimum.}  Even with positive
            curvature, if the quadratic model is a poor approximation (the iterate
            is far from the minimum), the step may overshoot.  A line search -- or
            the Levenberg-Marquardt damping described later -- can prevent this.
        \end{enumerate}
      \end{alertblock}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}

      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_newton_behaviors.pdf}

       toward the minimum).  Right: divergence near a region of negative curvature,

    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \centering

      \\[4pt]
      {\scriptsize Each dashed curve is the local quadratic approximation $q(x)$ at

    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{What to Look for in This Plot}
        Observe how the spacing between successive iterates shrinks rapidly: early
        steps are large (the quadratic approximation is coarse), while later steps
        are tiny (the approximation nearly matches the true function).  This
        geometric shrinkage -- where each step's error is roughly the \emph{square}
        of the previous error -- is the hallmark of quadratic convergence.
      \end{block}
      \begin{block}{Termination Criteria}
        We stop when any of the following is satisfied:
        \begin{enumerate}
          \item $\|\gbf^{(k)}\| \leq \epsilon$ (gradient magnitude below threshold
            $\epsilon$, meaning we are near a stationary point),
          \item $\|\xbf^{(k+1)} - \xbf^{(k)}\| \leq \epsilon$ (step size below
            threshold, meaning very little movement),
          \item The iteration count exceeds a preset maximum.
        \end{enumerate}
        Criteria 1 and 2 both indicate we are close to a minimum; hitting the
        maximum iteration count without satisfying criteria 1 or 2 suggests a
        convergence problem (e.g.\ negative curvature or a poorly scaled problem).
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Newton's Method: Python Implementation}
\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Newton's method (multivariate) in Python}]
import numpy as np
from numpy.linalg import solve, norm

def newtons_method(f, grad_f, hess_f, x0, eps=1e-8, max_iter=100):
    """
    Newton's method for unconstrained minimization.
    f       : objective function R^n -> R
    grad_f  : gradient function R^n -> R^n
    hess_f  : Hessian function  R^n -> R^{n x n}
    x0      : initial point (numpy array)
    """
    x = np.array(x0, dtype=float)
    history = [x.copy()]
    for k in range(max_iter):
        g = grad_f(x)
        if norm(g) < eps:
            break
        H = hess_f(x)
        # Solve H*d = -g  (more stable than inverting H)
        d = solve(H, -g)
        x = x + d
        history.append(x.copy())
    return x, history

# --- Example: Booth's function f(x) = (x1+2x2-7)^2 + (2x1+x2-5)^2 ---
def booth(x):
    return (x[0] + 2*x[1] - 7)**2 + (2*x[0] + x[1] - 5)**2

def grad_booth(x):
    return np.array([
        2*(x[0]+2*x[1]-7) + 4*(2*x[0]+x[1]-5),
        4*(x[0]+2*x[1]-7) + 2*(2*x[0]+x[1]-5)
    ])

def hess_booth(x):
    return np.array([[10., 8.], [8., 10.]])  # constant Hessian

x_opt, hist = newtons_method(booth, grad_booth, hess_booth, x0=[9., 8.])
print(f"Minimum at: {x_opt}")   # -> [1. 3.]
print(f"Iterations: {len(hist)-1}")  # -> 1 (one step!)
\end{lstlisting}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Worked Example: Newton's Method on Booth's Function}

  Booth's function is a classic 2D test problem that is quadratic in both
  variables.  Because it is exactly quadratic, Newton's method -- which builds a
  quadratic model -- captures $f$ perfectly and converges in a \emph{single step}
  from any starting point.  Working through the algebra manually is a good way to
  build intuition for what the Newton update is actually computing.

  \begin{block}{Booth's Function}
    \[
      f(\xbf) \;=\; (x_1 + 2x_2 - 7)^2 + (2x_1 + x_2 - 5)^2
    \]
    Here $x_1$ (x sub 1) and $x_2$ (x sub 2) are the two components of the
    input vector $\xbf = [x_1,\, x_2]^\top$.  The function is the sum of two
    squared linear expressions; geometrically it is an elliptic paraboloid (a
    bowl tilted in the $x_1$-$x_2$ plane).  The unique global minimum is at
    $\xbf^* = [1,\,3]^\top$, where both squared terms equal zero.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Gradient of Booth's Function}
    Differentiating with respect to $x_1$ and $x_2$ respectively:
    \[
      \nabla f(\xbf) \;=\;
      \begin{bmatrix}
        \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \\[6pt]
        \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}
      \end{bmatrix}
      \;=\;
      \begin{bmatrix}
        2(x_1+2x_2-7)\cdot 1 + 2(2x_1+x_2-5)\cdot 2 \\
        2(x_1+2x_2-7)\cdot 2 + 2(2x_1+x_2-5)\cdot 1
      \end{bmatrix}
      \;=\;
      \begin{bmatrix}10x_1 + 8x_2 - 34\\8x_1 + 10x_2 - 38\end{bmatrix}
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Hessian of Booth's Function (Constant!)}
    Differentiating the gradient again:
    \[
      \mathbf{H}(\xbf) \;=\;
      \begin{bmatrix}
        \partial^2 f/\partial x_1^2 & \partial^2 f/\partial x_1\partial x_2 \\
        \partial^2 f/\partial x_2\partial x_1 & \partial^2 f/\partial x_2^2
      \end{bmatrix}
      \;=\;
      \begin{bmatrix}10 & 8\\8 & 10\end{bmatrix}
    \]
    Notice that the Hessian is \emph{constant} -- it does not depend on $\xbf$ at
    all.  This is the defining property of a quadratic function: its second
    derivatives are the same everywhere.  Both eigenvalues of this matrix are
    positive ($18$ and $2$), confirming the function is strictly convex.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{One Newton Step from $\xbf^{(1)} = [9,\,8]^\top$}
    We start at $\xbf^{(1)} = [9,\,8]^\top$ (a point far from the minimum).

    \textbf{Step 1: compute the gradient at $\xbf^{(1)}$.}
    \[
      \gbf^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix}10(9)+8(8)-34\\8(9)+10(8)-38\end{bmatrix}
                = \begin{bmatrix}90+64-34\\72+80-38\end{bmatrix}
                = \begin{bmatrix}120\\114\end{bmatrix}
    \]

    \textbf{Step 2: solve $\Hbf\,\mathbf{d} = -\gbf^{(1)}$.}
    \[
      \begin{bmatrix}10&8\\8&10\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{d}
      = -\begin{bmatrix}120\\114\end{bmatrix}
    \]
    The inverse of the constant $2\times 2$ Hessian is
    $\Hbf^{-1} = \frac{1}{36}\begin{bmatrix}10&-8\\-8&10\end{bmatrix}$
    (using the formula for $2\times 2$ matrix inverse with determinant
    $10\cdot10 - 8\cdot8 = 36$).  Therefore:
    \[
      \mathbf{d} = -\Hbf^{-1}\gbf^{(1)}
                = -\frac{1}{36}\begin{bmatrix}10&-8\\-8&10\end{bmatrix}
                  \begin{bmatrix}120\\114\end{bmatrix}
                = -\frac{1}{36}\begin{bmatrix}288\\114\cdot10 - 120\cdot8\end{bmatrix}
    \]
    Working out the arithmetic:
    $10\cdot120 - 8\cdot114 = 1200-912 = 288$ and
    $-8\cdot120 + 10\cdot114 = -960+1140 = 180$, so
    $\mathbf{d} = -\frac{1}{36}[288,\,180]^\top = [-8,\,-5]^\top$.

    \textbf{Step 3: update.}
    \[
      \xbf^{(2)} = \xbf^{(1)} + \mathbf{d}
                = \begin{bmatrix}9\\8\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}-8\\-5\end{bmatrix}
                = \begin{bmatrix}1\\3\end{bmatrix}
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{Result: Converged in One Iteration}
        We have jumped directly from $[9,\,8]^\top$ to the global minimum
        $[1,\,3]^\top$ in a single Newton step.  This is possible because
        Booth's function is exactly quadratic, so the quadratic model is
        perfect everywhere -- not just locally.  The gradient at $\xbf^{(2)}$
        is exactly zero, confirming optimality.
      \end{alertblock}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{fig_booth_newton.pdf}
      \\[4pt]
      {\scriptsize The trajectory of Newton's method on Booth's function.  The
       arrow shows the single step from the starting point to the global minimum.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\section{Secant Method}
% =====================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{6.2 The Secant Method}

  Newton's method requires the second derivative $f''(x)$, which must be computed
  analytically or numerically.  For some functions this is expensive, error-prone,
  or simply inconvenient.  The \textbf{secant method} sidesteps this by
  \emph{approximating} $f''$ from two recent evaluations of the first derivative
  $f'$, in the same way that a secant line through two points approximates the
  derivative.

  \begin{block}{Second Derivative Approximation}
    Given two iterates $x^{(k-1)}$ and $x^{(k)}$ (x at the previous iteration
    and the current iteration), and the corresponding first-derivative values
    $f'(x^{(k-1)})$ and $f'(x^{(k)})$, we approximate the second derivative as:
    \[
      f''\!\left(x^{(k)}\right)
      \;\approx\;
      \frac{f'\!\left(x^{(k)}\right) - f'\!\left(x^{(k-1)}\right)}
           {x^{(k)} - x^{(k-1)}}
    \]
    This is a \textbf{finite difference} approximation: it measures how much the
    slope $f'$ has changed between the two points, divided by how far apart those
    points are.  When $x^{(k)}$ and $x^{(k-1)}$ are close, this ratio converges
    to the true second derivative.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Secant Update Rule}
    Substituting this approximation into the Newton update
    $x^{(k+1)} = x^{(k)} - f'(x^{(k)})/f''(x^{(k)})$ gives the secant step:
    \[
      x^{(k+1)} \;\leftarrow\; x^{(k)}
        - \frac{x^{(k)} - x^{(k-1)}}
               {f'\!\left(x^{(k)}\right) - f'\!\left(x^{(k-1)}\right)}
          \, f'\!\left(x^{(k)}\right)
    \]
    \textbf{Symbol guide:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $x^{(k)}$ and $x^{(k-1)}$: the two most recent iterates.
      \item $f'(x^{(k)})$ and $f'(x^{(k-1)})$: the first derivative evaluated at
        those two iterates.
      \item The ratio $(x^{(k)} - x^{(k-1)})/(f'(x^{(k)}) - f'(x^{(k-1)}))$: the
        reciprocal of the approximate second derivative, i.e.\ the approximate
        inverse curvature.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read This Formula}
    The secant step is exactly Newton's step, but with the true second derivative
    replaced by an approximation built from two successive gradient evaluations.
    The approximation improves as the iterates approach each other (and both
    approach the minimum), which is why the method still achieves better-than-linear
    convergence -- it converges at order $\approx 1.618$ (the golden ratio),
    compared to order 1 (linear) for gradient descent and order 2 (quadratic) for
    Newton's method.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_secant_method.pdf}
      \\[4pt]
      {\scriptsize The secant line (dashed) passes through the two most recent
       evaluations of $f'$.  Its slope approximates $f''$; where it crosses zero
       gives the next iterate.  As the iterates converge, the secant line becomes
       an increasingly good proxy for the tangent to $f'$.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Secant Algorithm}
        \begin{enumerate}
          \item Choose an initial point $x^{(1)}$ and a small step size
            $\alpha$ (alpha) to generate the second starting point:
            $x_\text{prev} = x^{(1)} + \alpha f'(x^{(1)})$.
          \item Evaluate $g_\text{prev} = f'(x_\text{prev})$ and set $x = x^{(1)}$.
          \item \textbf{Repeat until convergence:}
            \begin{enumerate}
              \item Compute $g = f'(x)$.
              \item If $|g| \leq \epsilon$ (epsilon), stop.
              \item Compute the secant step:
                $x' = x - \dfrac{x - x_\text{prev}}{g - g_\text{prev}}\cdot g$.
              \item Shift: $x_\text{prev} \leftarrow x$, $g_\text{prev} \leftarrow g$.
              \item Update: $x \leftarrow x'$.
            \end{enumerate}
        \end{enumerate}
      \end{block}
      \begin{block}{Properties and Trade-offs}
        The secant method requires only \emph{first} derivatives (no $f''$), but
        it needs one extra initialisation step to create two starting points.  It
        can suffer the same oscillation and divergence problems as Newton's method
        (when the approximate curvature is negative or near zero), but on average
        needs fewer function evaluations than full Newton because it reuses
        information across iterations.  The method as stated is \textbf{univariate
        only}; quasi-Newton methods (Section~6.5) generalise it to $n$ dimensions.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Secant Method: Python Implementation}
\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Secant method for univariate minimization in Python}]
import numpy as np

def secant_method(fp, x0, alpha=1e-3, eps=1e-8, max_iter=200):
    """
    Secant method for univariate minimization.
    fp  : first derivative f'(x)
    x0  : initial point
    alpha: step for initializing previous point
    """
    x_prev = x0 + alpha * fp(x0)  # small step in gradient direction
    g_prev = fp(x_prev)
    x = x0
    history = [x]

    for _ in range(max_iter):
        g = fp(x)
        if abs(g) < eps:
            break
        denom = g - g_prev
        if abs(denom) < 1e-14:
            break                        # avoid division by zero
        x_new = x - (x - x_prev) / denom * g
        x_prev, g_prev = x, g
        x = x_new
        history.append(x)

    return x, history

# Example: minimize f(x) = x^4 - 3x^3 + 2,  f'(x) = 4x^3 - 9x^2
fp = lambda x: 4*x**3 - 9*x**2
x_min, hist = secant_method(fp, x0=3.0)
print(f"Minimum at x = {x_min:.6f}")    # ~2.25 (local min)
print(f"Iterations:   {len(hist)-1}")
\end{lstlisting}
\end{frame}

\section{Levenberg-Marquardt Algorithm}
% =====================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{6.3 Levenberg-Marquardt Algorithm}

  Newton's method is powerful but brittle: it fails when the Hessian is not
  positive definite, and it can diverge when the quadratic model is a poor
  approximation (far from the minimum).  The \textbf{Levenberg-Marquardt (LM)}
  algorithm repairs both problems by adding a positive constant $\delta$ (delta)
  to the Hessian's diagonal before solving.  This ``damping'' pushes the
  effective curvature to be positive, making the modified Hessian positive
  definite and the resulting step a genuine descent direction.  Moreover, by
  adjusting $\delta$ at each iteration based on whether the last step was
  accepted, the algorithm smoothly interpolates between gradient descent (when far
  from the minimum, large $\delta$) and Newton's method (when close to the
  minimum, small $\delta$).

  \begin{block}{LM Update: Plain Damping}
    The simplest form adds $\delta$ (delta, the damping factor) times the
    identity matrix $\mathbf{I}$ (I, a matrix with ones on the diagonal and
    zeros elsewhere) to the Hessian:
    \[
      \xbf^{(k+1)} \;=\; \xbf^{(k)} - \left(\Hbf^{(k)} + \delta\Ibf\right)^{-1}\gbf^{(k)}
    \]
    \textbf{Symbol guide:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\Hbf^{(k)}$ (bold H): the Hessian matrix at the current iterate.
      \item $\delta$ (delta): the damping factor, a positive scalar we adjust
        between iterations.
      \item $\Ibf$ (bold I): the $n\times n$ identity matrix -- the matrix
        equivalent of the number 1.
      \item Adding $\delta\Ibf$ to $\Hbf^{(k)}$ shifts every eigenvalue of $\Hbf$
        up by $\delta$; with large enough $\delta$ all eigenvalues become positive,
        guaranteeing positive definiteness.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Damping Formula}
    When $\delta \to 0$ (delta approaches zero), the damped matrix approaches
    $\Hbf^{(k)}$ and the step approaches the pure Newton step.  When $\delta \to
    \infty$ (delta grows large), the damped matrix is dominated by $\delta\Ibf$
    and the step $-(\delta\Ibf)^{-1}\gbf \approx -(1/\delta)\gbf$ approaches a
    tiny gradient descent step.  By adaptively tuning $\delta$, LM gets the
    rapid convergence of Newton near the minimum while behaving like the safer
    gradient descent when far away.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{block}{LM Update: Diagonal Scaling (Improved)}
    Rather than adding a uniform $\delta$ to all diagonal entries, we can scale
    the damping by the existing curvature on each axis:
    \[
      \xbf^{(k+1)} \;=\; \xbf^{(k)}
        - \Bigl(\Hbf^{(k)} + \delta\,\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\Hbf^{(k)}\right)\Bigr)^{-1}\gbf^{(k)}
    \]
    Here $\mathrm{diag}(\Hbf^{(k)})$ is the diagonal matrix formed from the diagonal
    entries of $\Hbf^{(k)}$.  Dimensions with large curvature (large $H_{ii}$) get
    more damping; dimensions with small curvature get less.  This produces
    \emph{scale-invariant} damping that works well even when the variables have
    very different magnitudes.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_lm_damping.pdf}
      \\[4pt]
      {\scriptsize As the damping factor $\delta$ (delta) increases from left to
       right, the step size shrinks from the pure Newton size toward zero.  At
       very large $\delta$ the method behaves identically to a scaled gradient
       descent step.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Adaptive Damping Strategy}
        \begin{enumerate}
          \item At each iteration, compute the candidate step $\xbf'$ using the
            current $\delta$.
          \item \textbf{If $f(\xbf') < f(\xbf)$ (step accepted):} the quadratic
            model was a good approximation, so reduce $\delta$ (move toward Newton):
            $\delta \leftarrow \delta \cdot \gamma_\text{acc}$ where
            $\gamma_\text{acc} \in (0,1)$ (typically $0.1$).
          \item \textbf{If $f(\xbf') \geq f(\xbf)$ (step rejected):} the
            approximation was poor, so increase $\delta$ (move toward gradient
            descent):
            $\delta \leftarrow \delta \cdot \gamma_\text{rej}$ where
            $\gamma_\text{rej} > 1$ (typically $10$).
        \end{enumerate}
        Here $\gamma_\text{acc}$ (gamma subscript ``acc'') and $\gamma_\text{rej}$
        (gamma subscript ``rej'') are hyperparameters (user-set constants) governing
        how aggressively $\delta$ changes.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Parameter Guidelines}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      Parameter & Meaning & Typical value \\
      \midrule
      $\delta$ & initial damping factor (starts moderate) & $1.0$ \\
      $\gamma_\text{acc}$ (gamma-acc) & factor to \emph{reduce} $\delta$ on acceptance & $0.1$ \\
      $\gamma_\text{rej}$ (gamma-rej) & factor to \emph{increase} $\delta$ on rejection & $10.0$ \\
      $\epsilon$ (epsilon) & floor for diagonal elements (prevents division by zero) & $10^{-6}$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{block}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_lm_convergence.pdf}
      \\[4pt]
      {\scriptsize LM convergence (top panel) and optimisation trajectories on
       Wheeler's Ridge (bottom panel).  The diagonal-scaling variant (LM-diag)
       adapts its step to the local curvature in each dimension, reaching the
       minimum more efficiently than the plain damping variant.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Why Diagonal Scaling Helps}
        Plain damping adds the same $\delta$ to every diagonal entry, treating all
        dimensions equally.  Diagonal scaling adds $\delta H_{ii}$ to the $i$-th
        diagonal, so directions with high curvature receive more damping.  This is
        especially helpful when the Hessian is poorly conditioned (eigenvalues span
        many orders of magnitude), which is common in machine learning and
        physics-based optimisation.  The floor $\epsilon$ on the diagonal values
        ensures the damped matrix is always invertible, even if $\Hbf$ has small or
        zero diagonal entries.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Levenberg-Marquardt: Python Implementation}
\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm in Python (NumPy)}]
import numpy as np
from numpy.linalg import solve

def levenberg_marquardt(f, grad_f, hess_f, x0,
                        delta=1.0, gamma_acc=0.1, gamma_rej=10.0,
                        eps=1e-6, max_iter=100):
    x = np.array(x0, dtype=float)
    history = [x.copy()]
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        g = grad_f(x)
        if np.linalg.norm(g) < eps:
            break
        H = hess_f(x)
        # Diagonal scaling: take component-wise max with eps
        d = np.maximum(np.diag(H), eps)
        M = H + delta * np.diag(d)
        try:
            step = solve(M, g)
        except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
            step = g   # fallback: gradient step
        x_new = x - step
        if f(x_new) < f(x):
            x = x_new
            delta *= gamma_acc          # accept: reduce damping
        else:
            delta *= gamma_rej          # reject: increase damping
        history.append(x.copy())
    return x, history
\end{lstlisting}
\end{frame}

\section{Levenberg-Marquardt for Sum of Squares}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{6.4 LM for Sum of Squares (Nonlinear Least Squares)}

  A very common problem in science and engineering is \textbf{nonlinear least
  squares}: fitting a parametric model to data by minimising the sum of squared
  residuals (the differences between observed and predicted values).  Although
  this is a special case of general optimisation, its structure allows us to
  approximate the Hessian cheaply using only first-order information -- and the
  approximation is always positive semi-definite, avoiding the indefinite Hessian
  problem that plagues plain Newton's method.

  \begin{block}{Objective: Sum of Squared Residuals}
    Let $f_i : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ (f sub i) be the $i$-th residual
    function -- for example, $f_i(\xbf) = \text{model}_i(\xbf) - y_i$ where
    $y_i$ is the $i$-th observation and $\text{model}_i(\xbf)$ is the model
    prediction.  The objective is:
    \[
      f(\xbf) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{m} f_i(\xbf)^2
    \]
    where the sum runs over all $m$ (m) data points or residuals.  We want to find
    $\xbf \in \mathbb{R}^n$ that makes this total squared error as small as
    possible.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Gradient of the Sum-of-Squares Objective}
    Differentiating with respect to $\xbf$ using the chain rule:
    \[
      \nabla f(\xbf) \;=\; 2\sum_{i=1}^{m} f_i(\xbf)\,\nabla f_i(\xbf)
    \]
    Here $\nabla f_i(\xbf) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (nabla f sub i) is the gradient of
    the $i$-th residual -- a vector of partial derivatives of the $i$-th residual
    with respect to each parameter.  This is the $i$-th row of the
    \textbf{Jacobian matrix} $\mathbf{J}$ (bold J), which stacks all residual
    gradients into a single $m \times n$ matrix.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Linearising the Residuals (Gauss-Newton Idea)}
    If we approximate each residual as linear in $\xbf$ around the current
    iterate $\xbf^{(k)}$:
    \[
      f_i(\xbf) \;\approx\; f_i\!\left(\xbf^{(k)}\right)
        + \nabla f_i\!\left(\xbf^{(k)}\right)^\top\!\bigl(\xbf - \xbf^{(k)}\bigr)
    \]
    and substitute into the gradient formula, we obtain an approximation of the
    gradient and Hessian using only first derivatives:
    \begin{align*}
      \tilde{\gbf}^{(k)} &= \sum_i f_i(\xbf^{(k)})\,\nabla f_i(\xbf^{(k)})
                          \;=\; \mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{r} \\[4pt]
      \tilde{\Hbf}^{(k)} &= \sum_i \nabla f_i(\xbf^{(k)})\,\nabla f_i(\xbf^{(k)})^\top
                          \;=\; \mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{J}
    \end{align*}
    where $\mathbf{r} = [f_1(\xbf^{(k)}),\ldots,f_m(\xbf^{(k)})]^\top$ (bold r)
    is the residual vector and $\mathbf{J}$ (bold J) is the Jacobian matrix with
    entry $J_{ij} = \partial f_i / \partial x_j$.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Advantage: No True Second Derivatives Needed}
    The approximate Hessian $\tilde{\Hbf}^{(k)} = \mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}$ is
    an \emph{outer product} (a sum of rank-1 matrices), which means it is always
    \textbf{positive semi-definite} (all eigenvalues $\geq 0$).  In particular,
    it never has negative eigenvalues, so the Gauss-Newton step is always at worst
    a neutral direction -- never an uphill direction.  This is a huge advantage
    over full Newton, which can produce saddle-point steps when the Hessian has
    negative eigenvalues.  Furthermore, building $\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}$
    requires only first derivatives $\partial f_i / \partial x_j$, which are
    typically far cheaper to compute than second derivatives.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{block}{LM Update for Sum of Squares}
    Incorporating the LM diagonal damping into the Gauss-Newton update:
    \[
      \xbf^{(k+1)} \;=\; \xbf^{(k)}
        - \Bigl(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}
          + \delta\,\mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}\bigr)\Bigr)^{-1}
          \mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{r}
    \]
    When residuals are small at the solution (which happens in well-fitting
    models), $\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}$ is an excellent approximation to the
    true Hessian, and the LM update gives near-Newton convergence rates while
    remaining stable throughout.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_lm_least_squares.pdf}
      \\[4pt]
      {\scriptsize Left: LM fitting the model $a\sin(bt+c)$ (a times sine of
       $(bt+c)$) to noisy observations (blue dots).  The fitted curve (solid line)
       agrees closely with the true signal.  Right: the objective value
       $f(\xbf) = \sum_i f_i(\xbf)^2$ decreases rapidly over iterations.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Comparison: LM vs.\ Full Newton}
        \begin{tabular}{lcc}
          \toprule
          Property & LM (sum-of-sq.) & Full Newton \\
          \midrule
          Hessian approx. & $\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}$ & exact $\nabla^2 f$ \\
          Always positive & \checkmark & No \\
          semi-definite? & & \\
          Cost per iter. & $O(mn^2)$ & $O(n^3)$ \\

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import least_squares

# --- Curve fitting: model a*sin(b*t + c) ---
np.random.seed(42)
t_obs = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 20)
y_obs = np.sin(t_obs) + 0.15 * np.random.randn(len(t_obs))

def model(params, t):
    a, b, c = params
    return a * np.sin(b * t + c)

def residuals(params):
    return model(params, t_obs) - y_obs  # vector of f_i(x)

# SciPy uses LM (method='lm') or trust-region approaches
result = least_squares(residuals, x0=[0.8, 0.9, 0.1], method='lm')

print(f"Fitted params:   a={result.x[0]:.4f}, b={result.x[1]:.4f}, c={result.x[2]:.4f}")
print(f"True params:     a=1.0000, b=1.0000, c=0.0000")
print(f"Final residual:  {0.5 * result.cost:.6f}")
print(f"Function evals:  {result.nfev}")

# Manual approach: build Jacobian explicitly
def jacobian(params, t):
    a, b, c = params
    dfa = np.sin(b*t + c)           # df/da
    dfb = a * t * np.cos(b*t + c)   # df/db
    dfc = a * np.cos(b*t + c)       # df/dc
    return np.column_stack([dfa, dfb, dfc])  # shape (m, 3)

J = jacobian(result.x, t_obs)
H_approx = J.T @ J                  # Gauss-Newton Hessian (3x3)
g_approx = J.T @ residuals(result.x)
print(f"\nGauss-Newton gradient norm: {np.linalg.norm(g_approx):.2e}")

\section{Quasi-Newton Methods}
% =====================================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{6.5 Quasi-Newton Methods: The Big Idea}

  Full Newton's method computes the Hessian $\Hbf$ exactly at every iteration,
  costing $O(n^2)$ gradient evaluations and $O(n^3)$ arithmetic to solve the
  resulting linear system.  For large problems ($n \geq 10^4$ or so) this is
  prohibitive.  \textbf{Quasi-Newton methods} avoid computing the Hessian
  altogether.  Instead, they maintain an approximation $\mathbf{Q}^{(k)}$ (bold Q
  at iteration $k$) to the \emph{inverse} Hessian $(\Hbf^{(k)})^{-1}$, which
  they update cheaply at each step using only gradient information.  Because the
  update costs $O(n^2)$ arithmetic and the approximation improves over time, these
  methods achieve \emph{superlinear} convergence (faster than linear, slower than
  quadratic) without ever forming or inverting the full Hessian.

  \begin{block}{Quasi-Newton Update Rule}
    The iterate update is:
    \[
      \xbf^{(k+1)} \;=\; \xbf^{(k)} - \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\,\gbf^{(k)}
    \]
    where $\mathbf{Q}^{(k)} \approx (\Hbf^{(k)})^{-1}$ is the current
    inverse-Hessian approximation.  Compare to Newton's update $\xbf^{(k+1)} =
    \xbf^{(k)} - (\Hbf^{(k)})^{-1}\gbf^{(k)}$: the structure is identical; the
    difference lies entirely in how $\mathbf{Q}$ is obtained.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{The Secant Condition}
    After each step we have new information: the gradient changed from $\gbf^{(k)}$
    to $\gbf^{(k+1)}$ as we moved from $\xbf^{(k)}$ to $\xbf^{(k+1)}$.  We
    require the updated approximation $\mathbf{Q}^{(k+1)}$ to satisfy the
    \textbf{secant condition}:
    \[
      \mathbf{Q}^{(k+1)}\,\deltabf^{(k)} \;=\; \gammabf^{(k)}
    \]
    where:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\deltabf^{(k)} = \xbf^{(k+1)} - \xbf^{(k)}$ (delta superscript k,
        bold) is the \emph{step vector} -- how far we just moved.
      \item $\gammabf^{(k)} = \gbf^{(k+1)} - \gbf^{(k)}$ (gamma superscript k,
        bold) is the \emph{gradient difference} -- how much the gradient changed.
    \end{itemize}
    The secant condition says: the new inverse-Hessian approximation, when applied
    to the step we just took, should predict the gradient change we just observed.
    This is the multidimensional analogue of the secant approximation to $f''$.
    With $n$ dimensions this condition provides $n$ equations but $n^2$ unknowns
    in $\mathbf{Q}^{(k+1)}$, so there are infinitely many solutions -- different
    quasi-Newton variants impose additional criteria (symmetry, positive
    definiteness, minimum change) to pick a unique update.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Summary: Three Main Variants}
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{llll}
      \toprule
      Method & Update rank & Always PSD? & Memory cost \\
      \midrule
      SR1  & Rank-1 (one outer product) & No  & $O(n^2)$ \\
      DFP  & Rank-2 (two outer products) & Yes (under curvature cond.) & $O(n^2)$ \\
      BFGS & Rank-2 (dual of DFP) & Yes (under curvature cond.) & $O(n^2)$ \\
      L-BFGS & Rank-2 (implicit, limited history) & Yes & $O(mn)$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \\[4pt]
    {\scriptsize PSD = positive semi-definite; $m$ = history length (typically 3--20).}
  \end{block}

\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{DFP Update (Davidon-Fletcher-Powell)}

  The DFP method (named after William Davidon, Roger Fletcher, and Michael
  Powell, who developed it in the 1960s) was the first widely used quasi-Newton
  method.  At each iteration it performs a \emph{rank-2 update} of the
  inverse-Hessian approximation $\mathbf{Q}$ -- meaning the new $\mathbf{Q}$
  differs from the old one by a matrix of rank at most 2 (the sum of two
  rank-1 outer-product matrices).  This keeps the update cheap ($O(n^2)$) while
  incorporating information from both the step and the gradient change.

  \begin{block}{DFP Update Formula}
    \[
      \mathbf{Q}^{(k+1)} \;=\; \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}
        - \frac{\mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gammabf\,\gammabf^\top \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}}
               {\gammabf^\top \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gammabf}
        + \frac{\deltabf\,\deltabf^\top}{\deltabf^\top\gammabf}
    \]
    \textbf{Symbol guide:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\mathbf{Q}^{(k)}$ (bold Q): the current inverse-Hessian approximation
        (an $n\times n$ positive definite matrix).
      \item $\deltabf = \xbf^{(k+1)} - \xbf^{(k)}$ (bold delta): the step vector.
      \item $\gammabf = \gbf^{(k+1)} - \gbf^{(k)}$ (bold gamma): the gradient
        difference vector.
      \item $\gammabf^\top \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gammabf$: a scalar quadratic form --
        a positive scalar if $\mathbf{Q}^{(k)}$ is positive definite.
      \item $\deltabf^\top\gammabf$: a scalar curvature measure; must be positive
        (the \emph{curvature condition}) for the update to preserve positive
        definiteness.
      \item The first fraction subtracts the component of $\mathbf{Q}$ that is
        ``inconsistent'' with the new gradient information; the second fraction
        adds a new outer product that satisfies the secant condition exactly.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{DFP Algorithm Steps}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Initialise: $\mathbf{Q}^{(1)} = \Ibf_n$ (start with the identity
        matrix as the inverse-Hessian approximation -- this is equivalent to a
        gradient descent step at iteration 1).
      \item Compute search direction: $\mathbf{d}^{(k)} = -\mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gbf^{(k)}$.
      \item Perform a line search: find $\alpha^* = \argmin_\alpha f(\xbf^{(k)} +
        \alpha\mathbf{d}^{(k)})$ (alpha-star, the optimal step length).
      \item Update: $\xbf^{(k+1)} = \xbf^{(k)} + \alpha^*\mathbf{d}^{(k)}$.
      \item Compute $\deltabf = \xbf^{(k+1)} - \xbf^{(k)}$ and $\gammabf =
        \gbf^{(k+1)} - \gbf^{(k)}$.
      \item Apply the DFP formula to obtain $\mathbf{Q}^{(k+1)}$.
      \item Repeat from step 2 until convergence.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Properties}
        If $\deltabf^\top\gammabf > 0$ (the curvature condition holds, meaning the
        gradient increased in the direction we stepped), then:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item $\mathbf{Q}^{(k+1)}$ remains positive definite -- guaranteed
            descent directions at every iteration.
          \item The secant condition $\mathbf{Q}^{(k+1)}\gammabf = \deltabf$ is
            exactly satisfied.
          \item The update is a rank-2 change to $\mathbf{Q}$.
        \end{itemize}
        DFP works well with \emph{exact} line search but can underperform with
        inexact line search (where the Wolfe conditions are satisfied only
        approximately).
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{Curvature Condition $\deltabf^\top\gammabf > 0$}
        This condition says that the gradient and the step point in the same
        ``direction'' (their inner product is positive).  It is guaranteed when the
        line search satisfies the Wolfe conditions.  If it fails, skip the DFP
        update for that iteration and keep $\mathbf{Q}$ unchanged.
      \end{alertblock}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{BFGS Update (Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno)}

  The BFGS method (named after Charles Broyden, Roger Fletcher, Donald Goldfarb,
  and David Shanno, who independently discovered it in 1970) is currently
  \textbf{the most widely used quasi-Newton method} in general optimisation
  software.  It can be derived as the ``dual'' of DFP by swapping the roles of
  $\deltabf$ and $\gammabf$, which gives it superior numerical stability when line
  searches are performed inexactly.

  \begin{block}{BFGS Update Formula}
    \[
      \mathbf{Q}^{(k+1)} \;=\;
        \left(\Ibf - \rho\,\deltabf\,\gammabf^\top\right)
        \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}
        \left(\Ibf - \rho\,\gammabf\,\deltabf^\top\right)
        + \rho\,\deltabf\,\deltabf^\top
    \]
    where $\rho = \dfrac{1}{\deltabf^\top\gammabf}$ (rho, the reciprocal of the
    curvature measure).

    \textbf{Symbol guide:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\rho$ (rho): a positive scalar that normalises the curvature;
        $\rho = 1/(\deltabf^\top\gammabf)$ where $\deltabf^\top\gammabf$ is the
        inner product of step and gradient change.
      \item $\Ibf - \rho\,\deltabf\,\gammabf^\top$: a rank-1 correction to the
        identity matrix; it ``rotates'' $\mathbf{Q}^{(k)}$ to be more consistent
        with the observed gradient change.
      \item $\rho\,\deltabf\,\deltabf^\top$: a rank-1 matrix added to ensure the
        secant condition is satisfied.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the BFGS Formula}
    Think of it as three steps: (1) the term $(\Ibf - \rho\,\deltabf\,\gammabf^\top)$
    on the right ``de-rotates'' the old approximation; (2) $\mathbf{Q}^{(k)}$
    applies the old inverse Hessian; (3) the left factor and the added term
    together re-orient the result to satisfy the secant condition.  The net effect
    is a rank-2 update that is often more numerically stable than DFP because it
    spreads the correction symmetrically around $\mathbf{Q}$.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Why BFGS Outperforms DFP in Practice}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item BFGS is the \emph{dual} of DFP (the roles of $\deltabf$ and
            $\gammabf$ are swapped), but this seemingly minor change dramatically
            improves robustness with inexact line searches.
          \item With an exact line search both DFP and BFGS produce identical
            iterates on quadratic functions; with inexact search BFGS almost always
            wins.
          \item BFGS has better self-correcting properties: even if one update is
            inaccurate, later updates pull $\mathbf{Q}$ back toward the true
            inverse Hessian.
          \item BFGS is the default method in many optimisation libraries (SciPy,
            MATLAB \texttt{fminunc}, etc.).
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_bfgs_update.pdf}
      \\[4pt]
      {\scriptsize The secant condition $\mathbf{Q}\deltabf = \gammabf$ (or
       equivalently $\mathbf{H}\deltabf = \gammabf$ for the Hessian approximation)
       ensures the inverse-Hessian approximation is consistent with the gradient
       change we observed after taking a step.}

      \begin{alertblock}{When to Skip the BFGS Update}
        If $\deltabf^\top\gammabf \approx 0$ (the curvature condition is nearly
        violated), the scalar $\rho$ becomes very large and the update is
        numerically unstable.  Simply skip the update for that iteration and keep
        $\mathbf{Q}^{(k+1)} = \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}$.
      \end{alertblock}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{BFGS: Python Implementation}
\begin{lstlisting}[caption={BFGS quasi-Newton method in Python}]
import numpy as np
from numpy.linalg import norm

def bfgs(f, grad_f, x0, eps=1e-8, max_iter=200):
    """BFGS quasi-Newton minimization (NumPy implementation)."""
    x = np.array(x0, dtype=float)
    n = len(x)
    Q = np.eye(n)          # initial inverse Hessian approx = I
    history = [f(x)]

    for _ in range(max_iter):
        g = grad_f(x)
        if norm(g) < eps:
            break
        d = -Q @ g         # descent direction

        # Backtracking line search (Armijo condition)
        alpha, c1 = 1.0, 1e-4
        for _ in range(50):
            if f(x + alpha*d) <= f(x) + c1*alpha*(g @ d):
                break
            alpha *= 0.5

        x_new = x + alpha * d
        g_new = grad_f(x_new)
        delta = x_new - x              # s in standard notation
        gamma = g_new - g              # y in standard notation
        dg = delta @ gamma

        if abs(dg) > 1e-10:            # curvature condition
            rho = 1.0 / dg
            A = np.eye(n) - rho * np.outer(delta, gamma)
            B = np.eye(n) - rho * np.outer(gamma, delta)
            Q = A @ Q @ B + rho * np.outer(delta, delta)  # BFGS update

        x = x_new
        history.append(f(x))
    return x, history

# Compare BFGS with scipy.optimize.minimize
from scipy.optimize import minimize
result = minimize(lambda x: (1-x[0])**2 + 100*(x[1]-x[0]**2)**2,  # Rosenbrock
                  x0=[-1.5, 1.0], method='BFGS',
                  jac=lambda x: np.array([-2*(1-x[0])-400*x[0]*(x[1]-x[0]**2),
                                           200*(x[1]-x[0]**2)]))
print(f"scipy BFGS minimum: {result.x}")      # -> [1. 1.]
print(f"Function evals:     {result.njev}")
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{SR1 Update: Symmetric Rank-1}

  The SR1 (Symmetric Rank-1) update is the simplest quasi-Newton update: it
  modifies $\mathbf{Q}$ by adding only a single outer-product matrix (a rank-1
  matrix).  Although it is simpler than BFGS, it does \emph{not} guarantee
  positive definiteness, which can occasionally cause the method to explore
  non-descent directions.  Despite this, SR1 is used in some trust-region
  frameworks because its rank-1 structure makes it easier to capture the
  non-convex (saddle-like) geometry that trust-region methods need to navigate.

  \begin{block}{SR1 Update Formula}
    \[
      \mathbf{Q}^{(k+1)} \;=\; \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}
        + \frac{\left(\deltabf - \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gammabf\right)
                \left(\deltabf - \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gammabf\right)^\top}
               {\left(\deltabf - \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gammabf\right)^\top\gammabf}
    \]
    \textbf{Symbol guide:}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\deltabf - \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gammabf$ (bold delta minus bold Q times
        bold gamma): the \emph{residual vector} -- the difference between the
        actual step and the step the current approximation would predict.  If
        $\mathbf{Q}^{(k)}$ were the true inverse Hessian this would be zero.
      \item The numerator $(\deltabf - \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gammabf)(\deltabf -
        \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gammabf)^\top$ is a symmetric rank-1 outer product.
      \item The denominator $(\deltabf - \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gammabf)^\top\gammabf$
        is a scalar; the update is undefined (division by zero) when this is zero.
    \end{itemize}
    \textbf{Interpretation:} the update adds a rank-1 matrix that ``corrects'' the
    current approximation in the direction of the prediction residual.  Only one
    outer product is needed (rank-1), whereas DFP and BFGS use two (rank-2).
  \end{block}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{SR1 Properties and Use Cases}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Simplicity}: requires only one outer product per iteration.
          \item \textbf{No positive-definiteness guarantee}: the updated
            $\mathbf{Q}^{(k+1)}$ may have negative eigenvalues.
          \item \textbf{Better non-convex approximation}: because it is not forced
            to remain positive definite, SR1 can sometimes better approximate
            Hessians with negative eigenvalues (near saddle points).
          \item \textbf{Trust-region pairing}: often paired with a trust-region
            step limit to keep the iterate safe even when $\mathbf{Q}$ is
            indefinite.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{Skip Condition for SR1}
        The SR1 update is numerically unstable or undefined when:
        \[
          \left|(\deltabf - \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gammabf)^\top\gammabf\right|
          < \epsilon \cdot \|\deltabf - \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}\gammabf\|\cdot\|\gammabf\|
        \]
        (the denominator is small relative to the numerator components).  In
        practice: skip the update whenever the denominator is below a threshold
        $\epsilon$ (epsilon) times the norms of the constituent vectors.
      \end{alertblock}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{6.5 L-BFGS: Limited-Memory BFGS}

  The full BFGS method stores an $n \times n$ matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ and updates it
  at each iteration, costing $O(n^2)$ memory and $O(n^2)$ arithmetic per step.
  For $n = 10^6$ (one million parameters, as in a medium-sized deep learning
  model), storing a dense $n\times n$ matrix would require $10^{12}$ numbers --
  completely infeasible.  \textbf{L-BFGS} (Limited-memory BFGS) solves this by
  never forming $\mathbf{Q}$ explicitly.  Instead, it stores only the $m$ most
  recent $(\deltabf, \gammabf)$ pairs ($m$ is typically 5--20) and uses a clever
  \textbf{two-loop recursion} to compute the matrix-vector product
  $\mathbf{Q}\gbf$ implicitly in $O(mn)$ time.  The rest of the algorithm is
  identical to BFGS.

  \begin{block}{L-BFGS: Two-Loop Recursion}
    Given the $m$ most recent pairs $\{(\deltabf^{(i)}, \gammabf^{(i)})\}$ stored
    in a circular buffer, and the current gradient $\nabla f(\xbf)$, the two-loop
    recursion computes the descent direction $\mathbf{d} \approx
    -\mathbf{Q}\nabla f(\xbf)$ as follows:

    \textbf{Backward pass} (iterates from most recent to oldest, $i = m$ down to
    $1$): this pass computes scalar weights $\alpha^{(i)}$ (alpha superscript i)
    and modifies a running vector $\mathbf{q}$:
    \[
      \alpha^{(i)} = \frac{\left(\deltabf^{(i)}\right)^\top\mathbf{q}^{(i)}}
                          {\left(\deltabf^{(i)}\right)^\top\gammabf^{(i)}},
      \qquad
      \mathbf{q}^{(i-1)} = \mathbf{q}^{(i)} - \alpha^{(i)}\gammabf^{(i)}
    \]
    Starting with $\mathbf{q}^{(m)} = \nabla f(\xbf)$ (the current gradient).

    \textbf{Initial scaling}: after the backward pass, scale the result:
    \[
      \mathbf{z}^{(0)} \;=\;
      \frac{\left(\deltabf^{(m)}\right)^\top\gammabf^{(m)}}
           {\left(\gammabf^{(m)}\right)^\top\gammabf^{(m)}}\,\mathbf{q}^{(0)}
    \]
    This scaling (the ratio of two inner products) normalises the initial Hessian
    approximation to roughly the true Hessian scale -- a key stabilisation step.

    \textbf{Forward pass} (iterates from oldest to most recent, $i = 1$ to $m$):
    \[
      \mathbf{z}^{(i)} \;=\; \mathbf{z}^{(i-1)} + \deltabf^{(i)}
        \left(\alpha^{(i)} - \frac{\left(\gammabf^{(i)}\right)^\top\mathbf{z}^{(i-1)}}
                                  {\left(\deltabf^{(i)}\right)^\top\gammabf^{(i)}}\right)
    \]

    \textbf{Final descent direction:} $\mathbf{d} = -\mathbf{z}^{(m)}$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Two-Loop Recursion}
    The two loops together apply the implicit matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ (built from the
    stored history) to the gradient vector $\nabla f(\xbf)$ without ever forming
    $\mathbf{Q}$ explicitly.  The backward pass ``undoes'' the effect of each
    stored gradient-change pair on the query vector, accumulating scalar
    corrections $\alpha^{(i)}$.  The forward pass re-applies these corrections in
    the opposite order.  The net result is the same vector you would get if you
    had the full $n\times n$ BFGS matrix stored and computed $\mathbf{Q}\nabla f(\xbf)$ directly.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{L-BFGS Algorithm Summary}
        \begin{enumerate}
          \item Initialise: empty history deque, starting point $\xbf^{(1)}$.
          \item Compute $\gbf = \nabla f(\xbf)$.
          \item If the history is empty, use $\mathbf{d} = -\gbf$ (steepest
            descent -- the identity matrix as inverse Hessian).
          \item Otherwise, compute $\mathbf{d}$ via the two-loop recursion above.
          \item Perform a line search: find step length $\alpha^*$ satisfying the
            Wolfe conditions (sufficient decrease and curvature condition).
          \item Update: $\xbf \leftarrow \xbf + \alpha^*\mathbf{d}$.
          \item Compute new $\gbf_\text{new} = \nabla f(\xbf_\text{new})$.
          \item Store $\deltabf = \xbf_\text{new} - \xbf$ and $\gammabf =
            \gbf_\text{new} - \gbf$ in the history.  Discard the oldest pair if
            the history exceeds $m$ entries.
          \item Go to step 2.
        \end{enumerate}
      \end{block}
      \begin{block}{Memory Comparison}
        \begin{tabular}{lll}
          \toprule
          Method & Memory & Notes \\
          \midrule
          BFGS    & $O(n^2)$ & stores full $\mathbf{Q}$ \\
          L-BFGS  & $O(mn)$  & stores $m$ vector pairs \\
          \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
        \\[2pt]
        {\scriptsize With $m = 10$ and $n = 10^6$: BFGS needs $\sim 8$ terabytes;
         L-BFGS needs $\sim 160$ megabytes.}
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_quasi_newton_comparison.pdf}
      \\[4pt]
      {\scriptsize Objective function value vs.\ iteration count for DFP, BFGS,
       and L-BFGS with history sizes $m = 1, 3, 5$ on the 2D Rosenbrock function.
       L-BFGS with $m = 1$ (only the last pair stored) behaves like a scaled
       gradient descent.  As $m$ increases, L-BFGS approaches full BFGS
       performance.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{L-BFGS: Python Implementation}
\begin{lstlisting}[caption={L-BFGS with two-loop recursion in Python}]
import numpy as np
from collections import deque

def lbfgs(f, grad_f, x0, m=5, eps=1e-8, max_iter=200):
    """L-BFGS quasi-Newton minimization."""
    x = np.array(x0, dtype=float)
    deltas = deque(maxlen=m)    # step differences s_i
    gammas = deque(maxlen=m)    # gradient differences y_i
    history = [f(x)]

    for _ in range(max_iter):
        g = grad_f(x)
        if np.linalg.norm(g) < eps:
            break

        # ---- Two-loop recursion ----
        q = g.copy()
        alphas = []
        for dk, yk in zip(reversed(deltas), reversed(gammas)):
            rho_k = 1.0 / (dk @ yk + 1e-15)
            a = rho_k * (dk @ q)
            q = q - a * yk
            alphas.append((a, rho_k, dk, yk))
        alphas.reverse()

        # Initial scaling
        if deltas:
            dm, ym = deltas[-1], gammas[-1]
            scale = (dm @ ym) / (ym @ ym + 1e-15)
        else:
            scale = 1.0
        z = scale * q

        for (a, rho_k, dk, yk) in alphas:
            beta = rho_k * (yk @ z)
            z = z + dk * (a - beta)
        d = -z
        # ---- End two-loop ----

        # Backtracking line search
        alpha = 1.0
        for _ in range(50):
            if f(x + alpha*d) <= f(x) + 1e-4*alpha*(g @ d):
                break
            alpha *= 0.5

        x_new = x + alpha * d
        g_new = grad_f(x_new)
        deltas.append(x_new - x)
        gammas.append(g_new - g)
        x = x_new
        history.append(f(x))
    return x, history

# SciPy equivalent (uses L-BFGS-B):
from scipy.optimize import minimize
res = minimize(lambda x: (1-x[0])**2 + 100*(x[1]-x[0]**2)**2,
               [-1.5, 1.0], method='L-BFGS-B',
               jac=lambda x: np.array([-2*(1-x[0])-400*x[0]*(x[1]-x[0]**2),
                                        200*(x[1]-x[0]**2)]))
print(f"L-BFGS-B: {res.x}")   # -> [1. 1.]
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{6.6 Chapter Summary}

  This chapter has presented a family of optimisation algorithms that share a
  common theme: using curvature information (second-order information) to take
  more informed steps than gradient descent alone.  Each method represents a
  different trade-off between computational cost, robustness, and convergence
  speed.

  \begin{block}{What We Learned}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Second-order information} (the Hessian $\Hbf$, nabla-squared
        $f$) enables us to build a quadratic local model of $f$ and jump directly
        to its minimum, rather than following the gradient by small steps.  This
        is why second-order methods are much faster near a minimum.

      \item \textbf{Newton's method} updates as $\xbf^{(k+1)} = \xbf^{(k)} -
        (\Hbf^{(k)})^{-1}\gbf^{(k)}$ (minus H-inverse times gradient), achieves
        \emph{quadratic} convergence near a local minimum (error squares at each
        step), and converges in a single step on exactly quadratic functions.
        However, it requires computing and inverting the $n\times n$ Hessian at
        every iteration ($O(n^3)$ cost), and it fails when $\Hbf$ is indefinite.

      \item \textbf{The secant method} approximates $f''$ from two successive
        gradient evaluations, avoiding the need for second derivatives.  It
        achieves superlinear convergence of order $\approx 1.618$ and is
        applicable to univariate problems.

      \item \textbf{Levenberg-Marquardt} adds damping $\delta\Ibf$ (or
        $\delta\,\mathrm{diag}(\Hbf)$) to the Hessian before inverting,
        interpolating between gradient descent (large $\delta$) and Newton's
        method (small $\delta$).  Adaptive $\delta$ adjustments make it robust
        globally while still converging fast near the minimum.

      \item \textbf{LM for sum of squares} replaces the true Hessian with the
        Gauss-Newton approximation $\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}$ -- always positive
        semi-definite and requiring only first-order Jacobian information.  This
        is the workhorse of nonlinear least-squares fitting in practice.

      \item \textbf{Quasi-Newton methods} (DFP, BFGS, SR1) maintain a running
        approximation $\mathbf{Q}$ to the inverse Hessian, updated at each step
        using only gradient differences.  This achieves superlinear convergence
        without computing $\Hbf$, at a cost of $O(n^2)$ per iteration.  BFGS is
        the most robust quasi-Newton method and should be the default choice for
        problems with up to $\sim 10^4$ variables.

      \item \textbf{L-BFGS} reduces memory from $O(n^2)$ to $O(mn)$ by discarding
        all but the last $m$ history pairs and using the two-loop recursion to
        compute $\mathbf{Q}\gbf$ implicitly.  It is the method of choice for
        large-scale problems with $n \geq 10^5$ (deep learning, PDE-constrained
        optimisation, etc.).
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Overarching Takeaway}
    There is no single best second-order method: Newton for small problems with
    available second derivatives, BFGS for medium-scale problems with only
    gradient access, L-BFGS for large-scale, and LM for sum-of-squares
    structure.  Understanding when to apply each is as important as understanding
    how each works.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{6.7 Selected Exercises}

  \begin{block}{Exercise 6.1: Second-Order vs.\ First-Order Information}
    \textbf{Question.} What advantage does second-order information provide about
    convergence that first-order information lacks?

    \medskip
    \textbf{Answer.} Second-order information (the Hessian $\Hbf$) can
    \emph{certify} that a stationary point is a local minimum: a point where
    $\nabla f(\xbf) = \mathbf{0}$ (gradient is zero) is a local minimum if and
    only if $\Hbf$ is positive definite there.  First-order information alone
    cannot distinguish between a local minimum, a local maximum, and a saddle
    point -- all three have zero gradient.  Moreover, the Hessian directly
    provides the curvature information needed to determine an optimal step size,
    enabling quadratic convergence.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Exercise 6.9: One Newton Step on a Quadratic}
    \textbf{Question.} Starting from the origin, apply one step of Newton's method
    to $f(\xbf) = (x_1+1)^2 + (x_2+3)^2 + 4$.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Answer.} At $\xbf^{(1)} = [0,\,0]^\top$:
    \[
      \nabla f(\xbf^{(1)}) = \begin{bmatrix}2(0+1)\\2(0+3)\end{bmatrix}
                            = \begin{bmatrix}2\\6\end{bmatrix},
      \qquad
      \Hbf = \begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix} = 2\Ibf.
    \]
    The Newton step is:
    \[
      \xbf^{(2)} = [0,\,0]^\top - (2\Ibf)^{-1}[2,\,6]^\top
                 = [0,\,0]^\top - [1,\,3]^\top = [-1,\,-3]^\top.
    \]
    We have found the global minimum $\xbf^* = [-1,\,-3]^\top$ in a single step.
    This is expected because $f$ is exactly quadratic, so the quadratic
    approximation is perfect everywhere.  The minimum value is $f(\xbf^*) =
    0 + 0 + 4 = 4$.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Exercise 6.7: Advantage of Quasi-Newton over Full Newton}
    \textbf{Question.} What is the advantage of a quasi-Newton method over
    Newton's method?

    \medskip
    \textbf{Answer.} Quasi-Newton methods avoid computing the Hessian matrix
    $\Hbf$ and solving the $n \times n$ linear system $\Hbf\,\mathbf{d} = -\gbf$
    at every iteration.  This is important for three reasons:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Cost per iteration:} solving $\Hbf\,\mathbf{d} = -\gbf$
        requires $O(n^3)$ arithmetic (via LU or Cholesky decomposition); the BFGS
        update costs only $O(n^2)$.
      \item \textbf{Derivative availability:} many practical problems do not have
        analytical second derivatives; automatic differentiation of second
        derivatives can be expensive.
      \item \textbf{Memory:} for $n = 10^6$, the Hessian has $10^{12}$ entries and
        cannot be stored.
    \end{enumerate}
    The trade-off is that quasi-Newton methods achieve superlinear convergence
    rather than the quadratic convergence of full Newton -- still much faster than
    gradient descent.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Exercise 6.2: Newton vs.\ Bisection for Root Finding}
    \textbf{Question.} When finding roots in one dimension, when would we use
    Newton's method instead of the bisection method?

    \medskip
    \textbf{Answer.} Newton's method is preferred when:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item The derivative $f'$ is available and cheap to compute.
      \item High accuracy is needed quickly: Newton converges quadratically (the
        number of correct decimal digits roughly doubles each step), while
        bisection converges only linearly (adding about one bit of precision per
        step).
      \item A good starting point close to the root is available, so the quadratic
        approximation is accurate.
    \end{itemize}
    Bisection is preferred when:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Derivatives are unavailable.
      \item Guaranteed convergence is essential: bisection always converges if the
        function is continuous and the root is bracketed.
      \item Low accuracy is sufficient (bisection is simpler to implement
        reliably).
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Exercise 6.8: When BFGS Update Does Not Exist}
    \textbf{Question.} Give an example where the BFGS update does not exist.
    What would you do?

    \medskip
    \textbf{Answer.} The BFGS update is undefined when $\deltabf^\top\gammabf
    \approx 0$, i.e.\ the inner product of the step vector $\deltabf = \xbf^{(k+1)}
    - \xbf^{(k)}$ and the gradient difference $\gammabf = \gbf^{(k+1)} - \gbf^{(k)}$
    is near zero.  This can happen when:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item The function is nearly flat along the step direction (curvature $\approx 0$).
      \item The step crosses a saddle point (curvature changes sign).
      \item The line search does not enforce the Wolfe curvature condition.
    \end{itemize}
    \textbf{What to do:} simply skip the BFGS update for that iteration and keep
    $\mathbf{Q}^{(k+1)} = \mathbf{Q}^{(k)}$.  This is safe: the approximation
    does not worsen, and subsequent iterations with positive curvature will resume
    updating.  In practice, a threshold $\deltabf^\top\gammabf > \epsilon
    \|\deltabf\|\|\gammabf\|$ (with $\epsilon \approx 10^{-10}$) is used.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Exercise 6.6: Decreasing Sequence That Does Not Converge to Minimum}
    \textbf{Question.} Give a sequence $\{x^{(k)}\}$ with decreasing $f$ that
    does not converge to the minimiser.

    \medskip
    \textbf{Answer.} Consider $f(x) = x^2 - x$ with minimiser $x^* = 1/2$ (where
    $f'(x^*) = 2x^* - 1 = 0$).  The sequence $x^{(k+1)} = x^{(k)}/2$ starting
    from $x^{(1)} = -1$ gives $x^{(k)} \to 0$.  We have $f(x^{(k)}) = (x^{(k)})^2
    - x^{(k)}$ and since $x^{(k)} \in [-1, 0)$ is negative, $-x^{(k)} > 0$, so
    $f$ is decreasing along the sequence.  Yet the sequence converges to $x = 0$,
    not to the minimiser $x^* = 1/2$.  This example illustrates that decreasing
    objective values do not guarantee that the iterates converge to a minimum.
  \end{block}
