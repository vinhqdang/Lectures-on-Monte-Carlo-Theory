\section{4.1 Descent Direction Iteration}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Motivation: What Problem Are We Solving?}

  In this chapter we tackle the central problem of applied optimization:
  find a point $\bx^* \in \R^n$ (read: ``x-star in R-n'', meaning an
  $n$-dimensional real-valued vector) that minimises a scalar objective
  function $f : \R^n \to \R$.

  Real-world functions are typically \emph{non-convex} and possess many
  local minima, so there is no algebraic shortcut that solves everything
  at once.  Instead we use an \emph{iterative} strategy: start from an
  initial guess and repeatedly move the design point in a direction that
  makes $f$ smaller, stopping when progress becomes negligibly small.

  \begin{block}{The Core Idea of Local Descent}
    Local descent methods build a sequence of points
    \[
      \bx^{(0)},\; \bx^{(1)},\; \bx^{(2)},\; \ldots
    \]
    (read: ``x superscript 0, x superscript 1, \ldots'', where the
    superscript is the iteration number) such that
    $f\!\left(\bx^{(k+1)}\right) \le f\!\left(\bx^{(k)}\right)$
    at every step.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\bx^{(k)}$ --- the current design point at iteration $k$.
            This is a vector of $n$ real numbers (e.g.\ the coordinates
            of a point in 2-D space if $n=2$).
      \item $f(\bx^{(k)})$ --- the value of the objective function
            evaluated at the current point; this is a single real number.
      \item $\R^n$ --- the set of all $n$-dimensional real-valued vectors.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  The figure below illustrates how successive iterates trace a path
  downhill across the objective landscape.  Each arrow represents one
  step of the algorithm --- a carefully chosen direction combined with a
  carefully chosen step size.

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\linewidth]{fig_descent_iteration.pdf}
  \end{center}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight}
    The challenge is not whether to move downhill --- that is easy ---
    but \emph{how far} to move (the step size) and in \emph{exactly
    which downhill direction} (the descent direction).  Different answers
    give methods with very different convergence speeds and computational
    costs, as we will see throughout this chapter.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{The Four-Step Template of Every Descent Method}

  Nearly every gradient-based optimization algorithm in use today is a
  specialisation of the following four-step template.  Understanding this
  template deeply makes it straightforward to analyse any new method you
  encounter.

  \begin{block}{Descent Direction Iteration (Algorithm 4.1)}
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Check convergence.}  Evaluate the local gradient
            $\grad f(\bx^{(k)})$ (read: ``nabla f of x-k'', where $\grad$
            is the nabla symbol representing the gradient, i.e.\ the
            vector of all partial derivatives).  If the gradient is
            sufficiently small --- meaning the surface is nearly flat ---
            stop and report $\bx^{(k)}$ as the solution.
      \item \textbf{Choose a descent direction $\bd^{(k)}$.}  The vector
            $\bd^{(k)} \in \R^n$ (read: ``d superscript k'') must point
            in a direction along which $f$ decreases.
      \item \textbf{Choose a step factor $\alpha^{(k)} > 0$.}  The scalar
            $\alpha^{(k)}$ (alpha, pronounced ``al-fa'', the first letter
            of the Greek alphabet) controls how far we move in the chosen
            direction.
      \item \textbf{Update the design point:}
            \[
              \bx^{(k+1)} \;=\; \bx^{(k)} + \alpha^{(k)}\bd^{(k)}.
            \]
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for the Update Rule}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\bx^{(k+1)}$ --- the \emph{new} design point after the update.
      \item $\bx^{(k)}$ --- the \emph{current} design point before the update.
      \item $\alpha^{(k)}$ (alpha) --- step factor (also called step size
            or learning rate in machine learning); a positive scalar that
            scales the length of the step.
      \item $\bd^{(k)}$ (bold d) --- descent direction vector; it encodes
            \emph{which way} to move.
      \item The product $\alpha^{(k)}\bd^{(k)}$ is the actual displacement
            added to the current point.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read This Update Rule}
    Think of $\bx^{(k)}$ as your current location on a hillside.  The
    vector $\bd^{(k)}$ is a compass direction pointing downhill.  The
    scalar $\alpha^{(k)}$ is how many metres you walk in that direction.
    After walking, your new location is $\bx^{(k+1)}$.  The art of
    local descent lies entirely in choosing $\bd^{(k)}$ and $\alpha^{(k)}$
    wisely.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{What Makes a Direction a ``Descent'' Direction?}

  Before we can choose a descent direction we need to know precisely what
  the term means mathematically.  The key tool is the \emph{directional
  derivative}, which measures how fast $f$ changes as we move from
  $\bx$ in direction $\bd$.

  \begin{block}{Definition: Directional Derivative}
    The directional derivative of $f$ at point $\bx$ in direction $\bd$ is
    \[
      \nabla_{\bd} f(\bx) \;=\; \grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd,
    \]
    where $\grad f(\bx)$ (nabla f of x) is the gradient vector --- the
    column vector of all partial derivatives of $f$ evaluated at $\bx$ ---
    and the superscript ${}^\top$ denotes the transpose (turning a column
    vector into a row vector so that the dot product is well-defined).
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\nabla_{\bd} f(\bx)$ --- the directional derivative of $f$ at
            $\bx$ along direction $\bd$; this is a single real number.
      \item $\grad f(\bx)$ --- the gradient of $f$ at $\bx$; an
            $n$-dimensional column vector.
      \item ${}^\top$ --- transpose operator.
      \item $\grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd$ --- the dot product (inner product)
            of the gradient with the direction vector; geometrically it
            equals $\|\grad f\|\,\|\bd\|\cos\theta$ (theta, pronounced
            ``thay-ta''), where $\theta$ is the angle between the two
            vectors.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Definition: Descent Direction}
    A vector $\bd \in \R^n$ is called a \emph{descent direction} at $\bx$
    if and only if
    \[
      \grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd \;<\; 0.
    \]
    This condition means the directional derivative is \emph{negative},
    so moving any sufficiently small positive distance $\alpha$ along
    $\bd$ from $\bx$ will decrease the value of $f$.
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: Verifying a Descent Direction}
    Let $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + x_2^2$ (a simple bowl).  At the point
    $\bx = [3, 4]^{\top}$ the gradient is
    \[
      \grad f(\bx) = [2x_1,\; 2x_2]^{\top} = [6,\; 8]^{\top}.
    \]
    Is the direction $\bd = [-1, -1]^{\top}$ a descent direction?
    \[
      \grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd
      = [6, 8] \cdot [-1, -1]
      = 6\times(-1) + 8\times(-1)
      = -14 \;<\; 0. \quad \checkmark
    \]
    Yes --- moving in the $[-1,-1]$ direction from $[3,4]$ will
    decrease $f$.

    The negative gradient direction $-\grad f(\bx) = [-6,-8]^{\top}$
    is also a descent direction (and in fact is the direction of
    \emph{steepest} descent):
    \[
      \grad f(\bx)^{\top}(-\grad f(\bx))
      = -\|\grad f(\bx)\|^2 = -(6^2+8^2) = -100 < 0. \quad \checkmark
    \]
  \end{exampleblock}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight}
    The gradient $\grad f(\bx)$ points in the direction of \emph{steepest
    ascent} (uphill).  Therefore the \emph{negative} gradient
    $-\grad f(\bx)$ always points in the direction of steepest descent
    (downhill).  This is the basis of gradient descent, the most widely
    used optimisation method in machine learning.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{block}{Three Important Descent Directions}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Steepest descent:} $\bd = -\grad f(\bx)$.  Always a
            descent direction; easy to compute; may converge slowly.
      \item \textbf{Newton direction:} $\bd = -\bH^{-1}\grad f(\bx)$,
            where $\bH$ (bold H) is the Hessian matrix of second
            derivatives.  Exploits curvature; converges much faster near
            the minimum but requires computing and inverting $\bH$.
      \item \textbf{Quasi-Newton directions:} approximate $\bH^{-1}$
            cheaply from gradient differences.  A practical middle ground
            (covered in later chapters).
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

\end{frame}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[fragile]{Algorithm 4.1 in Python: Generic Descent Iteration}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode]
import numpy as np

def descent_direction_iteration(f, grad_f, x0, direction_fn,
                                 step_fn, max_iter=1000, tol=1e-6):
    """
    Generic descent direction iteration.
    Args:
        f          : objective function  f(x) -> float
        grad_f     : gradient function   grad_f(x) -> ndarray
        x0         : initial design point (ndarray)
        direction_fn: d = direction_fn(x, g) -> descent direction
        step_fn    : alpha = step_fn(f, grad_f, x, d) -> step factor
    Returns:
        x       : converged design point
        history : list of all iterates
    """
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    history = [x.copy()]
    for k in range(max_iter):
        g = grad_f(x)
        if np.linalg.norm(g) < tol:   # gradient-magnitude termination
            break
        d = direction_fn(x, g)         # choose descent direction
        alpha = step_fn(f, grad_f, x, d)  # choose step factor
        x = x + alpha * d              # update rule: x^(k+1) = x^(k) + alpha*d
        history.append(x.copy())
    return x, history
\end{lstlisting}

\medskip
The function takes \texttt{direction\_fn} and \texttt{step\_fn} as
arguments, making it a \emph{template}: swapping in different choices
for these two functions gives steepest descent, Newton's method,
quasi-Newton methods, and so on.

\end{frame}

\section{4.2 Step Factors}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Step Factors: How Far Should We Move?}

  Once we have chosen a descent direction $\bd^{(k)}$, the next decision
  is how far to walk along it.  The \emph{step factor} $\alpha^{(k)}$
  (alpha, the first Greek letter, pronounced ``al-fa'') is a positive
  scalar multiplying the direction vector.  A step factor that is too
  large causes the algorithm to overshoot and may even diverge; one that
  is too small wastes iterations and slows convergence dramatically.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Fixed Step Factor}
        The simplest strategy is to use the same value $\alpha_0$ at
        every iteration:
        \[
          \alpha^{(k)} = \alpha_0 \quad \text{for all } k.
        \]
        Here $\alpha_0$ (alpha sub zero) is a constant chosen before
        the algorithm starts.  This approach is easy to implement but
        requires careful tuning: if $\alpha_0$ is too large the iterates
        diverge; if it is too small the method crawls.
      \end{block}

      \vspace{0.4em}
      A smarter strategy is to \emph{decrease} the step factor over time.
      Early in the search, when we are far from the minimum, large steps
      help us explore.  Later, when we are close, small steps allow
      fine-tuned convergence without overshooting.  The figure on the
      right shows several common decay schedules.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_step_factor_decay.pdf}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Exponential Decay (Eq.\ 4.2)}
    \[
      \alpha^{(k)} \;=\; \gamma^k \alpha^{(0)},
      \qquad 0 < \gamma < 1.
    \]
    At each iteration the step factor is multiplied by $\gamma$ (gamma,
    the third Greek letter, pronounced ``gam-a''), so after $k$ steps it
    has been shrunk by a factor of $\gamma^k$.  Since $0 < \gamma < 1$,
    this converges to zero exponentially fast.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for Exponential Decay}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\alpha^{(0)}$ (alpha superscript 0) --- the initial step
            factor, i.e.\ the step size used on the very first iteration.
      \item $\gamma$ (gamma) --- the decay rate, a number strictly between
            0 and 1, e.g.\ $\gamma = 0.9$ means each step is 90\% of
            the previous one.
      \item $\gamma^k$ (gamma to the power k) --- the cumulative
            shrinkage after $k$ iterations.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read This: Total Distance Bound}
    If the direction vectors have bounded length ($\|\bd^{(k)}\| \le
    \delta$, delta pronounced ``del-ta''), the total distance travelled
    by the algorithm is at most
    \[
      \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \alpha^{(k)} \|\bd^{(k)}\|
      \;\le\;
      \delta\, \alpha^{(0)} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k
      \;=\;
      \frac{\delta\, \alpha^{(0)}}{1 - \gamma}.
    \]
    Here $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}$ (Sigma, the capital Greek letter for
    ``sum'') sums over all iterations from $k=0$ to infinity.  This
    geometric series converges because $\gamma < 1$, guaranteeing that
    the algorithm does not wander off to infinity even if it runs forever.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Polynomial Decay (Eq.\ 4.4)}
    \[
      \alpha^{(k)} \;=\; \frac{\alpha^{(0)}}{(1 + \beta k)^p},
      \qquad \beta > 0,\; p > 0.
    \]
    Here $\beta$ (beta, pronounced ``bay-ta'') is a scaling constant that
    controls how quickly the denominator grows, and $p$ (p) is the
    polynomial decay exponent.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation for Polynomial Decay}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\beta$ (beta) --- positive constant; larger $\beta$ means
            faster initial decay.
      \item $p$ (p) --- exponent; $p=1$ gives harmonic decay
            ($\alpha^{(k)} \propto 1/k$), $p=2$ gives faster decay.
      \item When $0 < p \le 1$, the sum $\sum_k \alpha^{(k)}$ is
            \emph{unbounded}, meaning the algorithm can in principle
            reach \emph{any} point in space given enough iterations ---
            a property required by stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
            theory to guarantee convergence to a global minimum.
      \item When $p > 1$, the sum is bounded and the algorithm is
            constrained to a finite region.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Clamped Exponential Decay (Eq.\ 4.5)}
    \[
      \alpha^{(k)} \;=\; \max\!\left(\alpha_{\min},\; \gamma^k \alpha^{(0)}\right).
    \]
    Here $\alpha_{\min}$ (alpha sub min) is a floor value that prevents
    the step from shrinking all the way to zero.  The $\max$ operator
    returns whichever of its two arguments is larger, so the step never
    drops below $\alpha_{\min}$.  This avoids \emph{premature stagnation}:
    the algorithm keeps making progress rather than grinding to a halt.
  \end{block}

\end{frame}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[fragile]{Step Factor Decay Schedules in Python}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode]
import numpy as np

def exponential_decay(alpha0, gamma, k):
    """Exponential decay: alpha^(k) = gamma^k * alpha0, 0 < gamma < 1."""
    return gamma**k * alpha0

def polynomial_decay(alpha0, beta, p, k):
    """Polynomial decay: alpha^(k) = alpha0 / (1 + beta*k)^p, beta>0, p>0."""
    return alpha0 / (1.0 + beta * k)**p

def clamped_decay(alpha0, gamma, alpha_min, k):
    """Clamped decay: alpha^(k) = max(alpha_min, gamma^k * alpha0)."""
    return max(alpha_min, gamma**k * alpha0)

# ── Worked numerical example ───────────────────────────────
# f(x) = x^2, initial point x0 = 3.0, exponential decay
def f(x):   return x**2
def df(x):  return 2 * x     # gradient of f(x) = x^2 is f'(x) = 2x

x = 3.0
print(f"k=0: x={x:.4f}, f(x)={f(x):.4f}, grad={df(x):.4f}")
for k in range(10):
    alpha = exponential_decay(alpha0=0.5, gamma=0.85, k=k)
    x = x - alpha * df(x)   # x_{k+1} = x_k - alpha * f'(x_k)
    print(f"k={k+1}: alpha={alpha:.4f}, x={x:.4f}, f(x)={f(x):.6f}")
    if abs(df(x)) < 1e-4:
        print("Converged!")
        break
\end{lstlisting}

\medskip
In the loop, \texttt{x = x - alpha * df(x)} is the steepest descent
update: we subtract $\alpha \cdot f'(x)$ because the gradient $f'(x)$
points \emph{uphill}, so subtracting it moves us \emph{downhill}.

\end{frame}

\section{4.3 Line Search}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Line Search: Finding the Best Step Along a Direction}

  Once we have fixed the descent direction $\bd$, choosing the step
  factor $\alpha^{(k)}$ reduces to a one-dimensional problem: how far
  along the ray $\bx + \alpha\bd$ should we walk?  This is called a
  \emph{line search}.

  \begin{block}{Exact Line Search}
    Exact line search selects the $\alpha$ that minimises $f$ along the
    ray:
    \[
      \alpha^* \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\alpha \,\ge\, 0}
                     f\!\left(\bx + \alpha\bd\right).
    \]
    The notation $\operatorname*{arg\,min}$ (``argument of the minimum'')
    means: find the value of $\alpha$ (alpha) that makes the expression
    $f(\bx + \alpha\bd)$ as small as possible, subject to $\alpha \ge 0$
    (we do not want to step backwards).
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\alpha^*$ (alpha-star) --- the optimal step size; the unique
            minimiser of the one-dimensional function
            $\phi(\alpha) = f(\bx + \alpha\bd)$.
      \item $\phi(\alpha)$ --- a shorthand for the 1-D function we are
            minimising during line search; it depends on the fixed current
            point $\bx$ and direction $\bd$.
      \item The constraint $\alpha \ge 0$ ensures we move in the direction
            $\bd$, not opposite to it.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Exact Line Search for Quadratic Functions}
        For a quadratic objective
        $f(\bx) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bx^{\top}\bH\bx - \bg^{\top}\bx$,
        with steepest descent direction $\bd = -\grad f$,
        the optimal step has a closed form:
        \[
          \alpha^* \;=\; \frac{\bd^{\top}\bd}{\bd^{\top}\bH\bd}.
        \]
        Here $\bH$ (bold H) is the \emph{Hessian matrix} --- the matrix
        of second partial derivatives of $f$ --- and the two dot products
        measure the squared length of $\bd$ and the curvature of $f$
        along $\bd$, respectively.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_exact_line_search.pdf}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Quadratic Step Formula}
    The numerator $\bd^{\top}\bd = \|\bd\|^2$ is the squared length of
    the descent direction.  The denominator $\bd^{\top}\bH\bd$ measures
    how much the function curves along $\bd$ (the curvature).  When the
    curvature is high (the function is sharply curved), the optimal step
    is small.  When the curvature is low (the function is nearly flat),
    the optimal step is large.  This makes intuitive sense: on a flat
    slope you should take big steps; on a tight curve you should take
    small ones to avoid overshooting.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{exampleblock}{Worked Example: Exact Line Search for a Quadratic}
    Let $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + x_1 x_2 + x_2^2$.  Starting at
    $\bx = [3, 2]^{\top}$:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Gradient: $\grad f = [2x_1 + x_2,\; x_1 + 2x_2]^{\top}
            = [8, 7]^{\top}$.
      \item Descent direction: $\bd = -\grad f = [-8, -7]^{\top}$.
      \item Hessian: $\bH = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$.
      \item Numerator: $\bd^{\top}\bd = 64 + 49 = 113$.
      \item Denominator: $\bd^{\top}\bH\bd
            = [-8,-7]\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}[-8,-7]^{\top}
            = [-8,-7][-23,-22]^{\top} = 184 + 154 = 338$.
      \item Optimal step: $\alpha^* = 113/338 \approx 0.334$.
      \item New point: $\bx' = [3,2] + 0.334\times[-8,-7]
            \approx [0.33, -0.34]$.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{exampleblock}

\end{frame}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[fragile]{Exact Line Search in Python (using SciPy)}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode]
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

def exact_line_search(f, x, d):
    """
    Minimize the 1-D function phi(alpha) = f(x + alpha * d)
    over alpha >= 0.  Returns optimal step size alpha*.
    """
    phi = lambda alpha: f(x + alpha * d)
    result = minimize_scalar(phi, bounds=(0, 10), method='bounded')
    return result.x

# ── Example: f(x1, x2) = x1^2 + x1*x2 + x2^2 ─────────────
def f(x):
    return x[0]**2 + x[0]*x[1] + x[1]**2

def grad_f(x):
    return np.array([2*x[0] + x[1],   # partial derivative w.r.t. x1
                     x[0] + 2*x[1]])  # partial derivative w.r.t. x2

x = np.array([3.0, 2.0])
d = -grad_f(x)                   # steepest descent direction
alpha_star = exact_line_search(f, x, d)
x_new = x + alpha_star * d

print(f"Gradient at x:   {grad_f(x)}")
print(f"Descent dir d:   {d}")
print(f"Optimal alpha*:  {alpha_star:.6f}")
print(f"New point x':    {x_new}")
print(f"f(x_new):        {f(x_new):.6f}")
\end{lstlisting}

\medskip
The lambda \texttt{phi} converts the $n$-dimensional function $f$ into
a 1-D function of $\alpha$ alone.  \texttt{minimize\_scalar} then finds
the minimum of this 1-D function efficiently using golden-section search.

\end{frame}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Backtracking Line Search: A Cheaper Alternative}

  Exact line search guarantees the best possible step in a given
  direction, but evaluating $f$ many times along the ray is expensive
  when $f$ is costly (e.g.\ running a simulation).  \emph{Backtracking
  line search} is a much cheaper alternative that merely asks: is this
  step \emph{good enough}?

  The ``good enough'' criterion is the \emph{Armijo condition} (also
  called the first Wolfe condition or the sufficient decrease condition).
  It requires that the function value drops by at least a fixed fraction
  of what a linear model would predict:

  \begin{block}{Armijo (Sufficient Decrease) Condition}
    \[
      f(\bx + \alpha\bd)
      \;\le\;
      f(\bx) + \beta\,\alpha\,\grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd.
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $f(\bx + \alpha\bd)$ --- the actual function value at the
            proposed new point.
      \item $f(\bx) + \beta\,\alpha\,\grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd$ --- a linear
            prediction of how much $f$ should decrease, scaled by $\beta$
            (beta, the second Greek letter, pronounced ``bay-ta'').
      \item $\beta$ (beta) --- a small positive constant, typically
            $\beta = 10^{-4}$.  It sets how much decrease we require:
            $\beta = 0$ would accept any decrease; $\beta$ close to 1
            would require almost the full predicted decrease.
      \item $\grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd$ --- the directional derivative at
            $\bx$ along $\bd$; this is negative (since $\bd$ is a descent
            direction), so the right-hand side is \emph{less than}
            $f(\bx)$.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Armijo Condition}
    The right-hand side of the inequality is a line that starts at
    $f(\bx)$ when $\alpha = 0$ and slopes downward (since
    $\grad f^{\top}\bd < 0$).  The slope is scaled by $\beta < 1$, so
    it decreases more slowly than the tangent to $\phi(\alpha) = f(\bx
    + \alpha\bd)$.  The condition says: the actual function value must
    lie \emph{below} this gentle downward-sloping line.  Intuitively,
    we require a \emph{sufficient} decrease --- not just any decrease.
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \textbf{The backtracking algorithm} starts with a large trial step
      $\alpha$ (often $\alpha = 1$) and repeatedly reduces it by a
      factor $p \in (0,1)$ (p, the reduction factor, typically $p = 0.5$)
      until the Armijo condition is satisfied.

      \begin{block}{Backtracking Algorithm}
        \begin{enumerate}
          \item Compute $y = f(\bx)$ and $\bg = \grad f(\bx)$.
          \item \textbf{While} $f(\bx + \alpha\bd) > y + \beta\alpha(\bg^{\top}\bd)$:
                \[ \alpha \;\leftarrow\; p\,\alpha. \]
          \item Return $\alpha$.
        \end{enumerate}
      \end{block}

      The loop terminates because $\bd$ is a descent direction
      ($\bg^{\top}\bd < 0$), so for small enough $\alpha$ the condition
      must eventually be met.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_sufficient_decrease.pdf}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Numerical Example: Backtracking Line Search (Example 4.2)}

  Let us trace through the backtracking algorithm step by step on a
  concrete example so there is no ambiguity about how the computation
  proceeds.

  \begin{block}{Setup}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Objective: $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + x_1 x_2 + x_2^2$.
      \item Current point: $\bx = [1, 2]^{\top}$.
      \item Descent direction: $\bd = [-1, -1]^{\top}$.
      \item Parameters: $\alpha_{\max} = 10$, reduction factor $p = 0.5$,
            Armijo parameter $\beta = 10^{-4}$.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  First, compute the quantities that stay constant throughout the search:
  \begin{align*}
    f(\bx) &= 1^2 + 1\cdot 2 + 2^2 = 1 + 2 + 4 = 7, \\
    \grad f(\bx) &= [2x_1 + x_2,\; x_1 + 2x_2]^{\top}
                  = [2\cdot1+2,\; 1+2\cdot2]^{\top}
                  = [4, 5]^{\top}, \\
    \bg^{\top}\bd &= [4, 5]\cdot[-1,-1] = -4 - 5 = -9.
  \end{align*}
  The threshold at step size $\alpha$ is:
  \[
    y + \beta\alpha(\bg^{\top}\bd)
    = 7 + 10^{-4}\cdot\alpha\cdot(-9)
    = 7 - 0.0009\alpha.
  \]

  \framebreak

  \begin{exampleblock}{Iteration 1: $\alpha = 10$}
    \begin{align*}
      \bx + \alpha\bd &= [1,2] + 10\cdot[-1,-1] = [-9,-8], \\
      f([-9,-8]) &= 81 + 72 + 64 = 217, \\
      \text{Threshold} &= 7 - 0.009 = 6.991.\\
      217 &> 6.991 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{FAIL.} \quad \alpha \leftarrow 5.
    \end{align*}
  \end{exampleblock}

  \begin{exampleblock}{Iteration 2: $\alpha = 5$}
    \begin{align*}
      \bx + \alpha\bd &= [1,2] + 5\cdot[-1,-1] = [-4,-3], \\
      f([-4,-3]) &= 16 + 12 + 9 = 37, \\
      \text{Threshold} &= 7 - 0.0045 = 6.9955.\\
      37 &> 6.9955 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{FAIL.} \quad \alpha \leftarrow 2.5.
    \end{align*}
  \end{exampleblock}

  \begin{exampleblock}{Iteration 3: $\alpha = 2.5$}
    \begin{align*}
      \bx + \alpha\bd &= [1,2] + 2.5\cdot[-1,-1] = [-1.5,-0.5], \\
      f([-1.5,-0.5]) &= 2.25 + 0.75 + 0.25 = 3.25, \\
      \text{Threshold} &= 7 - 0.00225 = 6.99775.\\
      3.25 &\le 6.998 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \textbf{ACCEPT.}\quad \alpha^* = 2.5.
    \end{align*}
  \end{exampleblock}

  \begin{alertblock}{Summary}
    After only 3 halvings the algorithm accepts $\alpha^* = 2.5$,
    moving the design point from $[1,2]$ to $[-1.5,-0.5]$ and reducing
    $f$ from 7 to 3.25.  Three function evaluations --- much cheaper
    than a full 1-D optimisation.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

import numpy as np

def backtracking_line_search(f, grad_f, x, d,
                              alpha=1.0, p=0.5, beta=1e-4):
    """
    Backtracking line search enforcing the Armijo condition.
    Reduces alpha by factor p until sufficient decrease is satisfied.

    Parameters
    ----------
    f       : objective function
    grad_f  : gradient function
    x       : current design point
    d       : descent direction (must satisfy grad_f(x) @ d < 0)
    alpha   : initial (maximum) step size (default 1.0)
    p       : reduction factor in (0, 1) (default 0.5)
    beta    : Armijo parameter in (0, 1) (default 1e-4)
    """
    y     = f(x)               # current function value
    g     = grad_f(x)
    slope = g @ d              # directional derivative; must be < 0

    while f(x + alpha * d) > y + beta * alpha * slope:
        alpha *= p             # shrink step by factor p

    return alpha

def grad_f(x): return np.array([2*x[0]+x[1], x[0]+2*x[1]])

x = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
d = np.array([-1.0, -1.0])

print(f"Accepted alpha = {alpha}")          # => 2.5
print(f"New point:     {x + alpha * d}")   # => [-1.5, -0.5]
print(f"f(new point):  {f(x + alpha*d):.4f}")  # => 3.25

\section{4.4 Approximate Line Search --- Wolfe Conditions}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Why Backtracking Alone Is Not Enough}

  The Armijo (backtracking) condition prevents steps that are too
  \emph{large} --- it ensures we decrease $f$ by at least a fraction of
  the predicted decrease.  However, it places \emph{no lower bound} on
  the step size: a step of $\alpha = 10^{-100}$ would satisfy the Armijo
  condition trivially (any tiny decrease counts), yet it would make no
  practical progress.

  We therefore need a \emph{second condition} that prevents steps from
  being too small.  The \emph{Wolfe conditions} combine the Armijo
  condition with a curvature condition to give a pair of requirements
  that together guarantee the step is both ``not too big'' and ``not too
  small''.

  \begin{block}{The Two Wolfe Conditions}
    \textbf{First Wolfe Condition (Armijo / sufficient decrease):}
    \[
      f(\bx + \alpha\bd)
      \;\le\;
      f(\bx) + \beta\,\alpha\,\grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd.
    \]
    \textbf{Second Wolfe Condition (curvature condition):}
    \[
      \grad f(\bx + \alpha\bd)^{\top}\bd
      \;\ge\;
      \sigma\,\grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd.
    \]
    The parameters satisfy $0 < \beta < \sigma < 1$, with typical
    values $\beta = 10^{-4}$ and $\sigma = 0.9$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\sigma$ (sigma, the eighteenth Greek letter, pronounced
            ``sig-ma'') --- the curvature parameter; controls how tight
            the curvature condition is.
      \item $\grad f(\bx + \alpha\bd)^{\top}\bd$ --- the directional
            derivative at the \emph{new} point $\bx + \alpha\bd$ along
            direction $\bd$; it is the slope of $\phi(\alpha)$ at the
            proposed step.
      \item $\grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd$ --- the directional derivative at
            the \emph{current} point; it is the initial slope (negative,
            since $\bd$ is a descent direction).
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Curvature Condition}
    The second Wolfe condition says that the slope of $\phi(\alpha)$
    at the accepted step must be \emph{less negative} than $\sigma$
    times the initial slope.  In other words, the function must have
    ``flattened out'' somewhat by the time we stop.  If the slope is
    still very steep (very negative) at the accepted point, the step is
    too short and we could profitably have gone further.  Setting $\sigma$
    close to 1 is lenient (allows nearly any step); setting $\sigma$
    close to $\beta$ is strict (nearly exact line search).
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Strong Wolfe Conditions}
        The \emph{strong} version of the curvature condition uses an
        absolute value:
        \[
          \left|\grad f(\bx+\alpha\bd)^{\top}\bd\right|
          \;\le\;
          \sigma\left|\grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd\right|.
        \]
        This prevents accepting a step where the slope has merely
        changed sign (passed through a local maximum of $\phi$),
        which the weak condition would allow.  The strong Wolfe
        conditions are required by conjugate gradient and quasi-Newton
        methods (e.g.\ L-BFGS) to guarantee convergence.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_curvature_condition.pdf}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{The Strong Backtracking Algorithm}

  Finding a step that satisfies the \emph{strong} Wolfe conditions
  requires a more sophisticated procedure than simple backtracking.
  The standard approach proceeds in two phases.

  \begin{block}{Phase 1 --- Bracket Phase: Find a Bracketing Interval}
    Start from $\alpha = 0$ and double the trial step until one of
    three \emph{bracketing conditions} is triggered, which guarantees
    that a qualifying step exists somewhere in the bracket
    $[\alpha_{\rm prev},\, \alpha]$:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item $f(\bx+\alpha\bd) > f(\bx) + \beta\alpha\,g_0$
            (Armijo violated --- overshot).
      \item $f(\bx+\alpha\bd) \ge f_{\rm prev}$
            (function increased --- passed a local minimum).
      \item $\grad f(\bx+\alpha\bd)^{\top}\bd \ge 0$
            (gradient changed sign --- passed through a minimum of
            $\phi$).
    \end{enumerate}
    Here $g_0 = \grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd$ (the initial directional
    derivative, which is negative) and $f_{\rm prev}$ is the function
    value at the previous trial step.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Phase 2 --- Zoom Phase: Narrow Down the Bracket}
    With the bracket $[\alpha_{\rm lo}, \alpha_{\rm hi}]$ in hand,
    bisect the interval repeatedly.  At each bisection step, the
    bracket is updated so that it always contains a qualifying step.
    This is guaranteed to terminate with a step satisfying the strong
    Wolfe conditions.
  \end{block}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_strong_backtracking.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \small
      The left panel shows the bracket phase doubling $\alpha$ until a
      bracketing condition fires.  The right panel shows the zoom phase
      bisecting the bracket.  Each bisection halves the interval,
      giving rapid convergence to a qualifying step.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Bracketing Guarantee (Theorem)}
    An interval $[0, a]$ is guaranteed to contain a step satisfying
    the strong Wolfe conditions whenever any of the following holds:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item $f(\bx+a\bd) \ge f(\bx)$ \quad (function has not decreased).
      \item $f(\bx+a\bd) > f(\bx) + \beta\,a\,\nabla_{\bd}f(\bx)$
            \quad (Armijo violated).
      \item $\nabla_{\bd}f(\bx+a\bd) \ge 0$
            \quad (slope has become non-negative).
    \end{enumerate}
    This theorem underpins the correctness of the strong backtracking
    algorithm: as soon as any of these conditions fires, we know a
    good step must lie in the current bracket.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Key Insight}
    The Wolfe conditions are the gold standard for line searches in
    quasi-Newton methods such as BFGS and L-BFGS.  Satisfying them
    guarantees that the Hessian approximation matrices remain positive
    definite (so the Newton-like direction remains a descent direction),
    a property that backtracking alone cannot guarantee.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Strong Backtracking Line Search in Python}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode]
import numpy as np

def strong_backtracking(f, grad_f, x, d,
                         alpha=1.0, beta=1e-4, sigma=0.1):
    """Strong backtracking: finds alpha satisfying strong Wolfe conditions.

    Phase 1 (bracket): expand alpha until a bracketing condition fires.
    Phase 2 (zoom):    bisect the bracket until strong Wolfe is met.
    """
    y0 = f(x)
    g0 = grad_f(x) @ d          # initial directional derivative (< 0)
    y_prev, alpha_prev = float('nan'), 0.0
    alpha_lo = alpha_hi = float('nan')

    # ── Phase 1: Bracket ───────────────────────────────────
    while True:
        y = f(x + alpha * d)
        # Bracketing condition: Armijo violated or function increased
        if y > y0 + beta * alpha * g0 or \
           (not np.isnan(y_prev) and y >= y_prev):
            alpha_lo, alpha_hi = alpha_prev, alpha
            break
        g = grad_f(x + alpha * d) @ d
        if abs(g) <= -sigma * g0:    # strong Wolfe curvature satisfied
            return alpha
        if g >= 0:                   # slope non-negative: bracket found
            alpha_lo, alpha_hi = alpha, alpha_prev
            break
        y_prev, alpha_prev, alpha = y, alpha, 2 * alpha  # double step

    # ── Phase 2: Zoom (bisection) ──────────────────────────
    y_lo = f(x + alpha_lo * d)
    while True:
        alpha = (alpha_lo + alpha_hi) / 2.0   # bisect
        y = f(x + alpha * d)
        if y > y0 + beta * alpha * g0 or y >= y_lo:
            alpha_hi = alpha              # upper end of bracket narrows
        else:
            g = grad_f(x + alpha * d) @ d
            if abs(g) <= -sigma * g0:
                return alpha              # strong Wolfe satisfied
            if g * (alpha_hi - alpha_lo) >= 0:
                alpha_hi = alpha_lo
            alpha_lo, y_lo = alpha, y
\end{lstlisting}

\end{frame}

\section{4.5 Trust Region Methods}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Trust Region: A Different Philosophy}

  Line search methods first choose a direction and then determine how
  far to step in that direction.  Trust region methods flip this
  strategy: they choose \emph{both} the direction and the step length
  simultaneously by solving a constrained subproblem within a local
  region where the model is trusted to be accurate.

  \begin{block}{The Core Idea}
    At the current point $\bx^{(k)}$, build a simple (quadratic) model
    $\hat{f}$ of the objective function, then minimise that model within
    a ball of radius $\delta$ (delta, the fourth Greek letter, pronounced
    ``del-ta'') centred at $\bx^{(k)}$:
    \[
      \hat{f}(\bx') \;\approx\;
      f(\bx^{(k)})
      + \grad f(\bx^{(k)})^{\top}(\bx' - \bx^{(k)})
      + \tfrac{1}{2}(\bx' - \bx^{(k)})^{\top}\bH(\bx' - \bx^{(k)}).
    \]
    The ball $\{\bx' : \|\bx' - \bx^{(k)}\| \le \delta\}$ is the
    \emph{trust region} --- the region within which we trust the model
    to be a good approximation to $f$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\bx'$ (x-prime) --- the candidate next point (the variable
            we optimise over in the subproblem).
      \item $\hat{f}(\bx')$ (f-hat of x-prime) --- the quadratic model
            approximating $f$ near $\bx^{(k)}$.
      \item $\bH$ (bold H) --- the Hessian matrix (matrix of second
            partial derivatives) of $f$ at $\bx^{(k)}$; or a
            quasi-Newton approximation thereof.
      \item $\delta$ (delta) --- the trust region radius; a positive
            scalar that is adapted at each iteration.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_trust_region.pdf}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      The circle in the figure is the trust region boundary.  Inside the
      circle the quadratic model (smooth curved surface) is a reliable
      approximation to the true function.  The algorithm finds the
      minimum of the model inside the circle and uses that as the next
      iterate.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Trust Region Subproblem (Eq.\ 4.14)}
    \[
      \bx^{(k+1)}
      = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\bx'}
      \Bigl[
        f(\bx^{(k)})
        + \grad f(\bx^{(k)})^{\top}(\bx'-\bx^{(k)})
        + \tfrac{1}{2}(\bx'-\bx^{(k)})^{\top}\bH(\bx'-\bx^{(k)})
      \Bigr]
      \quad
      \text{subject to } \|\bx' - \bx^{(k)}\| \le \delta.
    \]
    This is a \emph{quadratic program with a quadratic constraint} (a
    spherical constraint).  It can be solved efficiently in $O(n^3)$
    time using eigenvalue methods or iterative solvers.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{Contrast with Line Search}
    Line search: pick direction $\bd$, then optimise $f(\bx + \alpha\bd)$
    over $\alpha$.  Trust region: pick the step $\bx' - \bx^{(k)}$ that
    minimises the model, subject to the step length not exceeding $\delta$.
    The direction and step size emerge \emph{together} from the
    subproblem.  When $\delta$ is large the unconstrained Newton step
    may be taken; when $\delta$ is small the gradient descent step is
    taken.  The trust region radius thus interpolates between gradient
    descent and Newton's method automatically.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Adapting the Trust Region Radius}

  The trust region radius $\delta$ must be managed carefully: too small
  and we make tiny steps; too large and the quadratic model becomes
  inaccurate.  The algorithm adapts $\delta$ by measuring how well the
  model predicted the actual decrease.

  \begin{block}{Improvement Ratio $\eta$ (Eq.\ 4.15)}
    \[
      \eta \;=\;
      \frac{f(\bx^{(k)}) - f(\bx')}
           {f(\bx^{(k)}) - \hat{f}(\bx')}
      \;=\;
      \frac{\Delta f_{\rm actual}}{\Delta f_{\rm predicted}}.
    \]
    This ratio (eta, the seventh Greek letter, pronounced ``ay-ta'')
    compares the actual decrease in $f$ to the decrease predicted by
    the quadratic model.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Notation}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\eta$ (eta) --- the improvement ratio; a dimensionless
            number comparing model quality to actual progress.
      \item $f(\bx^{(k)}) - f(\bx')$ --- the \emph{actual} decrease in
            the objective function; positive means the step helped.
      \item $f(\bx^{(k)}) - \hat{f}(\bx')$ --- the \emph{predicted}
            decrease according to the quadratic model; this is always
            positive (the model always predicts improvement from its
            own minimum).
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Improvement Ratio}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $\eta \approx 1$: the model is excellent; the actual and
            predicted decreases nearly match.  Expand the trust region
            ($\delta \leftarrow \gamma_2 \delta$, gamma-two times
            delta) so future steps can be bolder.
      \item $\eta_1 \le \eta \le \eta_2$: the model is adequate; keep
            $\delta$ unchanged.
      \item $\eta < \eta_1$: the model is poor (or the actual decrease
            was far less than predicted).  Shrink the trust region
            ($\delta \leftarrow \gamma_1 \delta$) and reject the step.
      \item $\eta < 0$: the function actually \emph{increased} (bad
            step).  Reject the step and shrink $\delta$ substantially.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{alertblock}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Trust Region Update Rules}
        Typical parameters: $\eta_1 = 0.25$, $\eta_2 = 0.75$,
        $\gamma_1 = 0.5$, $\gamma_2 = 2.0$.
        \begin{itemize}
          \item If $\eta < \eta_1$: \textbf{reject} the step; set
                $\delta \leftarrow \gamma_1 \delta$.
          \item If $\eta_1 \le \eta \le \eta_2$: \textbf{accept} the
                step; keep $\delta$.
          \item If $\eta > \eta_2$: \textbf{accept} the step; set
                $\delta \leftarrow \gamma_2 \delta$.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_trust_region_ratio.pdf}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Elliptical Trust Region (Eq.\ 4.16)}
    The circular trust region can be generalised to an \emph{elliptical}
    trust region:
    \[
      \|\bx - \bx_0\|_{\bE}^2
      \;=\;
      (\bx - \bx_0)^{\top}\bE\,(\bx - \bx_0)
      \;\le\; 1,
    \]
    where $\bE$ (bold E) is a positive-definite matrix that defines the
    shape of the ellipse.  By elongating the trust region along
    directions of high curvature confidence, an elliptical region can
    model the actual function more accurately than a sphere.
  \end{block}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Notation}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item $\bE$ --- the ellipse-shaping matrix; a positive-definite
                symmetric matrix.  When $\bE = \mathbf{I}$ (the identity
                matrix) the ellipse is a circle.
          \item $\|\bx\|_{\bE}^2 = \bx^{\top}\bE\bx$ --- the
                $\bE$-weighted norm (``energy norm''); it measures length
                in a geometry distorted by $\bE$.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_elliptical_trust_region.pdf}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Trust Region Descent in Python (using SciPy)}

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def trust_region_step(grad, hess, delta):
    """
    Solve the circular trust region subproblem:
       min  grad @ s + 0.5 * s @ hess @ s
       s.t. ||s|| <= delta
    Returns the step vector s.
    """
    n = len(grad)
    def model(s):       return grad @ s + 0.5 * s @ hess @ s
    def model_grad(s):  return grad + hess @ s
    constraints = {'type': 'ineq',
                   'fun': lambda s: delta - np.linalg.norm(s)}
    res = minimize(model, np.zeros(n), jac=model_grad,
                   constraints=constraints, method='SLSQP')
    return res.x

def trust_region_descent(f, grad_f, hess_f, x0, delta=1.0,
                          eta1=0.25, eta2=0.75,
                          gamma1=0.5, gamma2=2.0, max_iter=100):
    """Trust region method with adaptive radius delta."""
    x = x0.copy().astype(float)
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        g = grad_f(x)
        H = hess_f(x)
        if np.linalg.norm(g) < 1e-6:
            break
        s = trust_region_step(g, H, delta)       # solve subproblem
        # Improvement ratio eta = actual_decrease / predicted_decrease
        actual    = f(x) - f(x + s)
        predicted = -(g @ s + 0.5 * s @ H @ s)  # model decrease (>0)
        eta = actual / (predicted + 1e-14)
        if eta > eta1:
            x = x + s                    # accept step
        # Adapt trust region radius
        if eta < eta1:
            delta *= gamma1              # poor model: shrink
        elif eta > eta2:
            delta *= gamma2              # good model: expand
    return x
\end{lstlisting}

\end{frame}

\section{4.6 Termination Conditions}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{When Should We Stop Iterating?}

  Every iterative algorithm needs a stopping criterion.  Stopping too
  early wastes the accuracy we could have achieved; stopping too late
  wastes computation (and sometimes causes numerical instability as
  we try to squeeze out the last few decimal places).

  There are four standard termination conditions for descent methods.
  In practice, good implementations check \emph{all four} and stop as
  soon as \emph{any} of them is triggered.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Condition 1: Maximum Iterations (Eq.\ 4.17)}
        \[
          k \;>\; k_{\max}.
        \]
        Here $k$ (k) is the current iteration number and $k_{\max}$
        (k-sub-max) is a user-specified upper limit, e.g.\
        $k_{\max} = 10\,000$.  This is a \emph{safety net}: the
        algorithm always terminates in finite time regardless of
        whether it has converged.
      \end{block}

      \begin{block}{Condition 2: Absolute Improvement (Eq.\ 4.18)}
        \[
          f(\bx^{(k)}) - f(\bx^{(k+1)}) \;<\; \epsilon_a.
        \]
        Here $\epsilon_a$ (epsilon sub a, epsilon pronounced
        ``ep-si-lon'') is a small positive threshold, e.g.\
        $\epsilon_a = 10^{-8}$.  The condition fires when the function
        value barely changes from one iteration to the next, suggesting
        we are near a minimum.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_termination.pdf}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Condition 3: Relative Improvement (Eq.\ 4.19)}
    \[
      f(\bx^{(k)}) - f(\bx^{(k+1)})
      \;<\;
      \epsilon_r \left|f(\bx^{(k)})\right|.
    \]
    Here $\epsilon_r$ (epsilon sub r) is the relative tolerance,
    e.g.\ $\epsilon_r = 10^{-6}$.  The condition fires when the
    improvement is small \emph{relative to the current function value}.
    This is important when $f$ takes very large values: an absolute
    improvement of $10^{-8}$ on a function with value $10^6$ may
    well be worth continuing.  The relative criterion is
    \emph{scale-invariant}: it automatically adapts to the magnitude
    of $f$.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Condition 4: Gradient Magnitude (Eq.\ 4.20)}
    \[
      \norm{\grad f(\bx^{(k+1)})} \;<\; \epsilon_g.
    \]
    Here $\epsilon_g$ (epsilon sub g) is the gradient tolerance, e.g.\
    $\epsilon_g = 10^{-6}$, and $\norm{\cdot}$ denotes the Euclidean
    norm (length of a vector).  This condition is the most theoretically
    principled: at a local minimum the gradient is exactly zero, so a
    small gradient is direct evidence of convergence.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{How to Read the Gradient Condition}
    The gradient $\grad f(\bx)$ is the slope of the surface.  When the
    slope is nearly zero the surface is nearly flat, meaning we are
    near a critical point (possibly a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle
    point).  Combined with the fact that $f$ has been decreasing at
    each step, a small gradient strongly suggests we have reached a
    local minimum.
  \end{alertblock}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Why Multiple Conditions?}
    Each individual condition has a failure mode:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \emph{Max iterations only:} may stop too early (before
            convergence) or too late (after convergence, wasting time).
      \item \emph{Gradient only:} fails on flat functions or very
            slowly converging sequences where $\|\grad f\|$ stays
            above $\epsilon_g$ for a very long time.
      \item \emph{Absolute improvement only:} fails when $f$ is very
            large (a tiny relative change gives a large absolute change)
            or when the algorithm makes slow but steady progress.
      \item \emph{Relative improvement only:} can terminate too early
            when $f$ is near zero (any improvement looks small relative
            to a tiny value).
    \end{itemize}
    Using all four conditions simultaneously handles all failure modes.
    A robust implementation typically uses: gradient + relative improvement
    + max iterations as the primary safety net.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Typical Parameter Values}
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
      \toprule
      Condition        & Symbol        & Typical value \\
      \midrule
      Max iterations   & $k_{\max}$    & $10^3$--$10^5$ \\
      Abs.\ improvement & $\epsilon_a$ & $10^{-8}$ \\
      Rel.\ improvement & $\epsilon_r$ & $10^{-6}$ \\
      Gradient norm    & $\epsilon_g$  & $10^{-6}$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
  \end{block}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Termination Conditions in Python}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode]
import numpy as np

def check_termination(x_prev, x_curr, f_prev, f_curr, grad_curr,
                       k, k_max=10000,
                       eps_a=1e-8, eps_r=1e-6, eps_g=1e-6):
    """
    Check all four standard termination conditions.

    Parameters
    ----------
    x_prev, x_curr  : design points at iterations k and k+1
    f_prev, f_curr  : function values at those points
    grad_curr       : gradient at x_curr
    k               : current iteration number
    k_max           : max iterations (safety net)
    eps_a           : absolute improvement tolerance
    eps_r           : relative improvement tolerance
    eps_g           : gradient-norm tolerance

    Returns
    -------
    (terminated: bool, reason: str)
    """
    # Condition 1: maximum iterations
    if k >= k_max:
        return True, f"max iterations ({k_max}) reached"

    # Condition 2: absolute improvement
    abs_improv = f_prev - f_curr
    if abs_improv < eps_a:
        return True, f"abs improvement {abs_improv:.2e} < {eps_a}"

    # Condition 3: relative improvement
    rel_improv = abs_improv / (abs(f_prev) + 1e-12)
    if rel_improv < eps_r:
        return True, f"rel improvement {rel_improv:.2e} < {eps_r}"

    # Condition 4: gradient magnitude
    grad_norm = np.linalg.norm(grad_curr)
    if grad_norm < eps_g:
        return True, f"gradient norm {grad_norm:.2e} < {eps_g}"

    return False, "continuing"
\end{lstlisting}

\end{frame}

\section{4.7 Summary \& Exercises}
% ============================================================

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Chapter 4 Summary}

  This chapter introduced the general framework of \emph{local descent}
  and the key design choices involved in making it work well in practice.
  Here is a concise recap of the main ideas.

  \begin{block}{Descent Direction Iteration}
    Every method in this chapter is a special case of the four-step
    template:
    \[
      \bx^{(k+1)} \;=\; \bx^{(k)} + \alpha^{(k)}\bd^{(k)},
    \]
    where $\bd^{(k)}$ is a descent direction (satisfying
    $\grad f(\bx^{(k)})^{\top}\bd^{(k)} < 0$) and $\alpha^{(k)}$
    is a step factor chosen to ensure sufficient decrease.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Step Factors}
    The step factor $\alpha^{(k)}$ (alpha) can be:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Fixed:} constant $\alpha_0$ --- simple but fragile.
      \item \textbf{Exponentially decaying:}
            $\alpha^{(k)} = \gamma^k \alpha^{(0)}$ --- bounded total
            distance; $\gamma$ (gamma) controls speed of decay.
      \item \textbf{Polynomially decaying:}
            $\alpha^{(k)} = \alpha^{(0)}/(1+\beta k)^p$ --- unbounded
            distance when $p \le 1$; needed for stochastic methods.
      \item \textbf{Chosen by line search:} most principled but more costly.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Line Search Methods}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Exact line search:} minimises $f$ along $\bd$
            exactly.  Costly; has a closed form for quadratic $f$.
      \item \textbf{Backtracking (Armijo):} reduces $\alpha$ by factor
            $p$ (p) until the sufficient-decrease condition
            $f(\bx+\alpha\bd) \le f(\bx) + \beta\alpha\,\grad
            f(\bx)^{\top}\bd$ is satisfied.  Cheap; no curvature
            guarantee.
      \item \textbf{Wolfe conditions:} add a curvature condition
            (second Wolfe) to prevent steps from being too small.
            The \emph{strong} Wolfe conditions use an absolute value
            on the curvature check, required by quasi-Newton methods.
      \item \textbf{Strong backtracking:} two-phase algorithm
            (bracket + zoom) that efficiently finds a step satisfying
            the strong Wolfe conditions.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Trust Region Methods}
    Trust region methods simultaneously choose direction and step length
    by solving a quadratic subproblem within a ball of radius $\delta$
    (delta).  The radius is adapted at each iteration via the improvement
    ratio $\eta$ (eta) = actual decrease / predicted decrease.  A high
    $\eta$ means the model is good and $\delta$ can be expanded; a low
    $\eta$ means the model is poor and $\delta$ must shrink.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Termination Conditions}
    Use at least two of the four conditions (max iterations, absolute
    improvement $\epsilon_a$, relative improvement $\epsilon_r$, gradient
    norm $\epsilon_g$) to ensure robust and efficient stopping.  Each
    condition alone has a failure mode; combining them covers all cases.
  \end{block}

  \begin{alertblock}{The Big Picture}
    Local descent is the foundation of virtually all practical
    optimisation algorithms.  The differences between methods --- gradient
    descent, Newton, quasi-Newton, conjugate gradient --- arise entirely
    from different choices of the descent direction $\bd^{(k)}$.  The
    step factor selection strategies (fixed, decaying, line search, trust
    region) apply equally to all of them.
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks]{Key Formulas at a Glance}

  The following table collects every major formula from this chapter
  with its full notation explained.

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Descent Update}
        \[
          \bx^{(k+1)} = \bx^{(k)} + \alpha^{(k)}\bd^{(k)}
        \]
        $\bx^{(k)}$: current point;
        $\alpha^{(k)}$ (alpha): step size;
        $\bd^{(k)}$: descent direction.
      \end{block}

      \begin{block}{Exponential Step Decay}
        \[
          \alpha^{(k)} = \gamma^k\alpha^{(0)},\quad 0<\gamma<1
        \]
        $\gamma$ (gamma): decay rate; $\alpha^{(0)}$: initial step.
      \end{block}

      \begin{block}{First Wolfe (Armijo)}
        \[
          f(\bx+\alpha\bd)\le f(\bx)+\beta\alpha\grad_{\bd}f(\bx)
        \]
        $\beta$ (beta): Armijo parameter, typically $10^{-4}$;
        $\grad_{\bd}f(\bx) = \grad f(\bx)^{\top}\bd < 0$.
      \end{block}

      \begin{block}{Second Wolfe (Curvature)}
        \[
          \grad_{\bd}f(\bx+\alpha\bd)\ge\sigma\,\grad_{\bd}f(\bx)
        \]
        $\sigma$ (sigma): curvature parameter, typically $0.9$.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Trust Region Subproblem}
        \[
          \min_{\|\bx'-\bx\|\le\delta}\hat{f}(\bx')
        \]
        $\delta$ (delta): trust region radius;
        $\hat{f}$: quadratic model of $f$.
      \end{block}

      \begin{block}{Improvement Ratio}
        \[
          \eta = \frac{f(\bx)-f(\bx')}{f(\bx)-\hat{f}(\bx')}
        \]
        $\eta$ (eta): improvement ratio;
        numerator: actual decrease; denominator: predicted decrease.
      \end{block}

      \begin{block}{Termination: Gradient}
        \[
          \|\grad f(\bx^{(k+1)})\|<\epsilon_g
        \]
        $\epsilon_g$ (epsilon sub g): gradient tolerance.
      \end{block}

      \begin{block}{Termination: Abs.\ Improvement}
        \[
          f(\bx^{(k)})-f(\bx^{(k+1)})<\epsilon_a
        \]
        $\epsilon_a$ (epsilon sub a): absolute tolerance.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\end{frame}

    Explain in detail why relying on the gradient magnitude condition
    $\|\grad f\| < \epsilon_g$ alone can be insufficient.  Give two

    algorithm has not found a good solution, and (b) a case where the

  \begin{block}{Exercise 4.2: First Wolfe Condition on a Sinusoid}
    Consider gradient descent on $f(x) = \sin(x)$ starting at
    $x^{(1)} = -2$.  The gradient is $f'(x) = \cos(x)$.
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Compute the steepest descent direction at $x^{(1)} = -2$.
      \item Using the first Wolfe condition with $\beta = 0.1$, determine
            whether $\alpha = 0.5$ is an acceptable step.
      \item What is the approximate maximum $\alpha$ that satisfies the
            Armijo condition?
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \framebreak

  \begin{block}{Exercise 4.3: Exact Line Search for a Quadratic}
    For $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + 2x_2^2$, starting at
    $\bx = [1, 1]^{\top}$:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Compute the gradient $\grad f(\bx)$ and verify that the
            steepest descent direction is $\bd = [-2, -4]^{\top}$.
      \item Write down the Hessian matrix $\bH$ of $f$.
      \item Apply the quadratic line search formula
            $\alpha^* = \bd^{\top}\bd / (\bd^{\top}\bH\bd)$
            to find the exact step size.
      \item Compute the new point $\bx' = \bx + \alpha^*\bd$ and verify
            that $f(\bx') < f(\bx)$.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Exercise 4.4: Trust Region Radius Adaptation}

    one iteration:
    \[
      f(\bx^{(k)}) = 10.0,\quad
      f(\bx') = 9.2,\quad
      \hat{f}(\bx') = 8.5.
    \]
    Compute the improvement ratio $\eta$.  With parameters $\eta_1 =
    0.25$, $\eta_2 = 0.75$, $\gamma_1 = 0.5$, $\gamma_2 = 2.0$, and
    current radius $\delta = 1.0$:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Should the step be accepted or rejected?
      \item What should the new trust region radius $\delta'$ be?
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}
